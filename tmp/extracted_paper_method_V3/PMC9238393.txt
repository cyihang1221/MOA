</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>ABSTRACT</h2>
<p><em>Metarhizium</em> is a genus of endophytic, insect-pathogenic fungi that is used as a biological control agent. The dual lifestyles of these fungi combine the parasitism of insect pests with the symbiotic association with plant roots. A major class of secreted metabolites by <em>Metarhizium</em> are cyclic depsipeptides called destruxins (DTXs). As prominent insecticidal compounds, their role during plant interactions is still largely unknown. Here, we examined the metabolomic profile of <em>Metarhizium</em>, with special emphasis on DTX production, using untargeted, liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Four <em>Metarhizium</em> species, two insect generalists (<em>M. robertsii</em> and <em>M. brunneum</em>), and two insect specialists (<em>M. flavoviride</em> and <em>M. acridum</em>) were inoculated onto agar plate cultures containing either bean (<em>Phaseolus vulgaris</em>) or corn (<em>Zea mays</em>) and grown for four and seven days. After methanol extraction, feature-based molecular networking (FBMN) was used to obtain DTX identification as defined by the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS). A total of 25 DTX analogs were identified, with several DTX-like compounds in coculture that could not be identified. <em>Metarhizium</em> species differed in the amount and type of DTXs they produced, with the insect specialists producing far fewer amounts and types of DTXs than the insect generalists. The production of these metabolites varied between cultures of different ages and plant hosts. Conditions that influence the production of DTXs are discussed. As the genetic arsenal of natural products relates to the lifestyle of the organism, uncovering conditions with an ecological context may reveal strategies for producing novel compounds or precursors suitable for synthetic biology.</p>
<p><strong>IMPORTANCE</strong> The development of an intimate and beneficial association between fungi and plants requires an exchange of a complex mixture of chemical cues. These compounds are a means of communication, promoting or limiting the interaction, but can have numerous other biological and ecological functions. Determining how the metabolome, or a subset thereof, is linked to plant host preference and colonization has implications for future functional studies and may uncover novel therapeutic compounds whose production is elicited only under cocultivation. In this study, we performed an untargeted metabolomic analysis of plate cocultures with individual plant-fungal pairs. The identification of a major group of fungal metabolites, the destruxins, was examined for their role in plant specificity. The diversity of these metabolites and the production of numerous unidentified, structural analogs are evidence of the sensitivity of the methodology and the potential for future mining of this living data set.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>KEYWORDS:</strong> metabolomics, feature-based molecular networking, MS-DIAL, GNPS, MetaboAnalyst, fungal metabolites, endophyte</p></section></section><section id="s1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p><em>Metarhizium</em> is an ascomycetous fungus that has a dual lifestyle. It is an insect pathogen as well as an endophyte. One of the metabolites that this fungus secretes is a group of cyclic depsipeptides, called destruxins (DTXs), which are composed of an alpha-hydroxy acid and five amino acids residues (<a href="#B1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1">1</a>). The type of hydroxy acid and the variation in the five amino acid residues defines the main DTX groups (i.e., DTX A through F) (<a href="#B1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1">1</a>). DTXs have a range of biological activity which is attributed structurally to the ester bond (<a href="#B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2">2</a>). In insects, DTXs act by suppressing both cellular and humoral immune responses and induce flaccid paralysis and visceral muscle contraction (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>). However, the versatile biological activities of DTXs have been used for suppressing the proliferation of hepatitis B virus (<a href="#B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4">4</a>), deterring insect herbivory (<a href="#B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5">5</a>), suppressing proliferation of leukemic cells (<a href="#B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6">6</a>), and phytotoxicity (<a href="#B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7">7</a>). DTXs have been isolated from other fungal species, including the selective plant pathogen, <em>Alternaria brassicae</em> (<a href="#B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7">7</a>), the insect pathogens, <em>Aschersonia aleyrodis</em> (<a href="#B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8">8</a>) and <em>Lecanicillium longisporum</em> (<a href="#B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9">9</a>), and the marine-derived fungus, <em>Pseudoceratina purpurea</em> (<a href="#B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10">10</a>).</p>
<p>The metabolomic profile of <em>Metarhizium</em> species is linked to insect host specificity (<a href="#B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11">11</a>). Species of <em>Metarhizium</em> with a broad insect-host range (“generalists”; <em>M. robertsii</em>, <em>M. anisopliae</em>, and <em>M. brunneum</em>) clustered separately from those species with a narrow insect-host range (“specialists”; <em>M. acridum</em> and <em>M. album</em>) in a multivariate analysis of the metabolome, which was supported by a genomic analysis that showed the loss of certain biosynthetic gene clusters in specialist species (<a href="#B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11">11</a>). Wang et al. (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>) and Donzelli et al. (<a href="#B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12">12</a>) both identified the nonribosomal peptide synthetase, <em>dtxS1</em>, gene cluster responsible for DTX production in <em>M. robertsii</em> (ARSEF 23 and 2575, respectively). The acridid specialist, <em>M. acridum</em> (CQMa 102), lacks <em>dtxS1</em> and thus is incapable of producing DTXs and has a narrow insect host range (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>). However, DTX production can differ between strains of the same species, for example, <em>M. acridum</em> ARSEF 324 has been reported to produce low levels of DTXs <em>in vitro</em> (<a href="#B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13">13</a>) while another study showed that ARSEF 324 did not produce DTXs (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>).</p>
<p>The dual lifestyle of <em>Metarhizium</em> species involves endophytic colonization of plants but the role of DTXs during these interactions is not well understood. A few studies have screened <em>Metarhizium</em><em>-</em>colonized plant tissue for detectable DTXs (<a href="#B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14">14</a><a href="#B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15">–</a><a href="#B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16">16</a>) because this has implications for food safety for both humans and animals. However, the role of DTXs during these plant interactions is still largely unknown. The ability to colonize plant roots has been retained by both insect generalists and specialist <em>Metarhizium</em> spp., however, <em>Metarhizium</em> appears to colonize monocots better than dicots (<a href="#B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17">17</a>). Determining how the metabolome, or a subset thereof (i.e., DTXs), is linked to plant host preference and colonization has implications in future functional studies and may uncover novel therapeutic compounds whose production is elicited only under cocultivation of the fungus with the plant host.</p>
<p>Here, we examined the metabolomic profile, with special emphasis on DTX production, using an untargeted metabolomic approach of four <em>Metarhizium</em> species, two of which are insect generalists (<em>M. robertsii</em> ARSEF 2575 and <em>M. brunneum</em> ARSEF 2974) and two of which are insect specialists (<em>M. flavoviride</em> ARSEF 2133 and <em>M. acridum</em> ARSEF 7486), during plate culture cocultivation with the dicot plant, haricot bean (<em>Phaseolus vulgaris</em>) and the monocot plant, corn (<em>Zea mays</em>), after four and seven days. Feature-based molecular networking (FBMN) was used to identify DTXs to a confidence level as defined by Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) (i.e., gold, silver, or bronze). As the genetic arsenal of natural products relates to the lifestyle of the organism, uncovering conditions with an ecological context may reveal strategies for producing novel compounds or precursors suitable for synthetic biology.</p></section><section id="s2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS</h2>
<section id="s2-1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Destruxin identification.</h3>
<p>The raw metabolomic data were processed using data-independent acquisition (DIA) in liquid chromatography (LC) coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) spectra that were matched to database entries within the open-source program, MS-DIAL (<a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">18</a>), and were manually verified using the methods of Arroyo-Manzanares et al. (<a href="#B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19">19</a>). In addition, the distribution of DTXs among the samples was checked to ensure no errors occurred due to gap-filling performed by MS-DIAL (i.e., noise identified as a peak of interest). DTXs were not detected in any of the uninoculated plant or agar control samples (unpublished data).</p>
<p>The FBMN that was created from the processed data showed a cluster of features that surrounded matched DTX compounds (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>). The precursor mass and an estimated molecular formula of these unknown features were compared to a list of DTXs, and putative matches were verified using the characteristic fragment ions of the MS/MS (<a href="#B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19">19</a>). The remaining spectra that could not be identified were renamed as “destruxin-like compound” (DLC), with the accompanying mass-to-charge ratio (DLC_<em>m/z</em>), due to their clustering with known DTXs.</p>

<p>The DTX-containing cluster of the FBMNs is shown in <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>. The DTX cluster consisted of 28 GNPS-identified DTXs with the remaining 48 nodes classified as DLCs in the haricot bean associated samples. In the corn-associated network, there was a total of 96 nodes, with 24 being identified as DTXs. The nodes were created as pie charts to visualize the contribution of each sample type to each feature (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>). From the FBMN, potential isomers of DTX analogs were apparent (e.g., DTX B2.2).</p>
<p>In a comparison among bean samples (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1A</a>), most features had an abundance originating from <em>M. robertsii</em> or <em>M. robertsii</em><em>-</em>bean coculture. <em>M. flavoviride</em> noticeably contributed to 14 features, with a predominance in DLC_722.2604. <em>M. brunneum</em> visibly contributed to six features, the largest being DLC_552.3366. <em>M. acridum</em> dominated only a single feature in the DTX-containing cluster, DLC_596.3407.</p>
<p>Similar to the DTX-containing cluster of haricot bean associated samples, the features of the corn samples were predominantly obtained from <em>M. robertsii</em> or <em>M. robertsii</em> coculture with corn (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1B</a>). In comparison, one-third of the features came from <em>M. brunneum</em> coculture with corn. The majority were unidentified DLCs. <em>M. flavoviride</em> metabolites were readily observed in 20 features of the FBMN with the single predominant DLC_722.2604 being almost exclusive to this species. The contribution of <em>M. acridum</em> to the network was observed in four nodes, all unidentified DLCs, and of which the same DLC_596.3407 was predominantly obtained from <em>M. acridum</em>. The differences observed in the raw intensity values displayed in the FBMNs indicated that there may be significant differences between DTX production of <em>Metarhizium</em> species when grown with plants. In addition, the DTX profile of a single species or strain of <em>Metarhizium</em> may differ depending on the plant host.</p></section><section id="s2-3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Destruxins detected in plant cocultures.</h3>

<p>Similar to 4-day bean cocultures, 7-day coculture with <em>M. robertsii</em> was significantly different in the production of all DTXs and was the only strain from which destruxin G was detected in bean coculture (Table S8). There were no significant differences between 7-day bean cocultures of <em>M. acridum</em>, <em>M. brunneum</em>, and <em>M. flavoviride</em>.</p></section><section id="s2-5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolite profiles of different <em>Metarhizium</em> spp. in corn coculture.</h3>
<p>Principal-component analysis (PCA) of the destruxin and total metabolomic profiles for corn cocultures with the four <em>Metarhizium</em> strains is shown in <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>. Unlike the DTX profiles from bean cocultures, <em>M. robertsii</em> and <em>M. brunneum</em> both clustered separately from those of <em>M. acridum</em> and <em>M. flavoviride</em> (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>). In comparison to the clustering of 4 and 7-day destruxin profiles of <em>M. robertsii</em> cocultures, <em>M. brunneum</em> cocultures were well separated by the duration of incubation. The PCA of the total metabolomic profile of corn cocultures was similar to the DTX PCA in that <em>M. robertsii</em> again was well separated from the other strains (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>). <em>M. brunneum</em> was similarly separated however, the replicates were not as tightly clustered with regard to the total metabolomic profile. Cocultures of <em>M. acridum</em> and <em>M. flavoviride</em> (except 7-day <em>M. flavoviride</em>), clustered with uninoculated corn. This may be a result of low levels of fungal metabolite production due to the relatively lower level of growth as seen in the culture plates (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>).</p>
<p>Statistical analysis of the DTX profiles revealed that similar to bean coculture, <em>M. robertsii</em> 4-day corn coculture differed significantly from all other cultures (Table S9). As was seen in the 4-day bean coculture, <em>M. acridum</em> and <em>M. flavoviride</em> corn cocultures were not significantly different for any DTXs. Among all the strains, DTXs CHL, CHL-E2, and G were not significantly different. <em>M. brunneum</em> and <em>M. robertsii</em> differed in 60% of DTXs (Table S9), with <em>M. robertsii</em> having higher levels for all DTXs detected. The DTXs that differed between all species (except between <em>M. acridum</em> and <em>M. flavoviride</em>) were DesmA, DesmC, A, A1, A2, A4, B1, B2.2, B4, C, C1, C2/F, and Ed1 (Table S9).</p>
<p>After 7 days in coculture with corn, both <em>M. robertsii</em> and <em>M. brunneum</em> were significantly different for nearly all DTXs in comparison to <em>M. acridum</em> and <em>M. flavoviride</em> (Table S10). Even though there were 17 DTXs detected from <em>M. flavoviride</em> 7-day corn cultures, the DTX profile was not significantly different from that of <em>M. acridum</em> (Table S10). To resolve this discrepancy the sample chromatograms were investigated, and it was noted that one replicate had much lower intensity throughout the run (unpublished data). The difference between cultures of <em>M. robertsii</em> and <em>M. brunneum</em> was significant for 56% (14/25) of DTXs (Table S10). All were detected in higher abundance in <em>M. robertsii</em> cultures, except for CHL-E2, Ed, Ed1, and G which were in greater abundance in <em>M. brunneum</em> cultures.</p></section><section id="s2-6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Presence/absence of a <em>dtxS1</em> gene in different <em>Metarhizium</em> species and strains.</h3>
<p>To support the mass spectra results, the presence of the <em>dtxS1</em> gene was confirmed by PCR in all fungal strains used in this study (Fig. S1). We also included <em>M. acridum</em> ARSEF 324 because it was previously shown not to have the <em>dtxS1</em> gene (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>) and we confirmed that a PCR product was not amplified.</p></section></section><section id="s3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">DISCUSSION</h2>
<p>Here, we examined the production of DTXs from four species of <em>Metarhizium</em> in plate coculture with beans and corn. There are currently over 40 known DTXs and of those, a total of 25 were detected in this study (including a potential analog, B2.2, a putative isomer of DTX B2). <em>M. robertsii</em> 2575 was the most prominent producer of DTXs under all conditions. Previous reports on DTX production by this strain demonstrated that it produces relatively high levels of DTXs compared to other <em>Metarhizium</em> spp. (<a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">20</a>). While all 25 DTXs were detected in <em>M. robertsii</em> cultures with all host plants, the remaining three <em>Metarhizium</em> spp. produced various combinations of DTXs. Although <em>M. brunneum</em> ARSEF 2974 produced all 25 DTXs, the majority occurred during coculture with corn. Few DTXs were detected from <em>M. brunneum</em> during growth in coculture with bean or in isolation on M100 agar; similar results were obtained for <em>M. flavoviride</em> and <em>M. acridum</em>. In some cases, particularly for <em>M. flavoviride</em> cultures, there was a substantial difference in the amount of DTX detected between biological replicates. Such variation has previously been observed, for example, in a study done by Carpio et al. (<a href="#B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21">21</a>), only 25% of samples from each of the four types of potato tissue tested (leaf, stem, tuber, root) yielded detectable levels of DTX A and B. The extraction of the DTXs was validated before the analysis and the recovery of injected standards was between 91.6 and 99.3% (<a href="#B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21">21</a>). Our study did not validate the extraction procedure, but the variation in isolation of DTXs from other studies lends support to this being due to biological sample variation, particularly with those DTXs that were produced in smaller quantities.</p>
<p>Except for the 7-day cultures of <em>M. flavoviride</em> and <em>M. robertsii</em>, DTX production was reduced on M100 agar without a plant. In addition to differences among species and strains, the type of growth medium has been shown to influence DTX production (<a href="#B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16">16</a>, <a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>). The length of time to produce DTXs varies by species and medium type (<a href="#B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23">23</a>). For example, growth in Czapek’s Dox medium + 0.5% peptone for 10 days was optimal for DTX production by <em>M. anisopliae</em> strain MaQ10 (<a href="#B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23">23</a>), while 20 days was optimal for <em>M. anisopliae</em> strain V245 (<a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">20</a>). In the current study, there were clear differences in detectable DTX production between two time points with greater amounts and number of DTX observed at 7 days compared to 4 days.</p>
<p>Caution must be used when making comparisons of DTX production between <em>Metarhizium</em> spp. as there exists interspecific and intraspecific variation (<a href="#B24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B24">24</a>). Studies comparing DTX production between <em>Metarhizium</em> spp. have found that <em>M. acridum</em> does not produce detectable levels of DTXs or is a relatively low producer (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>, <a href="#B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13">13</a>). Our results agree with this observation because <em>M. acridum</em> ARSEF 7486 produced the fewest DTXs (16/25) of the four species studied. DTXs were reportedly not detected from Czapek Dox broth cultures of <em>M. acridum</em> CQMa 102 (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>). This generally agrees with the literature on this species, in which <em>M. acridum</em> frequently lacks the gene, <em>dtxS1</em>, encoding the nonribosomal peptide synthetase responsible for the biosynthesis of DTX B (and subsequently DTX C, D, A, E) (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>). However, other strains of <em>M. acridum</em> may be DTX producers. Kershaw et al. (<a href="#B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13">13</a>) detected low levels of DTX A and E in Czapek Dox broth cultures from <em>M. acridum</em> ARSEF 324 after 9 days of growth. In this study, <em>M. acridum</em> ARSEF 7486 produced DTXs A, B, B2, B2.2, B4, D, and DesmC on M100 grown for 4 or 7 days.</p>
<p>DTX production by generalist <em>Metarhizium</em> species is highly variable. Golo et al. (<a href="#B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14">14</a>) and Kershaw et al. (<a href="#B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13">13</a>) showed that strains within the same species may or may not produce DTXs. Furthermore, it is not clear whether DTX production is ancestral or not. Wang et al. (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>) suggested that “DTX producing <em>Metarhizium</em> species acquired the toxin gene cluster by an as yet unknown mechanism” and Xu et al. (<a href="#B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11">11</a>) argued that “it cannot be ruled out that the ancestral <em>Metarhizium</em> contained the gene cluster but the specialist species lost it.” Evolution acts at a local level and not all strains within a species of <em>Metarhizium</em> are subjected to the same evolutionary pressures. Therefore, different strains of what is defined as <em>M. acridum</em> found in different environments worldwide may be under different evolutionary pressures. This is also true of strains of generalist <em>Metarhizium</em> species; some produce DTXs while others do not. Nielsen et al. (<a href="#B25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B25">25</a>) showed that <em>M. acridum</em> has “infrequent sexual cycles” and could have various ploidy levels and we speculate that this could contribute to differences in DTX production. Different <em>M. acridum</em> strains may have different adaptive landscapes that could contribute to reversion to a haploid state, which could contribute to gene loss and variability. However, it was apparent that the specialist strains either lost the ability to produce DTX or, as we showed, have a greatly reduced ability to produce DTX. It is unclear what evolutionary pressures contribute to differences in DTX production in <em>Metarhizium</em> species.</p>
<p>Xu et al. (<a href="#B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11">11</a>) showed that, when <em>Metarhizium</em> species were grown in a nutrient-rich broth, PCA plotting of the metabolite profiles resulted in the separation of insect specialists and insect generalists. In our study, PCA plots similarly revealed that the specialists <em>M. acridum</em> and <em>M. flavoviride</em> were typically well separated from the generalist species regarding DTX profiles and production. However, there was no clear separation of <em>M. acridum</em> from <em>M. flavoviride</em> even when different principal components were selected (unpublished data). In contrast to the trend observed by Xu et al. (<a href="#B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11">11</a>), we observed that <em>M. robertsii</em> and <em>M. brunneum</em> were well separated after 7 days of growth in plant coculture. Generalist and specialist spp. are capable of colonizing the roots of numerous plant hosts (<a href="#B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17">17</a>), with no significant differences in the colonization of the rhizosphere or root (rhizoplane and endosphere) of bean or corn, between species of <em>Metarhizium</em> (i.e., ARSEF 7486, 2575, 2133 are of relevance to this study). The one exception was that <em>M. acridum</em> ARSEF 7486 showed decreased rhizosphere persistence and rhizoplane colonization of beans, compared to <em>M. robertsii</em> ARSEF 2575. The reduced competency of <em>M. acridum</em> has been observed previously (<a href="#B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B26">26</a>). All species colonized both haricot bean and corn but were found to have greater levels of colonization on corn, including endophytic colonization which was not observed in beans (<a href="#B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17">17</a>). The questions that arise are whether the production of DTXs influences the capability of a particular fungus to colonize a plant host or is DTX production dependent on the colonization ability in a host plant.</p>
<p>DTXs have been identified in the tissues of potato (<a href="#B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16">16</a>, <a href="#B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21">21</a>), tomato (<a href="#B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27">27</a>), melon (<a href="#B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28">28</a>), cowpea, and cucumber (<a href="#B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14">14</a>) during colonization by <em>Metarhizium</em>. <em>M. robertsii</em> 2575 was able to endophytically colonize both cowpea and cucumber. However, DTXs A, B, and E were detectable only in plant tissue from cowpea (<a href="#B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14">14</a>). <em>M. acridum</em> ARSEF 324, which did not produce detectable DTXs in broth culture or <em>in planta</em>, was also found to endophytically colonize both cowpea and cucumber (<a href="#B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14">14</a>). Although we did not evaluate endophytic colonization of bean or corn in this study, previous reports of colonization of these plants by the strains used (<a href="#B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17">17</a>) taken together with the variability in DTX production observed here, supports the idea that DTX production is not related to the colonization capability of <em>Metarhizium</em> spp. This begs the question of why DTXs are produced by <em>Metarhizium</em> spp. during interactions with host plants, as their synthesis is energetically costly. DTXs may serve an as-yet-unknown ecological function during plant colonization or may be an evolutionary relic from a phytopathogenic lineage.</p>
<p>The plant pathogen <em>Alternaria brassicae</em> produces DTX B during colonization and elicits “blackspot” infection in leaves of <em>Brassica</em> species (<a href="#B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29">29</a>). However, the phytotoxicity of DTX B appears to be host-selective with resistant strains performing detoxification of DTX B through hydroxylation and glycosylation (<a href="#B30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30">30</a>). Despite the production of DTX B, <em>Metarhizium</em> has not been reported to cause disease symptoms in plants (<a href="#B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31">31</a>). Studies utilizing aerial sprays, one method of deployment for commercial formulations of <em>Metarhizium</em>, did not indicate any chlorosis of leaves or symptomology similar to that of <em>A. brassicae</em> (<a href="#B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31">31</a>, <a href="#B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32">32</a>). This study screened for DTX production among <em>Metarhizium</em> species during growth with different plants but did not evaluate if catabolism of DTXs, including DTX B detoxification, occurred.</p>
<p>Host plant species also influenced the observed DTXs profiles. Plant roots exude a plethora of compounds, including primary and secondary metabolites, root border cells, and mucilage (<a href="#B33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B33">33</a>). Most primary metabolites are amino acids, organic acids, and carbohydrates but the volume and composition of root exudates differ by species, cultivar, age, and environmental factors (<a href="#B33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B33">33</a>). Given that the growth medium highly influences the DTXs that are produced, the effect of the plant species may similarly reflect the nutritional components of the root exudates and their suitability for the associated microorganisms. For example, DTX production was shown to be highly influenced by nitrogen availability in <em>M. anisopliae</em> var. <em>anisopliae</em> Bipesco 5 (<a href="#B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34">34</a>). DTXs A, B, and E production, measured from shaken flask cultures and normalized to fungal biomass, was significantly reduced during growth on a medium with reduced amounts of nitrate, in contrast to increased production with glucose exhaustion (<a href="#B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34">34</a>). The dynamics of nutrient utilization and stress experienced by <em>Metarhizium</em> spp. during the plant, colonization may be reflected in the DTXs produced.</p>
<p>DTXs have exhibited various documented bioactivities, including antiviral activity toward hepatitis B virus (<a href="#B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4">4</a>), antiproliferative effects on leukemic cells (<a href="#B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6">6</a>), phytotoxicity (<a href="#B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7">7</a>), and suppression of immune responses (<a href="#B35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B35">35</a>) and toxicity in insects (reviewed in reference (<a href="#B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31">31</a>)). Treatment of Chinese cabbage leaves with DTX A, B, or E resulted in toxicity when ingested but also revealed significant antifeedant behavior by <em>Plutella xylostella</em> and <em>Phaedon cochleariae</em> (<a href="#B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5">5</a>). The effect was dose-dependent and the DTXs differed in the strength of the response, with DTX B having the lowest antifeedant index. This suggests that the synthesis of secondary metabolites (e.g., DTXs) during plant colonization may confer protection from insect herbivory. Maize plants endophytically colonized by the entomopathogenic fungus, <em>Beauveria bassiana</em>, had reduced damage from tunneling larvae, <em>Ostrina nubilalis</em> (<a href="#B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B36">36</a>), and reduced consumption by the defoliator, <em>Rachiplusia nu</em> (<a href="#B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B37">37</a>). <em>B. bassiana</em> can naturally grow systemically within plant hosts whereas <em>Metarhizium</em> spp. are typically restricted to root tissues (<a href="#B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38">38</a>). However, artificial inoculations with <em>Metarhizium</em> spp. (e.g., seed treatment and aerial spray) has resulted in the incidence of phyllosphere colonization (<a href="#B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B39">39</a><a href="#B40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B40">–</a><a href="#B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B41">41</a>). This has important implications for strain selection for use in food production. Thorough evaluation of the level of DTXs within tissues of a particular cultivar, when treated with a particular fungal strain, is prudent as the results are highly variable. Golo et al. (<a href="#B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14">14</a>) also suggest that continued research would allow the selection of non-DTX producing strains or cultivars that possess the ability to detoxify DTXs for use in this industry.</p>
<p>Secondary metabolites produced by plant colonizing fungi play a key role in communication with the host, as modulators of the immune system, or as a means by which to confer antifeedant protection (<a href="#B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42">42</a>). DTXs are but one, albeit well-known, major class of bioactive metabolites produced by numerous <em>Metarhizium</em> species. Apart from their importance in insecticidal and phytotoxic activity, they have valuable pharmaceutical applications. As there is an energetic cost associated with their production, the plethora of DTXs detected in culture with plants suggests that there may be a yet-to-be-defined function for these metabolites during plant colonization. Furthermore, there were clear differences in the production of DTXs by <em>Metarhizium</em> when grown with different plant species. The understanding of the plant symbiotic nature of insect pathogens, such as <em>Metarhizium</em>, is still in its infancy. Decoding the chemical ecology of prominent secondary metabolites, such as DTXs, and how they influence plant association will lend itself to increasing the efficacy and safety in the usage of these biocontrol agents in the field.</p></section><section id="s4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">MATERIALS AND METHODS</h2>
<section id="s4-1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Fungal and plant materials.</h3>
<p>The fungal species used in this study were: <em>Metarhizium acridum</em> (ARSEF 7486 and ARSEF 324), <em>M. brunneum</em> (ARSEF 2974), <em>M. flavoviride</em> (ARSEF 2133), and <em>M. robertsii</em> (ARSEF 2575). All cultures were maintained on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Bioshop, Canada). Plant seeds of corn (<em>Zea mays</em> “Sunnyvee”) and haricot bean (<em>Phaseolus vulgaris</em> “Soldier”) were purchased from OSC Seeds (ON, Canada). Before use, all plant seeds were surface sterilized by submersion in 2% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min and rinsed with sterile, distilled water (3×), followed by submersion in 15% hydrogen peroxide for 20 min. Seeds were rinsed and stored at 4°C overnight for stratification. Seeds were germinated on water agar plates incubated at 25°C for 5 to 7 days. A subsample of the seeds was checked for sterility by incubating on PDA at 25°C, and the final water rinse (100 μL) was inoculated onto PDA and incubated at 25°C.</p></section><section id="s4-2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample preparation.</h3>
<p>Germinated seedlings of corn and bean were placed in pairs onto 20 mL M-100 agar plates. The radicle of the seedling was dipped into sterile 0.01% Triton-X before placement for better dispersion of the conidial suspension. Eight 10 μL droplets of a 1.5 × 10<sup>6</sup> conidia/mL suspension were dispersed over the radicles and the lower portion of the plate where the roots would develop. M-100 plates inoculated with an equivalent amount of conidial suspension or seedlings treated only with Triton-X were controls. Samples were run in duplicate, and plates were sealed with parafilm and incubated at 25°C in a 16/8-h light/dark cycle for 4 and 7 days.</p></section><section id="s4-3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolite extraction.</h3>
<p>For coculture experiments and uninoculated plant controls, the plant material was cut 1 cm above the hypocotyl and discarded. The remaining contents of the agar plate (including agar with embedded plant roots and fungal growth) were transferred to a mortar and ground under liquid nitrogen. The entire contents of the fungal pure culture plates and the uninoculated agar plate controls were ground under liquid nitrogen. The contents were transferred to a 150 mL flask to which 50 mL of high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-grade methanol was added, and the flask was placed into a sonicating water bath set to 40 kHz for 15 min. The solution was then filtered twice (Whatman filter followed by a 0.2 μm filter) and reduced to a volume of 5 mL by rotary evaporation. A 1 mL aliquot was transferred to a 2 mL LC-MS/MS vial (Thermo Scientific) for analysis.</p></section><section id="s4-4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LC-MS/MS analysis.</h3>
<p>High-performance liquid chromatography (LC) coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) was performed on the Q Exactive Quadrupole-Orbitrap (Thermo Fisher). The ionization source was electrospray (ESI) and the samples were analyzed in positive ion mode. The LC column was SB-C18 (2.1 × 50 mm × 1.8 μm, Thermo Fisher) at a column temperature of 35°C. The mobile phase consisted of phases A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The gradient program was as follows: 0 to 2 min, 10% B; 2 to 17 min, 10 to 100% B; 17 to 18 min, 100 to 10% B. The flow rate was set at 0.4 mL/min. A 10 μL aliquot of the supernatant was injected for each analysis. The MS analysis conditions were as follows: mass range: <em>m/z</em> 150 to 2000; ion spray voltage: 4500 V; electrospray ionization probe temperature: 425°C; heated capillary temperature: 300°C; sheath gas: 50 arb (arbitrary units); auxiliary gas: 15 arb; collision energy: 30 V; resolution: 70,000; MS/MS resolution: 17,500; isolation window: 4 <em>m/z</em>; and (S)-lens RF level: 55. To control for potential cross-contamination from the LC column, LC-MS/MS samples were randomized before analysis, and arranged as follows: 3 MeOH &gt; 7 samples &gt; MeOH control &gt; 7 samples &gt; MeOH &gt; 7 samples &gt; 3 MeOH.</p></section><section id="s4-5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Processing of LC/MS data and statistical analysis.</h3>
<p>The raw data files were preprocessed using the program MS-DIAL (<a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">18</a>) for baseline correction, peak picking, alignment, and adduct identification. Agar plate extractions and methanol controls were used as the analysis baseline to further control for the metabolite background. The publicly available spectral file (29,269 spectra, as of June 2019; <a href="http://prime.psc.riken.jp/Metabolomics_Software/MS-DIAL/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://prime.psc.riken.jp/Metabolomics_Software/MS-DIAL/</a>) was combined with the in-house database curations of known DTX analogs. A table with peak height information for each sample was normalized using the mTIC method within MS-DIAL and then exported for transformation, data scaling and analysis using the web-based server, MetaboAnalyst 4.0 (<a href="#B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B43">43</a>). MetaboAnalyst 4.0 was used to generate the principal-component analyses (PCAs), and heatmaps and to determine significant differences in DTX production between groups using an analysis of variance (ANOVA; Tukey honestly significantly different [HSD] <em>post hoc</em>). Feature-based molecular networking (FBMN) was performed using the Global Natural Product Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) site. The data were deposited to the Mass Spectrometry Interactive Virtual Environment (<a href="http://massive.ucsd.edu" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://massive.ucsd.edu</a>) with MassIVE accession numbers: <a href="https://massive.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/dataset.jsp?task=cfadc114ef01426dbd66ff6b0cac0ecb" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">MSV000085394</a> (bean cocultures) and <a href="https://massive.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/dataset.jsp?task=7d920245591745618d1cf01174499559" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">MSV000085395</a> (corn cocultures).</p></section><section id="s4-6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Confirming the presence of the destruxin synthetase (<em>dtxS1</em>) gene.</h3>
<p>Genomic DNA of the fungi <em>Metarhizium</em>
<em>robertsii</em> (ARSEF 2575), <em>M. acridum</em> (ARSEF 7486), <em>M. acridum</em> (ARSEF 324), <em>M. brunneum</em> (ARSEF 2974), and <em>M. flavoviride</em> (ARSEF 2133), was extracted (DNeasy Plant Minikit, Qiagen) and amplified using the previously described primer pair, dtxF (<em>AGATTTGCCGCAGCTACCTA</em>) and dtxR (<em>CACCAGATGCGAGTTCTCAA</em>) for the <em>dtxS1</em> gene (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>). <em>M. acridum</em> ARSEF 324 was included as a negative-control because, in a previous study, the <em>dtxS1</em> gene could not be amplified (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>). The primers, itsF (<em>TTACGTCCCTGCCCTTTGTA</em>) and itsR (<em>ACCTCACCAAAAGCATCCTC</em>) were used to amplify the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions to confirm successful DNA extraction (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>). The 25 μL reactions consisted of 2.5 μL 10× ThermoPol Buffer (New England Biolabs), 1 μL DNA, 0.5 μL 10 mM dNTPs, and 0.2 μM each primer pair (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>). The PCR protocol was: 30 s initial denaturation at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 20 s at 95°C, 1 min at 65°C, and 45 s at 68°C for extension, then held at 68°C for 5 min. Gel electrophoresis was performed with 5 μL of each PCR product at 90 V for 40 min on a 1.2% agarose gel. The resulting band(s) for <em>dtxS1</em> were cut from the gel and purified by MinElute Gel Extraction kit (Qiagen) and sent for Sanger sequencing (The Centre for Applied Genomics, Toronto). Once the presence of the <em>dtxS1</em> gene in all fungal strains used in this study was confirmed (Fig. S1), the assembled sequences were submitted to GenBank (Accession numbers: <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/ON359876" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ON359876</a> to <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/ON359881" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ON359881</a>).</p></section><section id="s4-7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data availability.</h3>
