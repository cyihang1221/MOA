>METHODS   Animal Husbandry   Adult (110 dpf) zebrafish ( D. rerio)  of the AB wild-type strain obtained from a single clutch were maintained at 28.5°C on a 14:10-h light-dark cycle at a density of no more than five fish/liter on a water circulation system (Techniplast). All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the University of North Carolina Chapel Hill Animal Care and Use Committee. Animals were housed in an American Association for the Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC)-accredited facility in compliance with the  Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals  as detailed in protocols ( dx.doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.bg3jjykn ). Feeding Regimens   Zebrafish were randomized to one of the two diet groups for 8 wk ( n  = 40 fish/group) in a 1:1 ratio of male:female fish in each group. The normal calorie diet (ND) consisted of a single feeding of Gemma Micro 300 (GM300, Skretting) daily, providing 59% protein and 14% fat by weight, at the manufacturer’s recommended amount (3% of the total fish weight per tank). The overfed diet (OF) was the same as normal diet but daily feeding amounts were 10 times the amount by weight ( Table 1 ). Fish were weighed every 4 wk and feeding was adjusted to maintain caloric intake with respect to fish weight. Before euthanasia, fish were weighed by addition to a known quantity of aquaculture system water on a scale and weights obtained without anesthesia to minimize anesthetic effects on the kidney metabolome. Untargeted Metabolomics   The experimental schema is summarized in  Fig. 1 A . At 8 wk, zebrafish were fasted overnight (24 h from last meal) and euthanized in ice-cold system water. Kidneys were removed immediately after euthanasia by manual dissection and 5–8 kidneys from each of the groups were pooled as one biological replicate, weighed, transferred to empty 2-mL MagNA Lyser Green Beads tubes (Roche), and snap frozen at −80°C until analysis. Each of the dietary groups included five biological replicate samples which were prepared following published procedures with minor modifications ( 9 ). Study samples were randomized before sample preparation and data acquisition. Briefly, the frozen kidney samples were mixed with clean beads (∼0.2 g) and ice-cold methanol-water (80:20) with a ratio of 200 µL per 5 mg tissue and homogenized with the Bead Ruptor Elite Bead Mill Homogenizer (OMNI International) at 4.00 m/s for 20 s in one cycle. Aliquots of LC-MS grade water (5 mg each) were used as blank and extracted in an identical manner as study samples. The supernatants were collected from homogenates (and blanks) after centrifugation at 16,000 rcf for 10 min at 4°C. Quality control study pools (QCSP) were prepared by pooling 40 µL of the supernatant from each study sample. The supernatants (120 µL) of each study sample, blanks, and QCSPs were transferred to a new set of tubes and dried under Speed-vac. Each dried residue was reconstituted in a 100 µL water-methanol (95:5) mixture containing 500 ng/mL  l -tryptophan-d5. A 5-µL aliquot of the reconstituted supernatant was used for the metabolomics analysis.   Figure 1.     Open in a new tab   Overfeeding leads to obesity and increased kidney weight. Analysis and Data Acquisition   Untargeted metabolomics data were acquired on a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled with a Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (UPLC-HR-MS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were separated via an HSS T3 C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters Corporation) at 50°C with binary mobile phase of water ( A ) and methanol ( B ) with 0.1% formic acid (vol/vol) for each. The UHPLC linear gradient started from 2%  B , and increased to 100%  B  in 16 min and then held for 4 min, with a flow rate at 400 µL/min. The untargeted data were acquired from 70 to 1,050 m/z using the data-dependent acquisition mode. Progenesis QI (version 2.1, Waters Corporation) was used for peak picking, alignment, and normalization. Background signals irrelevant to the biological samples were excluded if the mean of intensity across blanks is greater than that across QCSPs based on the unnormalized data. The remaining signals were normalized by Progenesis QI using the “normalize to all” feature. Identification and Annotation of Metabolites   Signal identification/annotation were conducted by Progenesis QI (version 2.1, Waters Corporation) and using ADAP-KDB algorithms for matching against an in-house physical standard library developed with experimental spectra data from more than 2,500 compounds (analyzed under identical conditions to study samples), and against public databases (HDMB, NIST, and METLIN) that include experimental or theoretical data. Each of the identified/annotated metabolites were labeled with an ontology system that provides the evidence of retention time (RT), exact mass (MS), tandem mass fragmentation (MS/MS), and/or isotopic ion pattern (Supplemental Table S1) ( 9 ,  10 ). Electron Microscopy and Image Analysis   Transmission electron microscopy was carried out on three additional zebrafish kidneys per group following fixation in EM fixative (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Samples were stained with osmium tetroxide and counterstained with aqueous saturated uranyl acetate. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were obtained using Leica UC6 Ultratome and imaged using a JEM-1400 plus (JEOL) at 120 kV with image acquisition on a Gatan digital camera. We identified all of the cells within the available high magnification (×5,000) images of the tubules (6 sections/group, 13 individual cells in the OF group and 14 individual cells in the ND group). Using artificial intelligence-based image segmentation software trained on previous TEM images of zebrafish kidney tissue (Biodock,  www.biodock.ai ), 200 mitochondria from each group were identified and their two-dimensional characteristics (area, perimeter, major, and minor axis lengths) were measured, and the area-to-perimeter ratio and eccentricity for each mitochondrion were calculated. Statistical, Multivariate, and Pathway Analysis   GraphPad Prism was used for statistical analyses of zebrafish and kidney weights with the data expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and used to calculate each signal/metabolite’s  P  value (Welch’s  t  test) and fold change (FC, based on the mean) for the two phenotypic groups. A two-sided  P  value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.   The normalized metabolomics data were visualized by SIMCA 15.0 (Umerics, Umeå, Sweden) using unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) and supervised orthogonal partial least squares discriminate analysis (OPLS-DA). Metaboanalyst 5.0 was used to conduct clustering analysis via the statistical analysis (one factor) module and pathway analysis by using the functional analysis module (MS Peaks) built on the Mummichog algorithm and gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) ( 11 ,  12 ). All normalized signals (9,593) that remained after data preprocessing and quality control were submitted to the pathway analysis tool, together with the calculated  P  value and fold changes (presented as T-score). All signals were searched against the zebrafish pathway library with exact mass (m/z) with the following parameters: positive ion mode, mass tolerance 3 ppm with enforcing primary ions, using a  P  value cutoff at 0.05 when evaluating the size of permutation group that the algorithm used for selecting significant signals to match all possible metabolites in the pathway. Significance of the perturbed pathways was determined using a combined  P  value < 0.05 (Mummichog and GSEA). The enriched empirical annotations by Mummichog algorithm were verified by matching the corresponding signals (RT_exact mass) against the in-house physical standard library and public databases (NIST and HDMB). RESULTS   Overfeeding Leads to Increased Body and Kidney Weight   We randomly assigned fish to one of the two dietary groups: overfed (OF) or normal diet (ND), with 36 fish in the OF group and 40 in the ND group. At baseline, there was no significant difference in body weight between groups (mean weight = 207 mg in ND and 195 mg in OF,  P  = 0.3). After 8 wk, the body weights of individual fish in the OF group were significantly greater than in ND-fed controls (mean weight = 569 mg in OF vs. 261 mg in ND,  P  = 0.003,  Fig. 1 B ). Kidney weights in the OF group were also greater (mean kidney weight = 1.6 mg in OF vs. 0.7 mg in ND,  Fig. 1 C ). Overfeeding Leads to Abnormal Mitochondrial Morphology and Lipid Droplet Accumulation   We used electron microscopy to evaluate changes in cell morphology. Tubular cells from ND-fed fish were densely packed with elongated mitochondria with well-demarcated cristae with few vesicles ( Fig. 2,  A  and  C ). Tubular cell mitochondria from fish fed the OF diet, on the other hand, were shorter and rounded with loss of cristae ( Fig. 2,  B  and  D , arrows). This loss of cristae has previously been associated with mitochondrial dysfunction in obesity-related glomerulopathy ( 13 ). In addition to mitochondrial abnormalities, tubular cells also exhibited lipid droplet accumulation ( Fig. 2,  B  and  D , arrowheads), something we had previously observed in the setting of both high-fat and high-calorie feeding in zebrafish ( 8 ). Quantification of changes in the mitochondria revealed that mitochondria in the OF group were shorter and rounder than those in the ND group, with decreased eccentricity ( Fig. 2 E ) and area-to-perimeter ratio ( Fig. 2 F ). Mitochondria were present in podocytes in both conditions, but, consistent with prior work, at a much lower density than in tubular cells ( 14 ). Because of these changes in mitochondrial morphology, we hypothesized that overfeeding would also lead to changes in cellular metabolism in the kidney.   Figure 2. Overfeeding Induces Significant Differences in Kidney Metabotype   Untargeted metabolomics analysis was conducted on fish kidneys with each of the biological replicates including 5–8 kidneys collected from the same diet group. After data preprocessing, normalization, and filtration, we obtained 9,593 signals for the untargeted dataset. Unsupervised and supervised multivariate analyses ( Fig. 3,  A  and  B ) were used to visualize the differentiation of the metabolic profiles between fish receiving OF and ND diets, and the model statistics from the unsupervised PCA (R2X = 0.524) and the supervised OPLS-DA (R2X = 0.813, Q2 = 0.931) indicated that metabolomic profiles of kidneys from OF fish diverged from those of the ND-fed fish. The volcano plot in  Fig. 3 C  shows metabolites/signals that are significantly differentiated between the OF and ND groups; 661 metabolites/signals were determined as differentiators using the criteria of |FC|>2.0 and  P  < 0.05. Among the 661 metabolite/signal differentiators, 232 signals were identified or annotated by matching against the in-house library or public database based on exact mass, retention time, and/or tandem MS/MS spectra with the evidence labeled from OL1 to PDd (Supplemental Table S1). Among these, 15 were identified with the highest confidence levels (OL1 and OL2a). Supplemental Fig. S1 shows the top 20 features contributing to the OPLS-DA discrimination by variable importance in projection (VIP); VIP score is a measure of a variable or feature’s importance to the model, with important features having VIP scores greater than one. The top 50 differentiators (by  t  test significance) are annotated as fatty acids, lipids, and glucose metabolites ( Table 2 ). The majority of these metabolites ( 15 ) were decreased in the OF group versus ND group.   Figure 3.
