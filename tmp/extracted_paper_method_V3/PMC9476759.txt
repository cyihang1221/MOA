<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objective</h3>
<p>The quality evaluation of herbal products remains a big challenge. Traceable markers are the core concept of the authentication of herbal products. However, the discovery of traceable markers is labor-intensive and time-consuming. The aim of this study is to develop a convenient approach to rapidly screen the traceable markers for herbal product authentication.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>Commercial Jing Liqueur and its 22 species of herbal ingredients were analyzed using HPLC-QTOF-MS and GC–MS to characterize nonvolatile and volatile chemicals. The acquired data were imported into MZmine 2 software for mass detection, chromatogram building, deconvolution and alignment. The aligned data were exported into a csv file and then traceable markers were selected using the built-in filter function in Excel. Finally, the traceable markers were identified by searching against online databases or publications, some of which were confirmed by reference standards.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>A total of 288 chemical features transferred from herbal materials to Jing Liqueur product were rapidly screened out. Among them, 52 markers detected by HPLC-QTOF-MS were annotated, while nine volatile markers detected by GC–MS were annotated. Moreover, 30 of these markers were confirmed by comparing with reference standards. A chemical fingerprint consisting of traceable markers was finally generated to ensure the authentication and quality consistency of Jing Liqueur.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p>A strategy for rapid discovery of traceable markers in herbal products using MZmine 2 software was developed.</p></section><section id="kg005" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> GC–MS, herbal products, HPLC-QTOF-MS, Jing Liqueur, MZmine 2, traceable markers</p></section></section><section id="s0005"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0005">Herbal products have been widely used as both therapeutic medicines and functional foods in healthcare worldwide (<a href="#b0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0005">Aparicio-Soto et al., 2016</a>, <a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">Guo et al., 2017</a>). As the application of herbal products is increasingly integrated into the modern health care system, their quality has been one of the most important concerns in this field (<a href="#b0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0010">Cañigueral et al., 2008</a>, <a href="#b0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0070">Liang et al., 2004</a>). However, herbal products, commonly generated from multiple raw materials, have extremely complex chemical compositions and undergo sophistic manufacturing processes, which makes quality evaluation a challenging task.</p>
<p id="p0010">Chemical markers are often the bioactive basis for both raw materials and their related products, become a core concept of herbal product authentication (<a href="#b0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0020">Fan et al., 2006</a>, <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">Liu et al., 2018</a>, <a href="#b0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0130">Wang et al., 2017</a>, <a href="#b0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0145">Zhao et al., 2019</a>). Traceable chemical markers are compounds that exist in any form of an herbal medicine such as the raw materials, pieces, extracts and finished products. In general, traceable chemical markers are selected by analyzing the variation of targeted chemicals throughout the whole production process, which is labor-intensive and time-consuming (<a href="#b0080" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0080">Liu et al., 2020</a>, <a href="#b0140" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0140">Yu et al., 2013</a>, <a href="#b0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0145">Zhao et al., 2019</a>). Herein, we proposed that potential traceable markers could be rapidly discovered by comparing the chemical profiles in the raw and finished forms of herbal products.</p>
<p id="p0015">Liquid/gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LC/GC–MS) has become the most powerful tool to profile the chemical components in herbal products because of its high selectivity, sensitivity and throughput, as well as the ability to generate specific information including molecular mass and structural characteristics (<a href="#b0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0030">Gomathi et al., 2015</a>, <a href="#b0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0055">Lai et al., 2015</a>, <a href="#b0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0085">Müller and Bracher, 2015</a>, <a href="#b0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0135">Weitzel, 2011</a>). MZmine 2 is an open-source toolbox for the processing and visualization of mass spectrometry based on molecular profile data, which can automatically process a large number of mass spectra in numerous ways, including mass detection, chromatogram building, deconvolution, alignment and compound identification (<a href="#b0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0095">Pluskal, Castillo, Villar-Briones, &amp; Orešič, 2010</a>). As mentioned above, traceable chemical markers exist in both raw materials and finished products. That is, after feature detection and alignment in MZmine 2, the potential traceable markers can be highlighted by comparing the feature distribution among the raw materials and their related products. Although there were numerous analogous free software suites like XCMS (<a href="#b0125" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0125">Tautenhahn, Patti, Rinehart, &amp; Siuzdak, 2012</a>), OpenMS (<a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">Röst et al., 2016</a>) or SMART (<a href="#b0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0065">Liang et al., 2016</a>), MZmine 2 is one of the most popular and user-friendly packages for its straightforward operations for nonexpert users and a flexible and modular platform for MS data processing (<a href="#bib147" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib147">Olivon, Grelier, Roussi, Litaudon, &amp; Touboul, 2017</a>).</p>
<p id="p0020">Jing Liqueur, composed of 22 species of herbal materials, is a very popular healthcare liqueur with anti-fatigue, anti-inflammation and immunity-enhancing activities (<a href="#b0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0025">Feng et al., 2013</a>, <a href="#b0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0110">Shan et al., 2018</a>). The trace levels, large number, and diverse structures and properties of the chemical components in Jing Liqueur increase the difficulty of its authentication. In this study, LC-MS and GC–MS were complementarily applied to characterize nonvolatile and volatile compounds in both the herbal materials and finished product of Jing Liqueur. The MZmine 2 tool was utilized to detect the mass features in all the samples and those features were aligned according to the mass-to-charge ratio (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em>) and retention time (rt). The chemical transitivity from raw materials to products was visualized to select potential traceable markers, which were then identified by comparing with reference standards or by searching against the available MS/MS databases and publications (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>). Consequently, a total of 288 features were transferred into Jing Liqueur from the 22 raw materials and 61 were annotated. In addition, considering the practicability of authentication, a chemical fingerprint consisting of traceable markers of Jing Liqueur was constructed.</p>

<section id="s0030"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3.1. HPLC-QTOF-MS method</h4>
<p id="p0045">Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent 1290 LC system (Agilent Technologies, USA). The samples were separated on an Agilent Zorbax Extend-C<sub>18</sub> (octadecyl chemically bonded phase silica gel) analytical column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 µm) at 25 °C. The mobile phase was composed of solvent A (water containing 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (ACN containing 0.1% formic acid). The elution gradient was as follows: 0–2.5 min, 10% B; 2.5–20 min, 10%–20% B; 20–35 min, 20%–35% B; 35–40 min, 35%–60% B; 40–45 min, 60%–90% B; 45–50 min, 90%–95% B; 50–60 min, 95% B. The injection volume was set at 2 μL, and the flow rate was set at 0.8 mL/min.</p>
<p id="p0050">The above HPLC system was coupled to an Agilent 6545 QTOF-MS spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. The capillary voltage was set at 4000 V. The fragmentor voltage was set at 135 V. The flow rate of drying-gas (N<sub>2</sub>) was 10 L/min with a temperature of 350 °C, and the nebulizer pressure was 35 psi. The sheath gas was 11.0 L/min with a temperature of 350 °C, the cone voltage was 65 V and OCT 1 RF Vpp was set at 750 V. Mass spectra were recorded over a mass-to-charge ratio (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em>) range of 50–1700 using both positive and negative ion detection modes. Multiple collision energies at 15, 25, 35, and 50 V were set to acquire MS/MS data.</p></section><section id="s0035"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3.2. GC–MS method</h4>
<p id="p0055">GC analyses were performed using an Agilent 7890B GC (Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with an Agilent-5977A MSD (Agilent Technologies, USA) and an Agilent 19091S-433UI HP-5MS (5% phenyl-methylpolysiloxan) Ultra Inert (30 m × 250 μm, 0.25 µm) column. Helium (purity ≥ 99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. A 1 μL aliquot of sample was injected in split mode at a split ratio of 10:1 and at an injection temperature of 250 °C. The oven temperature was programmed to increase from an initial temperature of 40 °C (held for 5 min) to 250 °C at 8 °C/min. All samples were analyzed with electron ionization (70 eV) in full scan mode.</p></section></section><section id="s0040"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. Data processing in MZmine 2 software</h3>
<p id="p0060">Raw data files were converted into the open format mzML using MSConvert software, which was a part of the cross-platform ProteoWizard program (<a href="http://proteowizard.sourceforge.net/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://proteowizard.sourceforge.net/</a>). An optimized MZmine 2.41.2 workflow was developed for feature list generation.</p>
<p id="p0065">For HPLC-QTOF-MS data, centroid mass detection was performed with a noise level of 0 and then the ADAP chromatogram builder was implemented including a minimum group size of 5, a group intensity threshold of 10<sup>3</sup>, and a <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance set to 0.01 Da or a relative tolerance of 2 × 10<sup>−5</sup>. Then, the Wavelets (ADAP) algorithm was applied for chromatogram deconvolution including a signal-to-noise ratio of 10, a minimum feature height of 5 × 10<sup>3</sup>, a coefficient threshold of 110, a peak duration range of 0–10 min and an rt wavelet range of 0–0.1 min. The alignment was performed using RANSCAN algorithm with an <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance of 0.01 Da or a relative tolerance of 2 × 10<sup>−5</sup>, an rt tolerance of 0.5 min and an rt tolerance after correction of 0.1 min. RANSCAN iterations were estimated automatically and the threshold value was set at 4 s. In addition, isotopes were dismissed using an isotopic peak grouper with an <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance of 2, an rt tolerance of 0.1 and a maximum charge of 2; A duplicate peak filter was performed with an <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance of 0.01 and an rt tolerance of 0.1 min. The processed feature list was exported as a csv file containing rt, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> and peak area data of different samples.</p>
<p id="p0070">For GC–MS data, the workflow included the centroid mass detection algorithm, with a noise level of 0. ADAP chromatogram builder was performed with a minimum group size of 5, a group intensity threshold of 10<sup>3</sup>, and an <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance of 0.1. The next step was chromatogram deconvolution by applying the Wavelets (ADAP) algorithm, including a signal-to-noise ratio of 10, a minimum feature height of 10<sup>3</sup>, and a coefficient of 50. Finally, the detected features among different samples were aligned using the hierachical aligner algorithm with an <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance of 0.1 and an rt tolerance of 0.02. The aligned rt, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>, and peak area data were exported as a csv file.</p></section></section><section id="s0045"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results</h2>
<section id="s0050"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Analysis of nonvolatile chemicals based on HPLC-QTOF-MS</h3>
<p id="p0075">Due to the occurrence of complicated compounds in the 22 species of raw materials, HPLC-QTOF-MS analysis was performed in both positive and negative modes (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a> and <a href="#s0090" class="usa-link">Fig. S1</a>). To reduce the false positive results from high-intensity noise signals, features with peak areas over 5000 were defined as real features in the samples. In positive scan mode, 3150 features were detected, only 120 features were discovered in Jing Liqueur. That is, over 90% of the chemical components in raw herbal materials were either lost or diluted to levels below the limits of detection after the complex manufacturing process. Similarly, 1816 features were observed in negative scan mode, but only 116 were observed in Jing Liqueur.</p>

<p id="p0085">The detected features were aligned using MZmine 2 software and a csv file containing retention time, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> and peak area data of the different samples was generated. Traceable markers should exist both in some raw materials and in the Jing Liqueur product, which was used as a limitation to rapidly screen markers based on the filter function in Excel 2016. As a consequence, 205 traceable chemicals were discovered by HPLC-QTOF-MS analysis: 96 were detected in positive mode and 109 in negative mode; 83 volatile traceable chemicals were discovered by GC–MS analysis.</p>
<p id="p0090">The 205 nonvolatile features detected by HPLC-QTOF-MS were identified by searching against online databases, including MassBank (<a href="#b0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0040">Horai et al., 2010</a>), ChemSpider (<a href="#b0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0090">Pence &amp; Williams, 2010</a>) and ResPect (<a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">Sawada et al., 2012</a>) with a relative <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance of 10<sup>−5</sup> and similarity score of 0.7. Due to the limited natural product data in online databases, a custom database consisting of the 50 reference standards was constructed, including their rt, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> and characteristic product ion data. <div class="tw-foot p"><div class="fn" id="fn1"><p>#Represents that this compound was confirmed by comparing with reference standard.</p></div></div></section><p id="p0095">Flavonoids are a key group of compounds identified in Jing Liqueur. Of the 18 annotated flavonoids, 11 of them were present as flavonol glycosides and the others were present as isoflavone glycosides. Reference standards of flavonoids are easily obtained; Thus, 13 of the annotated flavonoids were identified by comparing with reference standards and the remaining were tentatively identified based on available databases or publications. For example, feature <strong>17</strong> (417.1164, [M + H]<sup>+</sup>, C<sub>21</sub>H<sub>20</sub>O<sub>9</sub>), a representative isoflavone glycoside, was identified as puerarin by its reference standard, and its typical fragmentation patterns were shown in <a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>A. Two diagnostic product ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 297.0786 and 267.0678 were observed, and deduced to be generated by cleavage at the glucopyranosyl moiety. Feature <strong>18</strong> (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>B) with a precursor ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 549.1584 ([M + H]<sup>+</sup>, C<sub>26</sub>H<sub>28</sub>O<sub>13</sub>) also generated product ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 297.0743 and 267.0657, which indicated its structural similarity with feature <strong>17</strong>.</p>

<figcaption><p>Characteristic fragmentation patterns of typical flavonoids.</p></figcaption></figure><p id="p0100">The product ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 417.1146 ([M + H]<sup>+</sup> - C<sub>5</sub>H<sub>8</sub>O<sub>4</sub>) demonstrated that feature <strong>18</strong> was a conjugated O-xyloside. Combined with the results obtained by database searching, feature <strong>18</strong> was finally annotated as puerarin-7-<em>O</em>-oxyloside.</p>
<p id="p0105">Feature <strong>43</strong> (677.2418, [M + H]<sup>+</sup>, C<sub>33</sub>H<sub>41</sub>O<sub>15</sub>) was a representative flavonol glycoside and was identified as icariin by the corresponding reference standard. As shown in <a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>C, the characteristic product ions of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 531.1852 ([M + H]<sup>+</sup> - C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>10</sub>O<sub>4</sub>), 369.1309 ([M + H]<sup>+</sup> - C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>10</sub>O<sub>4</sub> - C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>10</sub>O<sub>5</sub>) and 313.0662 ([M + H]<sup>+</sup> - C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>10</sub>O<sub>4</sub> - C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>10</sub>O<sub>5</sub> - C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>10</sub>) were generated by the neutral loss of glycosides or the cleavage of the side chain of isoamylene. Feature <strong>50</strong> produced very similar product ions and its characteristic fragmentation patterns are shown in <a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>D. The product ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 369.1317 and 313.0692 demonstrated the similar chemical scaffold of feature <strong>50</strong> to that of feature <strong>43</strong>. The mass difference of 162 (C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>10</sub>O<sub>5</sub>) indicated that feature <strong>43</strong> could be derived from feature <strong>50</strong> by the loss of glucose. Thus, feature <strong>43</strong> was finally annotated as icariside II, which has been reported to be an important component in <em>Epimedii Folium</em> (EF) (<a href="#b0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0015">Choi et al., 2008</a>).</p>
<p id="p0110">In the same way, the other nonvolatile components were annotated. Their characteristic product ions are summarized in <a href="#t0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>.</p>
<p id="p0115">Among the 83 unique volatile features in Jing Liqueur detected by GC–MS, 23 could be detected in CR, 24 detected in CF, 27 detected in CC, 27 detected in AF, 22 detected in AOF and 57 detected in ASR. The compounds were identified by comparing their mass spectra with those in the instrument’s National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) library (<a href="#b0115" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0115">Stein, 1995</a>). A score value over 700 was considered necessary for a good match (<a href="#b0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0120">Stein, 2011</a>). To avoid false positive markers resulting from automatic data processing, the selected traceable features were confirmed by manual extraction of the raw data in MassHunter software. And then eight annotated volatile compounds were defined as traceable markers. The detailed information is shown in <a href="#t0020" class="usa-link">Table 4</a>.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/t0020/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="s0065"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.4. Generation of chemical marker fingerprint</h3>
<p id="p0120">As shown in <a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>, among the 61 annotated markers, some were exclusively present in certain herbal species, and the others coexisted in all 22 species of raw materials. The chemical profile of Jing Liqueur was integrated by those of all 22 herbal species. The specificity of chemical markers was evaluated by their distribution ratio among the 22 raw herbal species. Theoretically, specific markers of an herbal material contributed the most to the relative intensity of the MS profile of Jing Liqueur. By limiting the distribution ratio to 50%, 29 chemical markers derived from all the herbal species except Allii Tuberosi Semen (ATS) were selected. With regard to ATS, the contribution intensity of the 52 features was evaluated by the ratio of the intensity of ATS features to the total intensity of features of the 22 herbal species. Feature <strong>1</strong> contributed the most to the corresponding component in Jing Liqueur, which was added to the marker group. The chemical profiles of the 30 markers were extracted from the raw LC-MS data of Jing Liqueur to generate the chemical marker fingerprint, which could be utilized to guide its authentication (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>).</p>

<p id="p0125">The quality evaluation of herbal products has troubled scientists and researchers. Quality markers with transitivity and traceability, proposed by <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">Liu et al. (2018)</a>, have been comprehensively explored to control the quality of herbal products. However, the discovery of quality markers was very tedious, which included the characterization of chemical ingredients, biosynthetic pathway analysis, bioactivity evaluation and conformation of quality markers (<a href="#b0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0060">Li et al., 2019</a>). The transitivity and traceability of chemical markers are the core concepts of herbal product authentication. Based on this view, we developed a novel strategy to rapidly discover traceable markers of herbal products.</p>
<p id="p0130">MZmine 2 software is usually applied for automatic mass detection, chromatogram building and deconvolution before batch identification of complex mixtures (<a href="#b0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0050">Korf et al., 2019</a>, <a href="#b0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0095">Pluskal et al., 2010</a>). The alignment function in MZmine 2 software was designed to align the features among different sample runs. Traceable markers exist both in certain materials and in the final product. Therefore, by aligning the chemical features among herbal materials and the product using MZmine 2, traceable chemical markers were rapidly discovered.</p>
<p id="p0135">In this work, MZmine 2 was first used to discover traceable markers of herbal products. This approach is very convenient. Once the raw data of the materials and products were imported, mass detection, chromatogram building, deconvolution and alignment could be completed in a few minutes. The aligned features were exported into a csv file. The traceable features could be rapidly screened using the filter function in Excel. In addition, the online and custom database search functions built in MZmine 2 software lead to an expedient identify cation of chemical markers.</p>
<p id="p0140">There are still some points that need to be improved. First, the identification of traceable markers is hampered by the limitation of reference standards and available databases, which is a common issue in this field. With the increasing knowledge of natural products and the expanding databases, this issue will be resolved. Second, the chemical marker fingerprint of Jing Liqueur must be validated using additional commercial samples. Once the product fingerprint is confirmed, it can be embedded into data analysis software. Coupled with the fingerprint similarity evaluation system, real-time authentication of Jing Liqueur can be realized.</p></section><section id="s0075"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">5. Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0145">In this work, a novel strategy for the rapid discovery of traceable markers of herbal products was developed. Jing Liqueur, produced from 22 species of herbal materials, was taken as a case study. A total of 61 traceable markers were rapidly discovered and annotated. The chemical fingerprint of traceable markers was also generated, and could be used to authenticate Jing Liqueur products.</p></section><section id="sec20"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Declaration of competing interest</h2>
