</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abs0001"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objective</h3>
<p>Gingival aging is closely associated with oxidative stress, yet its molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study employed proteomic analysis of gingival tissues from young and aged mice, combined with functional experiments, to elucidate the central role of oxidative stress in gingival aging and its underlying mechanisms.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and Methods</h3>
<p>Proteins differentially expressed in old mice compared to young mice were screened using a 4D label-free mass spectrometry platform. The differentially expressed proteins were then subjected to bioinformatics analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), and Protein-Protein Interaction Networks (PPI). At the same time, H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> was used to stimulate human periodontal ligament fibroblasts to induce an aging model. Combined with H&amp;E staining, immunohistochemistry, Western blot, SOD activity detection, and other experiments, the relationship between oxidative stress and gingival aging was comprehensively evaluated across.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>The level of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in gingival tissue of aged mice was significantly increased, accompanied by decreased expression of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and inhibition of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Proteomic analysis identified 224 differentially expressed proteins, of which up-regulated proteins were related to redox function, while down-regulated proteins were involved in cell proliferation and tissue repair. Further experiments showed that inhibition of cytochrome P450 (CYP450) reduced ROS levels and alleviated aging-related phenotypes. In addition, the functional inhibition of EGFR aggravated the decrease in the activity of gingival fibroblasts, and the antioxidant 1-aminobenzotriazole (ABT) pretreatment could partially reverse this effect.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h3>
<p>Our findings indicate that oxidative stress drives gingival functional decline and tissue aging by disrupting EGFR-mediated signaling pathways and amplifying CYP450 activity. This study provides novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of gingival aging and highlights the potential of targeting oxidative stress and EGFR signaling as anti-aging therapeutic strategies.</p></section><section id="keys0001" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Aging, Proteomics, Oxidative stress, Gingiva, Signaling pathways</p></section></section><section id="sec0001"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="para0006">Aging is a continuous and irreversible biological phenomenon characterized by the gradual deterioration of the body's structural and functional integrity.<a href="#bib0001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0001"><sup>1</sup></a> This deterioration leads to impaired physiological functions over time.<a href="#bib0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0002"><sup>2</sup></a> There are many causes of aging, including DNA instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, deregulated nutrient-sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cellular senescence.<a href="#bib0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0003"><sup>3</sup></a> Among them, oxidative stress, which indicates an excess production or reduced scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS), is believed to be a pivotal trigger of the aging process. The mechanism of aging induced by oxidative stress involves ROS damaging proteins, DNA, and lipids, which in turn impairs cell function and accelerates the aging process.<a href="#bib0004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0004"><sup>4</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0005"><sup>5</sup></a></p>
<p id="para0007">The gingiva, as a critical component of the periodontium, forms a protective barrier against microbial invasion through its biochemical and immunological defenses.<a href="#bib0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0006"><sup>6</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0007"><sup>7</sup></a> In aging gingiva, both cellular composition and tissue properties undergo significant changes.<a href="#bib0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0008"><sup>8</sup></a> Lamster et al. found that the increased presence of inflammatory and immune cells in aging gingival tissue triggers inflammatory responses via the release of mediators.<a href="#bib0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0009"><sup>9</sup></a> Additionally, the findings of Klein et al. suggest that aging is associated with a decrease in elastic fibers within the gingival connective tissue, which leads to thicker and more disordered collagen bundles.<a href="#bib0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0010"><sup>10</sup></a> Aging gingiva is also more vulnerable to conditions like periodontitis, which can escalate into broader oral and systemic health issues.<a href="#bib0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0011"><sup>11</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0012"><sup>12</sup></a></p>
<p id="para0008">Gingival aging involves a complex interplay of cellular mechanisms and molecular pathways, with oxidative stress playing a central role. For instance, Guo et al. demonstrated that oxidative stress-induced DNA and mitochondrial damage lead human gingival fibroblasts to adopt a senescent phenotype.<a href="#bib0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0013"><sup>13</sup></a> Similarly, Daisuke et al. found that oxidative stress impairs gingival wound healing in diabetes.<a href="#bib0014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0014"><sup>14</sup></a> Despite these findings, there is still a lack of research revealing the hallmarks of aging gingiva, and the detailed mechanisms through which oxidative stress occurs in aging gingiva and how it further drives gingival aging remain unclear.</p>
<p id="para0009">To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a proteomic analysis by comparing gingival tissues from old and young mice and determined that EGFR / PI3K-AKT signaling is the key axis of gingival aging. This study aims to identify key proteins involved in gingival aging and establish a mechanism framework for redox imbalance in age-related periodontal diseases. It provides new possibilities for the development of targeted therapies in the future to combat gingival aging.</p></section><section id="sec0002"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Method</h2>
<section id="sec0003"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Age-stratified murine model under SPF conditions for comparative aging studies</h3>
<p id="para0010">SPF-grade male C57BL/6J mice were used at 2 months (young) and 20 months (aged)​​ (n = 10 per group). All mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Shanghai Jiao Tong University. Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xinhua Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (XHEC-NSFC-2019-240) and followed the ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (22-26°C, 40-60% humidity). After intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (150 mg/kg)​and euthanasia, periodontal tissues were collected. Four randomly selected gingival tissue samples from each group were used for protein sequencing.</p></section><section id="sec0004"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Cell culture</h3>
<p id="para0011">Human periodontal ligament fibroblasts (hPDLFs) were cultured in a special medium (CM-H136, Pricella) and placed in a constant temperature cell culture box containing 5 % CO2 at a set temperature of 37°C.</p></section><section id="sec0005"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">H&amp;E staining</h3>
<p id="para0012">The tissue samples were fixed, embedded in paraffin and sectioned using H&amp;E staining kit (C0105S, Beyotime ), followed by xylene (534056, Sigma) dewaxing for 10 minutes, gradient ethanol hydration; the nucleus was stained with hematoxylin for 5 minutes, the cytoplasm was stained with eosin for 1 minute, dehydrated with gradient ethanol, and transparentized with xylene for 2 times, once every 5 minutes. Finally, neutral gum was sealed and observed under a microscope.</p></section><section id="sec0006"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunohistochemical staining</h3>
<p id="para0013">The collected mouse gingival tissue specimens were made into paraffin sections, and immunohistochemical staining was performed by dewaxing and hydration, rinsing, antigen repair (citric acid antigen method), antibody reaction (Anti-TNF alpha antibody, abcam, 5 µg/mL), staining and dehydration. Neutral gum was mounted, observed under a microscope, photographed and scored.</p></section><section id="sec0007"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Protein extraction</h3>
<p id="para0014">SDT buffer was added to the periodontium sample, and transferred to 2 ml tubes with amount quartz sand. The lysate was homogenized by MP Fastprep-24 Automated Homogenizer (6.0m/s, 30s, twice). The homogenate was ultrasonically treated. The collected hPDLFs were added to the lysate ( P0013B, Beyotime ) for 30 min. The treated cells and tissue samples were centrifuged at 14000 g for 15 min, and the supernatant was filtered with a 0.22 μm filter. The filtrate was quantified with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012, Beyotime). Then the samples were boiled for 10 minutes and stored at -80 °C.</p></section><section id="sec0008"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">SDS-PAGE electrophoresis</h3>
<p id="para0015">20 µg of proteins for each sample were mixed with 6X loading buffer respectively and boiled for 5 min. The proteins were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gel. Protein bands were visualized by Coomassie Blue R-250 staining.</p></section><section id="sec0009"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Western blot</h3>
<p id="para0016">Protein samples and protein Marker were added for gel electrophoresis for 1-2 h. The membrane was transferred for 1.5 h under ice bath conditions and blocked with 5% Blotting Grade (P0216-300g, Beyotime) for 2 h. EGFR antibody (2232S, Cell Signaling Technology , 1 : 1000) and GAPDH antibody (2118T, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000) were added respectively, and placed overnight at 4 °C. HRP-labeled secondary antibody (1: 10000) was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. After washing the membrane, the development photograph was taken, and the target band was selected by ImageJ software to measure the gray value for statistics.</p></section><section id="sec0010"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Total SOD activity detection</h3>
<p id="para0017">Using the SOD detection kit (S0101S, Beyotime), an appropriate amount of sod sample preparation solution was added to the cells or tissues, centrifuged at about 12,000 g at 4° C for 3-5 min, and the supernatant was retained. The protein concentration was measured by BCA method and the protein was diluted with diluent. An appropriate amount of WST-8 working solution and reaction starting working solution were prepared according to the volume of 160μl per reaction, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm.</p></section><section id="sec0011"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">FASP enzyme</h3>
<p id="para0018">A total of 100 μg protein from each sample was initially reduced in 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 100 °C for 5 minutes. To eliminate detergents, DTT, and other small molecules, the samples underwent repeated ultrafiltration using UA buffer (8 M urea, 150 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5) with 30 kDa centrifugal filter units (Sartorius). Subsequently, alkylation was performed by adding 100 μL of 100 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) prepared in UA buffer, followed by incubation in the dark for 30 minutes. The filters were then sequentially rinsed 3 times with UA buffer and twice with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (NH<sub>4</sub>HCO<sub>3</sub>) to remove excess reagents. Enzymatic digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C by adding 4 μg of sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega) in 40 μL of 50 mM NH<sub>4</sub>HCO<sub>3</sub>. The resulting peptides were recovered by centrifugation and desalted using a C18 reverse-phase column. Peptide concentration was estimated by measuring UV absorbance at 280 nm, applying an extinction coefficient of 1.1 for a 0.1% (g/L) solution, calculated based on the average content of tryptophan and tyrosine residues in invertebrate proteins.</p></section><section id="sec0012"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">NanoElute chromatography</h3>
<p id="para0019">Peptide samples were analyzed using a nanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) interfaced with a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker) equipped with a CaptiveSpray ion source. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a 25 cm × 75 μm fused silica column packed with 1.6 μm C18 particles and an integrated emitter (IonOpticks, Australia). The column was maintained at 50 °C using a thermostatically controlled oven (Sonation GmbH, Germany). Prior to sample loading, the column was equilibrated with 4 column volumes of buffer A (0.1% formic acid in Milli-Q water) at a pressure of 800 bar. Samples were loaded in 100% buffer A and eluted at a constant flow rate of 300 nL/min using a linear gradient.</p></section><section id="sec0013"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Mass spectrometry identification</h3>
<p id="para0020">The timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) was operated in parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode. Data were acquired over a mass-to-charge (m/z) range of 100-1700. Ion mobility separation was performed with a 1/K₀range from 0.75 to 1.40 V·s/cm² and a ramp time of 100 ms. The duty cycle was locked at 100%. Source parameters included a capillary voltage of 1500 V, a dry gas flow of 3 L/min, and a dry temperature of 180 °C. PASEF acquisition comprised 10 MS/MS scans per cycle, with a total cycle time of 1.16 s. Precursors with charge states from 0 to 5 were selected for fragmentation, with dynamic exclusion applied for 0.5 minutes. Target intensity was set at 10,000 with a threshold of 2500, and collision-induced dissociation (CID) energy ranged from 20 to 59 eV.</p></section><section id="sec0014"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Parameters for qualitative and quantitative protein analysis</h3>
<p id="para0021">The raw MS data were processed with MaxQuant (v1.6.17.0). Spectra were searched against a project-specific protein database. The precursor ion mass tolerance was set at 6 ppm for the initial search. Trypsin/P was specified for enzymatic digestion, allowing up to 2 missed cleavages and a 20 ppm tolerance for fragment ions. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification, while N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation were included as variable modifications. A global false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% was applied at both peptide and protein levels. Label-free quantification (LFQ) was based on normalized spectral intensities. Proteins with a fold change above 2 or below 0.5 and <em>P &lt; .</em>05 (Student’s t-test) were identified as differentially expressed.</p></section><section id="sec0015"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Clustering analysis</h3>
<p id="para0022">Quantitative values for target proteins were normalized prior to clustering. The Python package matplotlib was utilized to conduct hierarchical clustering of both samples and protein expression levels (distance metric: Euclidean; linkage: average). The resulting relationships were visualized through a hierarchical heatmap.</p></section><section id="sec0016"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">GO functional annotation</h3>
<p id="para0023">Gene Ontology (GO) annotation was performed using Blast2GO (v1.4.4) in 4 main steps: sequence alignment (BLAST), GO term mapping, annotation, and refinement via ANNEX.</p>
<p id="para0024">Initially, protein sequences were aligned to a designated database using NCBI BLAST+ (ncbi-blast-2.3.0+) on a Linux platform, retaining the top 10 hits with E-values ≤ 1e-3. GO terms corresponding to the highest scoring matches were extracted using Blast2GO Command Line (database: go_20190701.obo). During annotation, GO terms were assigned based on sequence homology, term reliability, and GO hierarchy structure. InterProScan (interproscan-5.30-69.0) was applied to detect conserved domains, which were incorporated to enhance functional interpretation. Finally, ANNEX refinement was conducted to improve annotation specificity and link related terms.</p></section><section id="sec0017"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">KEGG pathway annotation</h3>
<p id="para0025">Functional classification of proteins based on KEGG Orthology (KO) was conducted using KOALA (v3.0). Target protein sequences were aligned against the KEGG GENES database (version: KO_INFO_END.txt, 2023.10.17), and KO identifiers were assigned accordingly. Associated pathway data were subsequently retrieved for functional mapping.</p></section><section id="sec0018"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Enrichment analysis for GO and KEGG annotations</h3>
<p id="para0026">GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were carried out using a unified method. Fisher’s Exact Test was applied to evaluate the overrepresentation of specific GO terms or pathways in the target set compared to the background, enabling statistical assessment of enrichment significance.</p></section><section id="sec0019"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction</h3>
<p id="para0027">Total RNA was extracted from gingival tissues of young and aged mice by TRIzol method (15596018CN, Invitrogen). Reverse transcription kit ( R323-01, Vazyme company ) was used to convert it into c DNA, and a reverse transcription reaction system ( 20 μL system ) was configured. <p id="para0028">The hPDLFs were treated with H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2 (</sub>150μM)for 16 hours, and the ABT-treated (HY-103389, MCE, 20μM) hPDLFs were added with a final concentration of 10μM DCFH-DA dilution staining solution (S0033S, Beyotime ) and incubated in a 37℃ cell culture box for 20 minutes. <p id="para0029">The bioinformatic environment ShinyGO 0.76 (available at: <a href="http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go76/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go76/</a>) was used to create networks summarizing overlapping GO terms.</p></section><section id="sec0022"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">GSEA analysis</h3>
<p id="para0030">Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted for all identified genes using the GSEA software. Mouse dataset were obtained from the msigdbr package in R (V7.5.1).</p></section><section id="sec0023"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Protein-protein interactions</h3>
<p id="para0031">First, we found the direct or indirect interaction network between the target protein IDs in the String database, and then, we used the Cytoscape software (V8.11.0.1934) to generate interaction Network Diagram.</p></section><section id="sec0024"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="para0032">Data is expressed as SD ± mean, and analyzed using Graphpad Prism 8 software. Unpaired Student's t-test was used for evaluation. <section id="sec0026"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">The level of ROS in the aging gingival tissue increases significantly</h3>

<p id="para0041">To better understand the relationship between oxidative stress and gingival aging, and to identify potential therapeutic targets, we focused on 8 enriched KEGG pathways and 26 GO biological process and molecular function categories associated with oxidative stress. The differentially expressed proteins from these categories were analyzed using the STRING database, leading to the construction of a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network with 108 nodes and 234 interaction pairs using Cytoscape (v3.9.1) (<a href="#sec0037" class="usa-link">Appendix Figure 2</a>)</p>
<p id="para0042">Through the CytoHubba algorithm, we identified the top ten hub proteins with the highest degree values: EGFR, F2, COL1A1, HSPA1B, VTN, ITGB4, CALR, CAV1, ITGA2B, and ITGA6 (<a href="#fig0007" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>B). Among these, EGFR ranked highest, reinforcing our earlier conclusion that EGFR plays a pivotal role in the altered proliferative pathways in aging gingiva and is closely linked to oxidative stress (<a href="#fig0006" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a>A). To validate the above results, we further employed RT-qPCR experiments to detect the differential expression of <em>Egfr, F2, Col1a1, Hspa1b, Vtn</em>, and <em>Itgb4</em> in the gingival tissues of the control group and the aged group of mice. The results showed that, compared to the control group, the mRNA expression of <em>F2</em> and <em>Vtn</em> was upregulated in the aged mice, while the mRNA expression of <em>Egfr, Col1a1, Hspa1b</em>, and <em>Itgb4</em> exhibited the opposite trend (<a href="#fig0006" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a>B).</p>

<p id="para0043">Given that EGFR received the highest hub score, we selected it for further investigation. Upon treating human periodontal ligament fibroblasts (hPDLFs) with H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> for 24 hours, we observed a marked decrease in EGFR expression (<a href="#fig0007" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>A). Since oxidative stress is known to impair cell proliferation, induce cell cycle arrest, and promote senescence, we hypothesized that the decline in EGFR contributes to the diminished cellular activity observed under oxidative conditions. Indeed, treatment of hPDLFs with the EGFR inhibitor gefitinib reduced cell viability, while N-Acetylcysteine (NAC) pre-treatment restored cell activity, indicating that oxidative stress-induced EGFR downregulation is a key factor underlying the loss of cellular function during aging (<a href="#fig0007" class="usa-link">Figure7</a>B).</p></section></section><section id="sec0032"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p id="para0044">In this study, we observed a significantly increased CEJ–ABC distance and elevated mitochondrial ROS levels in aged gingival tissue, indicating oxidative stress and periodontal degeneration. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that oxidative stress is a major driver of age-related tissue damage.<a href="#bib0016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0016">16</a>, <a href="#bib0017" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0017">17</a>, <a href="#bib0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0018">18</a>, <a href="#bib0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0019">19</a>, <a href="#bib0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0020">20</a> Despite previous observations linking ROS to aging, the specific mechanisms by which oxidative stress impairs gingival structure and signaling pathways have remained unclear. Through integrated proteomic profiling and functional validation, we systematically characterized how oxidative stress modifies critical molecular networks in aging gingiva. These results provide novel insights into gingival aging pathophysiology and may reveal promising therapeutic targets for age-related periodontal disease.</p>
<p id="para0045">GSEA revealed enhanced cytochrome P450 (CYP450) metabolic activity in aging gingiva. Given that CYP enzymes are known contributors to intracellular ROS generation,<a href="#bib0021" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0021"><sup>21</sup></a> we performed functional validation using ABT, a pan-CYP inhibitor. Following ABT treatment in gingival fibroblasts, ROS levels were markedly reduced, confirming that enhanced CYP activity in aged gingival tissue contributes directly to the observed oxidative stress. These results establish a mechanistic link in which aging leads to CYP450 upregulation and consequent ROS accumulation in the gingival microenvironment. This aligns with studies showing that CYP2E1 knockdown reduces oxidative damage in diabetic periodontitis, suggesting that targeting CYP isoforms (such as CYP1A1/2B1) could disrupt the ROS-CYP450 positive feedback loop in aging gingiva. Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis highlighted increased expression of disulfide oxidoreductases, such as glutathione disulfide oxidoreductase, suggesting enhanced thiol oxidation in aged gingiva. While protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) has been shown to produce hydrogen peroxide during disulfide bond formation and accumulate in aged cells,<a href="#bib0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0022">22</a>, <a href="#bib0023" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0023">23</a>, <a href="#bib0024" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0024">24</a> its specific contribution to oxidative stress in the gingiva remains speculative and warrants further investigation.</p>
<p id="para0046">We then explored the detrimental effects of oxidative stress on gingival cells. Beyond causing direct molecular damage, oxidative stress can disrupt essential cellular functions by impairing intracellular signaling. Our KEGG enrichment analysis identified significant downregulation of genes within the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway—including <em>Itgb4, Col6a4</em>, and <em>Egfr</em>—in aged gingival tissue.<a href="#bib0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0025"><sup>25</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0026"><sup>26</sup></a> The core of our findings is the downregulation of EGFR and its downstream PI3K-AKT pathway, with EGFR being a major regulator of cell proliferation and survival,<a href="#bib0027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0027"><sup>27</sup></a> showing reduced expression in aging tissues and fibroblasts treated with H₂O₂. This is consistent with findings in aging skin and vascular endothelium, where the loss of EGFR exacerbates oxidative damage and affects tissue repair.<a href="#bib0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0028"><sup>28</sup></a> Mechanistically, oxidative stress may promote EGFR degradation through ubiquitin-proteasome activation<a href="#bib0029" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0029"><sup>29</sup></a> or inhibit its autophosphorylation by oxidizing cysteines in its kinase domain.<a href="#bib0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0030"><sup>30</sup></a> Notably, the downregulation of HSPA1B suggests reduced chaperone-mediated protein homeostasis, which may exacerbate the accumulation of misfolded proteins under oxidative conditions.<a href="#bib0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0031"><sup>31</sup></a></p>
<p id="para0047">The interplay between the PI3K-AKT pathway and oxidative stress is complex and context-dependent. While PI3K-AKT can promote ROS production through activation of NADPH oxidase, low levels of ROS also modulate this pathway by affecting regulators such as PTEN.<a href="#bib0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0032">32</a>, <a href="#bib0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0033">33</a>, <a href="#bib0034" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0034">34</a>, <a href="#bib0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0035">35</a>, <a href="#bib0036" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0036">36</a>, <a href="#bib0037" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0037">37</a> In aging tissues, however, sustained oxidative stress often leads to PI3K-AKT suppression, impairing cellular survival and repair. This mechanism is supported by recent studies: in skeletal muscle, Polygonatum sibiricum polysaccharide (PSP) reversed mitochondrial dysfunction and enhanced myoblast differentiation by restoring PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, and these effects were blocked by the PI3K inhibitor<a href="#bib0038" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0038"><sup>38</sup></a>; in the brain, lactoferrin improved redox balance and autophagy via modulation of the same pathway.<a href="#bib0039" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0039"><sup>39</sup></a> Together, these findings reinforce our observation that oxidative stress disrupts PI3K-AKT signaling in aging gingiva, and that reactivation of this pathway is critical for maintaining cellular function under oxidative challenge.</p></section><section id="sec0033"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="para0048">In summary, this study reveals the oxidative stress mechanism and its cellular effects on aged gums through proteomic analysis. Our findings deepen the understanding of gum aging and emphasize the complex interactions between oxidative stress, altered signaling pathways, and cell proliferation in aged gums. Future research should further investigate these key proteins and pathways to advance gum health management and anti-aging strategies, providing new targets for maintaining gum health and preventing periodontal disease in the aging population.</p></section><section id="sec0034"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Author contributions</h2>
