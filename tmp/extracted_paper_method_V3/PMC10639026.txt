<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="d96e159"><p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/2778/10639026/bb1295858be8/f-00113-2023r01.jpg" loading="lazy" height="500" width="666" alt="graphic file with name f-00113-2023r01.jpg"></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/d96e159/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></figure><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> diet, metabolomics, obesity, zebrafish</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Obesity is a global epidemic and risk factor for the development of chronic kidney disease. Obesity induces systemic changes in metabolism, but how it affects kidney metabolism specifically is not known. Zebrafish have previously been shown to develop obesity-related kidney pathology and dysfunction when fed hypercaloric diets. To understand the direct effects of obesity on kidney metabolic function, we treated zebrafish for 8 wk with a control and an overfeeding diet. At the end of treatment, we assessed changes in kidney and fish weights and used electron microscopy to evaluate cell ultrastructure. We then performed an untargeted metabolomic analysis on the kidney tissue of fish using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry and used mummichog and gene set enrichment analysis to uncover differentially affected metabolic pathways. Kidney metabolomes differed significantly and consistently between the control and overfed diets. Among 9,593 features, we identified 235 that were significantly different (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) between groups (125 upregulated in overfed diet, 110 downregulated). Pathway analysis demonstrated perturbations in glycolysis and fatty acid synthesis pathways, and analysis of specific metabolites points to perturbations in tryptophan metabolism. Our key findings show that diet-induced obesity leads to metabolic changes in the kidney tissue itself and implicates specific metabolic pathways, including glycolysis and tryptophan metabolism in the pathogenesis of obesity-related kidney disease, demonstrating the power of untargeted metabolomics to identify pathways of interest by directly interrogating kidney tissue.</p>
<p><strong>NEW &amp; NOTEWORTHY</strong> Obesity causes systemic metabolic dysfunction, but how this affects kidney metabolism is less understood. This study used ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry to analyze the kidneys of overfed zebrafish. Metabolites in the kidneys of obese zebrafish revealed perturbations in metabolic pathways including glycolysis and tryptophan metabolism. These data suggest obesity alters metabolism within the kidney, which may play an important role in obesity-related kidney dysfunction.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p>Obesity caused by hypercaloric diets is an increasingly common disorder leading to systemic changes in metabolism, including insulin resistance and hyperlipidemia, and is a risk factor for chronic kidney disease (CKD). The kidney is highly metabolically active, receiving 20% of the cardiac output (<a href="#B1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1">1</a>) and consuming an estimated 400 kcal/kg of tissue/day, on the order of that consumed by the heart (<a href="#B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2">2</a>). Not surprisingly, alterations in kidney metabolism have been demonstrated in both preclinical disease models and in humans with overt CKD. For example, mouse models of polycystic kidney disease develop alterations in alanine, polyamine, and tryptophan metabolism, which correlate with disease progression (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>). Metabolomic analysis of animal models of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) (e.g., <em>db/db</em> mice) also implies an important role for several metabolic pathways in the development of disease, including dysregulation of fatty acid oxidation and disturbances in multiple amino acid metabolic pathways, including those for arginine, proline, and histamine (<a href="#B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4">4</a>). The central role of altered metabolism in the development of diabetic kidney disease is supported by data from humans, which reveal evidence of suppression of mitochondrial function, including the suppression of mitochondrial metabolites and decreased mitochondrial DNA in urinary exosomes from patients with DKD (<a href="#B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5">5</a>). Still, little is known about how kidney tissue metabolism changes in response to overnutrition and obesity before the onset of overt hyperglycemia. Characterizing these changes is necessary to develop targeted interventions (both dietary and pharmacologic) to retard the development and progression of CKD in patients with obesity.</p>
<p>Zebrafish (<em>Danio rerio</em>) are an ideal model for this purpose. The mesonephros of the adult zebrafish is similar to mammalian kidneys, with several 100 nephrons comprising a glomerulus and proximal and distal tubules (<a href="#B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6">6</a>). In addition to this structural similarity, the zebrafish genome contains an ortholog for ∼70% of human genes (<a href="#B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7">7</a>). Their high fecundity, rapid generation time, and amenability to dietary manipulation represent additional advantages to delving into obesity-induced metabolic changes in the zebrafish kidney. Previous work has shown that zebrafish overfed for 8 wk develop both obesity and kidney pathology similar to that seen in humans with obesity, which occurred in the absence of overt hyperglycemia (<a href="#B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8">8</a>). However, no study has evaluated the metabolic changes occurring in the zebrafish kidney in response to diet.</p>
<p>We thus sought to use this model to answer two critical unanswered questions in the field: <em>1</em>) does diet-induced obesity lead to changes in the metabolic phenotype (i.e., the metabotype) of the zebrafish kidney, and, if so, <em>2</em>) what metabolic pathways are specifically altered? Here, we are the first to use untargeted metabolomics of zebrafish kidney tissue to answer these questions. Our primary findings suggest that obesity induces significant alterations in kidney energy metabolism in the overfed state and singles out metabolic pathways of interest, providing new insights into how overnutrition, even early in obesity, directly affects kidney function.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">METHODS</h2>
<section id="sec2-1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Animal Husbandry</h3>
<p>Adult (110 dpf) zebrafish (<em>D. rerio)</em> of the AB wild-type strain obtained from a single clutch were maintained at 28.5°C on a 14:10-h light-dark cycle at a density of no more than five fish/liter on a water circulation system (Techniplast). All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the University of North Carolina Chapel Hill Animal Care and Use Committee. Animals were housed in an American Association for the Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC)-accredited facility in compliance with the <em>Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals</em> as detailed in protocols (<a href="http://dx.doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.bg3jjykn" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">dx.doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.bg3jjykn</a>).</p></section><section id="sec2-2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Feeding Regimens</h3>
<p>Zebrafish were randomized to one of the two diet groups for 8 wk (<em>n</em> = 40 fish/group) in a 1:1 ratio of male:female fish in each group. The normal calorie diet (ND) consisted of a single feeding of Gemma Micro 300 (GM300, Skretting) daily, providing 59% protein and 14% fat by weight, at the manufacturer’s recommended amount (3% of the total fish weight per tank). The overfed diet (OF) was the same as normal diet but daily feeding amounts were 10 times the amount by weight (<a href="#T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). Fish were weighed every 4 wk and feeding was adjusted to maintain caloric intake with respect to fish weight. Before euthanasia, fish were weighed by addition to a known quantity of aquaculture system water on a scale and weights obtained without anesthesia to minimize anesthetic effects on the kidney metabolome.</p>

<p>The experimental schema is summarized in <a href="#F0001" class="usa-link">Fig. 1<em>A</em></a>. At 8 wk, zebrafish were fasted overnight (24 h from last meal) and euthanized in ice-cold system water. Kidneys were removed immediately after euthanasia by manual dissection and 5–8 kidneys from each of the groups were pooled as one biological replicate, weighed, transferred to empty 2-mL MagNA Lyser Green Beads tubes (Roche), and snap frozen at −80°C until analysis. Each of the dietary groups included five biological replicate samples which were prepared following published procedures with minor modifications (<a href="#B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9">9</a>). Study samples were randomized before sample preparation and data acquisition. Briefly, the frozen kidney samples were mixed with clean beads (∼0.2 g) and ice-cold methanol-water (80:20) with a ratio of 200 µL per 5 mg tissue and homogenized with the Bead Ruptor Elite Bead Mill Homogenizer (OMNI International) at 4.00 m/s for 20 s in one cycle. Aliquots of LC-MS grade water (5 mg each) were used as blank and extracted in an identical manner as study samples. The supernatants were collected from homogenates (and blanks) after centrifugation at 16,000 rcf for 10 min at 4°C. Quality control study pools (QCSP) were prepared by pooling 40 µL of the supernatant from each study sample. The supernatants (120 µL) of each study sample, blanks, and QCSPs were transferred to a new set of tubes and dried under Speed-vac. Each dried residue was reconstituted in a 100 µL water-methanol (95:5) mixture containing 500 ng/mL <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-tryptophan-d5. A 5-µL aliquot of the reconstituted supernatant was used for the metabolomics analysis.</p>

<p>Untargeted metabolomics data were acquired on a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled with a Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (UPLC-HR-MS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were separated via an HSS T3 C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters Corporation) at 50°C with binary mobile phase of water (<em>A</em>) and methanol (<em>B</em>) with 0.1% formic acid (vol/vol) for each. The UHPLC linear gradient started from 2% <em>B</em>, and increased to 100% <em>B</em> in 16 min and then held for 4 min, with a flow rate at 400 µL/min. The untargeted data were acquired from 70 to 1,050 m/z using the data-dependent acquisition mode. Progenesis QI (version 2.1, Waters Corporation) was used for peak picking, alignment, and normalization. Background signals irrelevant to the biological samples were excluded if the mean of intensity across blanks is greater than that across QCSPs based on the unnormalized data. The remaining signals were normalized by Progenesis QI using the “normalize to all” feature.</p></section><section id="sec2-5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Identification and Annotation of Metabolites</h3>
<p>Signal identification/annotation were conducted by Progenesis QI (version 2.1, Waters Corporation) and using ADAP-KDB algorithms for matching against an in-house physical standard library developed with experimental spectra data from more than 2,500 compounds (analyzed under identical conditions to study samples), and against public databases (HDMB, NIST, and METLIN) that include experimental or theoretical data. Each of the identified/annotated metabolites were labeled with an ontology system that provides the evidence of retention time (RT), exact mass (MS), tandem mass fragmentation (MS/MS), and/or isotopic ion pattern (Supplemental Table S1) (<a href="#B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9">9</a>, <a href="#B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10">10</a>).</p></section><section id="sec2-6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Electron Microscopy and Image Analysis</h3>
<p>Transmission electron microscopy was carried out on three additional zebrafish kidneys per group following fixation in EM fixative (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Samples were stained with osmium tetroxide and counterstained with aqueous saturated uranyl acetate. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were obtained using Leica UC6 Ultratome and imaged using a JEM-1400 plus (JEOL) at 120 kV with image acquisition on a Gatan digital camera. We identified all of the cells within the available high magnification (×5,000) images of the tubules (6 sections/group, 13 individual cells in the OF group and 14 individual cells in the ND group). Using artificial intelligence-based image segmentation software trained on previous TEM images of zebrafish kidney tissue (Biodock, <a href="http://www.biodock.ai" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.biodock.ai</a>), 200 mitochondria from each group were identified and their two-dimensional characteristics (area, perimeter, major, and minor axis lengths) were measured, and the area-to-perimeter ratio and eccentricity for each mitochondrion were calculated.</p></section><section id="sec2-7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical, Multivariate, and Pathway Analysis</h3>
<p>GraphPad Prism was used for statistical analyses of zebrafish and kidney weights with the data expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and used to calculate each signal/metabolite’s <em>P</em> value (Welch’s <em>t</em> test) and fold change (FC, based on the mean) for the two phenotypic groups. A two-sided <em>P</em> value &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant.</p>
<p>The normalized metabolomics data were visualized by SIMCA 15.0 (Umerics, Umeå, Sweden) using unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) and supervised orthogonal partial least squares discriminate analysis (OPLS-DA). Metaboanalyst 5.0 was used to conduct clustering analysis via the statistical analysis (one factor) module and pathway analysis by using the functional analysis module (MS Peaks) built on the Mummichog algorithm and gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (<a href="#B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11">11</a>, <a href="#B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12">12</a>). All normalized signals (9,593) that remained after data preprocessing and quality control were submitted to the pathway analysis tool, together with the calculated <em>P</em> value and fold changes (presented as T-score). All signals were searched against the zebrafish pathway library with exact mass (m/z) with the following parameters: positive ion mode, mass tolerance 3 ppm with enforcing primary ions, using a <em>P</em> value cutoff at 0.05 when evaluating the size of permutation group that the algorithm used for selecting significant signals to match all possible metabolites in the pathway. Significance of the perturbed pathways was determined using a combined <em>P</em> value &lt; 0.05 (Mummichog and GSEA). The enriched empirical annotations by Mummichog algorithm were verified by matching the corresponding signals (RT_exact mass) against the in-house physical standard library and public databases (NIST and HDMB).</p></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS</h2>
<section id="sec3-1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Overfeeding Leads to Increased Body and Kidney Weight</h3>
<p>We randomly assigned fish to one of the two dietary groups: overfed (OF) or normal diet (ND), with 36 fish in the OF group and 40 in the ND group. At baseline, there was no significant difference in body weight between groups (mean weight = 207 mg in ND and 195 mg in OF, <em>P</em> = 0.3). After 8 wk, the body weights of individual fish in the OF group were significantly greater than in ND-fed controls (mean weight = 569 mg in OF vs. 261 mg in ND, <em>P</em> = 0.003, <a href="#F0001" class="usa-link">Fig. 1<em>B</em></a>). Kidney weights in the OF group were also greater (mean kidney weight = 1.6 mg in OF vs. 0.7 mg in ND, <a href="#F0001" class="usa-link">Fig. 1<em>C</em></a>).</p></section><section id="sec3-2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Overfeeding Leads to Abnormal Mitochondrial Morphology and Lipid Droplet Accumulation</h3>
<p>We used electron microscopy to evaluate changes in cell morphology. Tubular cells from ND-fed fish were densely packed with elongated mitochondria with well-demarcated cristae with few vesicles (<a href="#F0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2, <em>A</em></a> and <a href="#F0002" class="usa-link"><em>C</em></a>). Tubular cell mitochondria from fish fed the OF diet, on the other hand, were shorter and rounded with loss of cristae (<a href="#F0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2, <em>B</em></a> and <a href="#F0002" class="usa-link"><em>D</em></a>, arrows). This loss of cristae has previously been associated with mitochondrial dysfunction in obesity-related glomerulopathy (<a href="#B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13">13</a>). In addition to mitochondrial abnormalities, tubular cells also exhibited lipid droplet accumulation (<a href="#F0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2, <em>B</em></a> and <a href="#F0002" class="usa-link"><em>D</em></a>, arrowheads), something we had previously observed in the setting of both high-fat and high-calorie feeding in zebrafish (<a href="#B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8">8</a>). Quantification of changes in the mitochondria revealed that mitochondria in the OF group were shorter and rounder than those in the ND group, with decreased eccentricity (<a href="#F0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2<em>E</em></a>) and area-to-perimeter ratio (<a href="#F0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2<em>F</em></a>). Mitochondria were present in podocytes in both conditions, but, consistent with prior work, at a much lower density than in tubular cells (<a href="#B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14">14</a>). Because of these changes in mitochondrial morphology, we hypothesized that overfeeding would also lead to changes in cellular metabolism in the kidney.</p>

<p>Untargeted metabolomics analysis was conducted on fish kidneys with each of the biological replicates including 5–8 kidneys collected from the same diet group. After data preprocessing, normalization, and filtration, we obtained 9,593 signals for the untargeted dataset. Unsupervised and supervised multivariate analyses (<a href="#F0003" class="usa-link">Fig. 3, <em>A</em></a> and <a href="#F0003" class="usa-link"><em>B</em></a>) were used to visualize the differentiation of the metabolic profiles between fish receiving OF and ND diets, and the model statistics from the unsupervised PCA (R2X = 0.524) and the supervised OPLS-DA (R2X = 0.813, Q2 = 0.931) indicated that metabolomic profiles of kidneys from OF fish diverged from those of the ND-fed fish. The volcano plot in <a href="#F0003" class="usa-link">Fig. 3<em>C</em></a> shows metabolites/signals that are significantly differentiated between the OF and ND groups; 661 metabolites/signals were determined as differentiators using the criteria of |FC|&gt;2.0 and <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05. Among the 661 metabolite/signal differentiators, 232 signals were identified or annotated by matching against the in-house library or public database based on exact mass, retention time, and/or tandem MS/MS spectra with the evidence labeled from OL1 to PDd (Supplemental Table S1). Among these, 15 were identified with the highest confidence levels (OL1 and OL2a). Supplemental Fig. S1 shows the top 20 features contributing to the OPLS-DA discrimination by variable importance in projection (VIP); VIP score is a measure of a variable or feature’s importance to the model, with important features having VIP scores greater than one. The top 50 differentiators (by <em>t</em> test significance) are annotated as fatty acids, lipids, and glucose metabolites (<a href="#T2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). The majority of these metabolites (<a href="#B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15">15</a>) were decreased in the OF group versus ND group.</p>

</div></section></section><section id="sec3-4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolites of Glycolysis and Unsaturated Fatty Acid Synthesis Decrease in Overfeeding</h3>
<p>We used the signal-to-pathway module in MetaboAnalyst 5.0 to discover pathways significantly perturbed between OF versus ND groups based on the normalized dataset containing 9,593 signals. We found four pathways associated with glucose metabolism and fatty acid/lipid biosynthesis (e.g., glycolysis or gluconeogenesis, pentose phosphate pathway, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, ubiquinone, and other terpenoid-quinone biosynthesis) were significantly perturbed between OF versus ND with combined <em>P</em> value &lt; 0.05 (<a href="#F0004" class="usa-link">Fig. 4, <em>A</em></a> and <a href="#F0004" class="usa-link"><em>B</em></a>). Other pathways affected by overfeeding (with combined <em>P</em> value &lt; 0.1) were all related to renal handling of sugar and lipid metabolism, as well as vitamin B9 and B6 metabolism (<a href="#F0004" class="usa-link">Fig. 4<em>B</em></a>).</p>

<p>The 15 metabolites able to be identified with the greatest confidence (i.e., by matching retention time and mass to those in the in-house library) are shown in <a href="#T3" class="usa-link">Table 3</a>. <p>Obesity is an important risk factor for CKD, but how the early stages of obesity directly affect kidney function remains unknown. Previous work has focused on the changes in metabolism reflected in biofluids like blood or urine. In this study, we used untargeted metabolomics to directly observe the metabolic changes in zebrafish kidneys in response to overnutrition and weight gain. Untargeted metabolomics was capable of capturing sufficient features (&gt;9,000 signals/peaks) in the kidney tissue with good reproducibility, even with small sample amounts (around 5 mg per biological replicate), uncovering distinctive metabotypes for fish in different dietary groups. This result supports the potential to use untargeted metabolomics and zebrafish models to study the interaction of nutrition and kidney diseases.</p>
<p>This analysis is the first to examine zebrafish kidney tissue metabolomics in the setting of obesity and highlights how the kidney adapts to nutrient overload at a whole organ level. Although previous work has identified differences in metabolic phenotypes between animals on high-calorie or high-fat diets (<a href="#B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16">16</a>), our study reinforces how consistently energy imbalance induces a broad tissue metabolic response within the kidney. Importantly, sample pools within groups clustered tightly together by correlation analysis. In addition, we identified over 200 differentially regulated metabolites within the kidney tissue after 8 wk of overfeeding. Changes in these metabolites were reproducible across the five sample pools in each group, supporting a robust effect across a large number of individual fish.</p>
<p>Results from metabolomics analysis indicated significant decreases in metabolites associated with glucose and lipid metabolism in the OF fish compared with the ND fish. The pathway enrichment analysis revealed reductions in glycolysis, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and ubiquinone (Coenzyme Q10) biosynthesis, all of which are intimately involved in regulation of energy homeostasis in the kidney.</p>
<p>The role of glycolysis in the kidney is both cell- and context-dependent. Prior work in this area has identified glycolysis as both protective against and pathogenic in the development of CKD. Acutely, damaged tubules undergo a “glycolytic” switch, thought to be an adaptive response to inhibition of fatty acid oxidation and a means to maintain energy production in the injured state (<a href="#B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17">17</a>). However, whether glycolytic activity returns to baseline during the transition from acute to chronic injury is less clear. Some investigators have found that, for example, persistent increases in glycolytic activity after kidney injury are indicative of nonregenerative healing and the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition that precedes the development of fibrosis (<a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">18</a>–<a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">20</a>). Experimentally, increasing glycolytic flux through manipulation of the rate-limiting enzyme pyruvate kinase demonstrates benefit in diabetic kidney disease models, an effect seen in both the glomerular and tubular compartments (<a href="#B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21">21</a>, <a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>). On the other hand, multiple investigations in both model animals and humans with CKD have illustrated diminished glycolysis in the setting of chronic kidney disease (including autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease) (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>, <a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">18</a>, <a href="#B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23">23</a>).</p>
<p>In overfed fish, metabolites associated with both glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway decreased. This is most consistent with decreased glycolytic flux in the setting of insulin resistance, given the known systemic pathophysiology of obesity. In this setting, decreased glucose uptake leads to decreased substrate for glycolytic and pentose phosphate pathway activity. In support of this hypothesis is the finding that de novo synthesis of fatty acids was also decreased in the kidneys of overfed fish. As with glycolysis, insulin drives fatty acid biosynthesis, and insulin resistance may reduce both processes simultaneously (<a href="#B24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B24">24</a>). Finding insulin resistance in overfed fish would be consistent with recent findings revealing elevated insulin expression without fasting hyperglycemia in diet-induced obese zebrafish (<a href="#B25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B25">25</a>). The fact that both glycolysis and fatty acid synthesis were decreased is also interesting in light of our observations of the mitochondrial pathology in obese fish.</p>
<p>Diet-induced obesity led to demonstrable changes in proximal tubular mitochondrial morphology consistent with dysfunction. These changes, including decreased eccentricity and decreased area-to-perimeter (“rounding”) of the mitochondria and loss of cristae, have been shown to correlate with diminished mitochondrial function and tubular injury in obesity (<a href="#B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8">8</a>, <a href="#B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13">13</a>, <a href="#B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B26">26</a>). Among the top metabolites identified in our dataset was octadecanoylcarnitine (also known as stearoylcarnitine, C18:0). Octadecanoylcarnitine is a long-chain acylcarnitine and was increased approximately twofold in overfed fish. The long-chain, even-numbered acylcarnitines, arise from excessive, incomplete β-oxidation of fatty acids, an increasingly well-recognized cause of tissue insulin resistance, particularly in skeletal muscle, known as mitochondrial substrate overload (<a href="#B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15">15</a>, <a href="#B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27">27</a>). Recent work has identified mitochondrial overload physiology in the kidneys of mice with mitochondrial transport mutations, which was exacerbated with a high-fat diet (<a href="#B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28">28</a>). Mitochondrial substrate overload secondary to chronic overnutrition may thus be an important contributor to the insulin-resistant phenotype and kidney pathology we observed and a tractable target for therapy.</p>
<p>Although there were no differences between groups in TCA cycle intermediates, the pathway for synthesis of ubiquinone (also known as coenzyme Q10, CoQ10) was different between groups. CoQ10 supports mitochondrial energy production through the electron transport chain and reduces oxidative stress. Levels of CoQ10 in tissues decline with the onset of obesity (<a href="#B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29">29</a>). These data do not allow us to determine whether changes in CoQ10 synthesis are causative in obesity, but other models suggest that supplementation with CoQ10 may help ameliorate obesity-related pathology (<a href="#B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29">29</a>).</p>
<p>In addition to the pathways above, our study identified several individual metabolites of interest; these metabolites are not only highly differentially expressed but have previously been hypothesized to play a role in the development of CKD. First, we found decreased levels of 5-MTP in the kidneys of overfed fish. 5-MTP is a derivative of <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-tryptophan produced in vascular endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and renal epithelial cells. It has previously been identified as being strongly associated with progressive CKD in human subjects (<a href="#B30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30">30</a>). Importantly, 5-MTP may not only be a marker of kidney damage but also functionally important in maintaining kidney health via maintenance of the vascular endothelium, reduction in inflammation, and inhibition of fibrosis (<a href="#B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31">31</a>, <a href="#B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32">32</a>). In a unilateral urinary obstruction (UUO) model mouse, for example, administration of 5-MTP reduced infiltration of inflammatory cells and induction of inflammatory mediators. In both TGF-β-induced HK-2 cells and in mice, administration of 5-MTP reduced indicators of fibrosis (<a href="#B30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30">30</a>). Tryptophan metabolism is consistently abnormal in patients with obesity, with a shift toward the kynurenine pathway and away from the 5-hydroxytryptophan pathway, which results in 5-MTP production (<a href="#B33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B33">33</a>). This shift has also been observed across metabolomic studies in patients with CKD (<a href="#B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34">34</a>). Although 5-MTP supplementation was protective in UUO mice, no study has yet evaluated dietary supplementation in obesity. Our data, however, provide additional evidence that kynurenine production may be increased.</p>
<p>We noted that α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) levels were elevated in the OF group, without detecting elevations in any other TCA cycle intermediates, suggesting an alternative metabolic cause for its accumulation. Although α-ketoglutarate is a rate-determining intermediate in the TCA cycle, it also participates in other metabolic processes, including nitrogen metabolism, and is a critical cosubstrate for 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases, a large family of enzymes critical in hypoxia response and extracellular matrix formation (<a href="#B35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B35">35</a>, <a href="#B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B36">36</a>). Several of these α-KG-dependent enzymes are kynurenine transaminases, which are rate-limiting in the production of kynurenine and kynurenic acid (<a href="#B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B37">37</a>). Acutely, increases in the kynurenine pathway may be protective against ischemic injury both in the heart and the kidney, but chronic elevations may be detrimental to organ function (<a href="#B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38">38</a>, <a href="#B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B39">39</a>). Increased α-KG in kidney tubular cells may be a result of diminished use by damaged mitochondria or result from local hypoxia. This increased α-KG could thus lead to a preference for tryptophan metabolism by the kynurenine pathway, with downstream effects regulated by both increased kynurenine metabolites and decreased 5-MTP. Shifting tryptophan metabolism down alternative pathways is an unexplored treatment strategy for obesity-associated kidney diseases.</p>
<p>Less adenosine was detected in the kidneys of overfed fish. Adenosine plays important roles in purinergic signaling in the kidney, acting as an extracellular messenger and helping to resolve inflammation after cell injury to prevent fibrosis (<a href="#B40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B40">40</a>). Injured cells release ATP, which stimulates inflammatory cells via the NLRP3 inflammasome. Hydrolysis of this ATP to adenosine is an important step in injury resolution and the limitation of chronic inflammation and fibrosis. Mice lacking the gene for CD73, which converts AMP to adenosine, spontaneously develop renal fibrosis (<a href="#B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B41">41</a>). Although the cause of the diminished adenosine levels in obese fish remains unclear, others have shown that activation of adenosine signaling in high-fat-fed rodents ameliorates weight gain and downstream organ dysfunction, suggesting potential therapeutic benefit (<a href="#B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42">42</a>).</p>
<p>The evaluation performed here has important limitations demanding further investigation. First, the kidney is a highly heterogeneous organ with cell-type-specific responses to metabolic stress differing across the nephron. In addition, although zebrafish nephrons are structurally similar to those of mammals, the zebrafish kidney also contains hematopoietic cells. Using whole kidneys does not account for these differences between cells, and further evaluation of metabolic changes, both in cell culture and using more advanced spatial technologies (including multiplexed in situ hybridization and spatial metabolomics), will provide deeper insights into the cell biology of diet-induced obesity in the kidney. Second, we did not examine changes over time. Early metabolic changes may be protective in the short term while leading to chronic injury in the longer term, and future experiments over longer time frames and with multiple time points can address the question of the persistence of the changes we observed. Whether zebrafish respond to overfeeding similarly to mammals, and in particular humans, is under active investigation. However, the metabolic pathways at play in energy balance are deeply conserved, and metabolites should be comparable across species. Existing human cohorts with large metabolomics datasets include the Kidney Precision Medicine Project and the Cure Glomerulopathy network, which can be used to validate the findings from our model. Finally, kidney metabolic responses to diet-induced obesity may differ between sexes. Population-based cohort studies suggest sex modifies the effect of obesity on both the risk of developing kidney disease and on the presence of albuminuria (<a href="#B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B43">43</a>, <a href="#B44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B44">44</a>). Previous work in zebrafish has identified sex-specific responses to high-fat diets, with female and male fish developing increased body fat mass in response to different dietary stimuli (e.g., total dietary fat vs. specific fatty acid ratios) (<a href="#B45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B45">45</a>). Our study controlled for sex differences by assigning fish to each dietary group in a 1:1 ratio of male to female, but due to limitations in sample number from a single clutch (to control for age, size, genetic background, and environmental history), we did not analyze male and female fish separately. Future work specifically designed to interrogate sex differences in the response to overfeeding will be needed to understand the differential association of obesity with kidney disease observed in patients. Despite these limitations, our metabolomics analysis represents a robust, whole organ evaluation of kidney metabolism in overnutrition and suggests multiple new pathways worthy of investigation.</p>
<section id="sec4-1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Perspectives and Significance</h3>
<p>Obesity is a common and increasingly prevalent risk factor for the development and progression of kidney dysfunction, but how overnutrition affects kidney metabolism is not known. We performed an untargeted metabolomic analysis of a zebrafish model of diet-induced obesity, which revealed changes in key metabolites in the zebrafish kidney during overfeeding. Pathway analysis and characterization of specific metabolites implicate perturbations in glucose and tryptophan metabolism in the kidneys of overfed fish. Understanding how these pathways can be altered by diet and pharmacotherapy, is the first step toward providing novel therapies for patients with obesity to reduce their risk of kidney disease.</p></section></section><section id="sec5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">DATA AVAILABILITY</h2>
