</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h3>
<p id="Par1">Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) exhibits a significant comorbidity with periodontitis (PD), yet its molecular mechanisms remain unclear. This study aims to elucidate the metabolic characteristics of MASLD Patients with Periodontitis (MASLD-PD) in a comorbid state through metabolomic analysis, thereby exploring potential biological mechanisms.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objectives</h3>
<p id="Par2">To elucidate metabolic characteristics of MASLD-PD patients via metabolomics, explore comorbidity mechanisms, and provide insights for early diagnosis and targeted intervention.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p id="Par3">Thirty subjects were recruited (15 each in the MASLD-PD group and PD group). Subgingival plaque samples were collected and subjected to non-targeted metabolomic analysis via ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). Differential metabolites were identified through multidimensional statistical analysis (PCA, PLS-DA, OPLS-DA), LASSO regression was further applied to screen core diagnostic features from the top 20 upregulated metabolites, with model performance evaluated using Kappa coefficient, F1 score, Precision, Recall, and Accuracy. followed by ROC curve analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment studies.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p id="Par4">A total of 2126 significantly differentially expressed metabolites were identified. Compared with the PD group, 49 metabolites were significantly upregulated, and 2077 metabolites were significantly downregulated in the MASLD-PD group. Multidimensional analysis revealed significant separation of the metabolomic profiles between the two groups. ROC analysis was performed as an exploratory approach to evaluate the discriminatory capacity of the 20 significantly upregulated metabolites between the MASLD-PD and PD groups. The combined model yielded an AUC of 1.0000, providing preliminary evidence that these metabolites may collectively distinguish the two groups in this small cohort and generating hypotheses for further validation. LASSO regression identified 5 core metabolites with non-zero regression coefficients, and the core feature combination achieved perfect discriminatory efficacy (AUC = 1.0000) with high consistency (Kappa = 0.8000), overall accuracy (0.9000), and no missed diagnoses (Recall = 1.0000). Building upon this, we further explored the functions of these top 20 upregulated metabolites through KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. This revealed their specific enrichment in pathways related to lipid metabolism (e.g., Steroid hormone biosynthesis), Signal transduction (e.g., ErbB signaling pathway), and the immune system (e.g., Th17 cell differentiation).</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p id="Par5">This study systematically reveals the unique metabolic phenotype of patients with MASLD-PD, suggesting that immune-metabolic network dysregulation may be involved in the pathophysiological mechanism of this comorbidity. The 5 core metabolites identified via LASSO regression exhibit promising discriminatory potential. The identified differentially expressed metabolites and their enriched pathways generate hypotheses for understanding the comorbidity mechanism, laying a foundation for future studies on early diagnosis and targeted intervention after large-scale validation.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h3>
<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11306-026-02400-0.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), Periodontitis (PD), Metabolomics, Subgingival plaque, Biomarkers, KEGG pathway</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par6">Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) ranks among the most prevalent chronic liver diseases globally, with its incidence rising rapidly alongside the prevalence of obesity and metabolic syndrome (Fernandez et al., <a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">2023</a>; McGinty &amp; Przemioslo, <a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">2024</a>; Sono et al., <a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">2025</a>). Previously termed metabolic associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), the terminology has been updated to MASLD in a recent international consensus statement to more accurately reflect the core pathophysiology of metabolic dysfunction-driven hepatic steatosis (Rinella et al., <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">2023</a>). MASLD is defined as hepatic steatosis (confirmed by imaging or histology) accompanied by evidence of metabolic dysfunction (e.g., abdominal obesity, dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia, hypertension, or metabolic syndrome) (Rinella et al., <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">2023</a>). It is not only closely associated with intrahepatic inflammation and fibrosis, but also exhibits pathophysiological links with multiple extrahepatic diseases (Boccatonda et al., <a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">2023</a>; Dayal et al., <a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">2025</a>; Virk et al., <a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">2023</a>). Periodontitis (PD), a common chronic inflammatory oral disease, has been found in recent years to exhibit a high comorbidity rate among patients with MASLD (Juzbašić et al., <a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">2025</a>; Vegda et al., <a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">2024</a>).</p>
<p id="Par7">PD is an immune-mediated inflammatory disease caused by dental plaque biofilm, characterised by the destruction of periodontal supporting tissues and alveolar bone resorption (Abdulkareem et al., <a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">2023</a>; Könönen et al., <a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">2019</a>). Extensive research indicates that PD may contribute to or exacerbate the progression of various metabolic and inflammatory diseases through mechanisms such as microbial translocation, systemic inflammatory responses, and immune dysregulation (Martínez-García &amp; Hernández-Lemus, <a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">2021</a>; Pirih et al., <a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">2021</a>; Tattar et al., <a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">2025</a>). It is particularly noteworthy that PD and MASLD exhibit a bidirectional relationship: PD can exacerbate hepatic steatosis and fibrosis by promoting systemic inflammation and insulin resistance; conversely, the metabolic dysregulation and immune abnormalities associated with MASLD may also impact the health of periodontal tissues (Juzbašić et al., <a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">2025</a>). A recent prospective cohort study encompassing 478,000 participants (Ye et al., <a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">2025</a>) further confirmed that poor oral health, particularly symptoms associated with PD is significantly linked to an elevated risk of developing MASLD. This association is mediated through pathways involving lipid metabolism and lipoprotein regulation, providing population level evidence for the “oral liver cross organ interaction”. Metabolic Hepatic cross organ interaction at the population level. This finding complements the metabolic profile analysis of MASLD-PD patients examined in the present study. Moreover, a bidirectional causal study based on Mendelian randomisation (Ye et al., <a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">2023</a>) further confirmed at the genetic level that a bidirectional causal relationship exists between gut microbiota and PD, PD-associated pathogens can colonise the gut via salivary ingestion, reshaping gut microbiota composition (e.g., enrichment of <em>Enterobacteriales</em>), whilst gut dysbiosis amplifies systemic inflammation through the “oral-gut-liver axis”, exacerbating hepatic metabolic dysfunction. This discovery provides crucial microbial-mediated mechanistic support for “oral-liver cross-organ interactions”, complementing the metabolic dysregulation highlighted in this study.</p>
<p id="Par8">Although MASLD and PD exhibit a significant clinical and epidemiological association, the molecular mechanisms underlying MASLD-PD remain incompletely elucidated (Martínez-García &amp; Hernández-Lemus, <a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">2024</a>). Previous studies have predominantly focused on individual aspects such as inflammatory pathways, bacterial translocation, or insulin resistance, lacking research that elucidates their co-morbid mechanisms from a systemic perspective (Juzbašić et al., <a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">2025</a>). Metabolomics, as a systems biology tool, allows for comprehensive detection of alterations in small-molecule metabolites within biological organisms. It may reflect the state of metabolic network regulation during pathological conditions, thereby providing a unique perspective for exploring the potential mechanisms underlying complex diseases (Clish, <a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">2016</a>). By comparing the metabolic phenotype differences between MASLD-PD and PD alone, we can not only identify co-disease-specific metabolic reprogramming patterns but also uncover key metabolic nodes and pathway networks driving disease progression. This provides a molecular basis for understanding the interactions between MASLD and PD.</p>
<p id="Par9">Based on this, the present study employed ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) to conduct non-targeted metabolomic analysis of subgingival plaque samples from patients with MASLD-PD and those with PD alone. Subgingival plaque was selected as the subject of study as it directly reflects metabolic activity within the local periodontal microenvironment, whilst potentially capturing manifestations of systemic metabolic disorders within the oral cavity (Basic &amp; Dahlén, <a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">2023</a>; Chen et al., <a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">2024</a>; Moradi et al., <a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">2025</a>). This study aims to compare metabolic profile differences between two sample groups, identify intergroup separation trends through multidimensional statistical analysis; screen and identify significantly differentially expressed metabolites to evaluate their potential diagnostic value as candidate biomarkers; and perform KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on differential metabolites to explore potential biological processes and related regulatory pathways specific to MASLD-PD. Ultimately, this study aims to provide novel metabolic-level evidence for the co-morbid mechanisms between MASLD and PD, thereby offering theoretical underpinnings and data support for the future development of early diagnostic strategies and targeted interventions for this co-morbidity.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Participants in the study</h3>
<p id="Par10">The study included 30 participants aged 30–60 years, comprising 15 in the MASLD-PD group and 15 in the PD group. MASLD-PD patients were required to meet diagnostic criteria for both PD and MASLD. According to the 2023 Multispecialty Delphi Consensus, MASLD diagnosis requires the presence of hepatic steatosis (confirmed by imaging or biopsy) alongside at least one cardiac metabolic risk factor (abnormal BMI/waist circumference, abnormal blood glucose or type 2 diabetes, elevated blood pressure, elevated triglycerides, or reduced high-density lipoprotein) (Rinella et al., <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">2023</a>). The 2018 Periodontitis Case Definition Criteria are used to diagnose periodontitis: Periodontitis can be identified during a clinical examination if one of the two following criteria is satisfied: Clinical attachment loss (CAL) is present on the interproximal surface of at least two non-adjacent teeth, or at least two teeth have CAL ≥ 3 mm on the buccal or lingual side, along with periodontal pockets ≥ 3 mm (Papapanou et al., <a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">2018</a>; Tonetti et al., <a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">2018</a>). Exclusion criteria include malignant tumors, pregnancy or lactation, history of periodontal therapy, use of immunosuppressants, antibiotics, or drugs related to metabolism during the previous six months, and other liver problems (drug-, alcohol-, or viral). The PD group’s diagnostic criteria are identical to those previously mentioned, with the following exclusions: pregnancy or lactation, a history of periodontal therapy, the use of antibiotics or anti-inflammatory drugs within the last six months, and other systemic disorders. No statistically significant differences were observed in baseline clinical characteristics such as age, CAL, and probing pocket depth (PPD) between the two groups (all <em>p</em> &gt; 0.05), and these data are detailed in Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/Tab1/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Collection of subgingival plaque samples</h3>
<p id="Par12">Through oral inspection, two problematic periodontal pockets that met the periodontitis categorization criteria (periodontal pocket depth ≥ 4 mm) were first chosen as sampling sites from different quadrants. The sampling locations were completely cleaned of supragingival plaque using professional instruments, and the gingival border was dried with sterile cotton balls to avoid blood or saliva contamination. A single sample of deep biofilm is carefully scraped from the bottom of the periodontal pocket along the inner wall of the pocket by skilled medical personnel using a sterile Gracey curette. To get a second sample, the same process is carried out in a different quadrant that satisfies the requirements. After that, the plaque samples from the two collection regions were mixed together and put in a centrifuge tube with 750 µL of lysis buffer called PowerLyzer™ PowerSoil<sup>®</sup> Bead Solution (Qiagen, Germany). After that, the tube was moved to a freezer and kept there at −80 °C. The technique is carried out with strict adherence to aseptic operating norms. To guarantee the accuracy of results from microbiological analyses and the uniformity of sample collection, all staff members have received standardized training. This efficiently avoids contamination from non-target regions, which can otherwise affect the outcomes of experiments (Jensen et al., <a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">2018</a>).</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample processing</h3>
<p id="Par13">Transfer all samples to 2 mL centrifuge tubes, add one 6 mm diameter grinding bead, then add 800 µL of extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1, v/v) containing four internal standards (e.g., L-2-chloro-phenylalanine (0.02 mg/mL), etc.) in extraction solvent (methanol: water = 4:1, v/v). Grind for 6 min at −10 °C and 50 Hz using a cryogenic tissue grinder, followed by cold ultrasonic extraction at 5 °C and 40 kHz for 30 min. Afterwards, the sample was left to stand at −20 °C for 30 min, then centrifuged at 13,000 g and 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to a sample vial with an insert tube for instrumental analysis. Additionally, 20 µL of supernatant was transferred from each sample, mixed, and used as a quality control sample.</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">QC sample</h3>
<p id="Par14">Equal volumes of all sample metabolites were pooled to prepare quality control (QC) samples. During instrumental analysis, one QC sample was inserted every 5–10 samples to assess the repeatability of the entire analytical process.</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LC-MS/MS detection</h3>
<p id="Par15">The instrument platform employed for this liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis was Thermo Fisher Scientific’s Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Fourier Transform Ion Spectrometry UHPLC-Orbitrap Exploris 240 system (Shanghai Meiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). 3 µL of the sample was separated on an HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm) before entering mass spectrometry detection. Mobile phase A comprised a water/acetonitrile (95/5, v/v) solution containing 0.1% formic acid, whilst mobile phase B was an acetonitrile/isopropanol/water (47.5/47.5/5, v/v/v) solution containing 0.1% formic acid. Flow rate was maintained at 0.40 mL/min with column temperature set to 40 °C. Mass spectrometry signal acquisition employed positive and negative ion scanning modes, with a mass range of m/z 70–1050. Ion spray voltage settings were 3500 V for positive ions and − 3000 V for negative ions. Sheath gas was 50 argon (Ar), auxiliary heating gas 13 Ar, and ion source heating temperature 450 °C. Collision energy cycling was set at 20–40–60 V.</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data analysis</h3>
<p id="Par16">Following instrument operation, the LC-MS raw data were imported into the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment. Yielding a final data matrix comprising retention times, mass-to-charge ratios, and peak intensities. Concurrently, MS and MS/MS mass spectrometry data were matched against public metabolic databases HMDB (<a href="http://www.hmdb.ca/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.hmdb.ca/</a>) and Metlin (<a href="https://metlin.scripps.edu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://metlin.scripps.edu/</a>), alongside MajorBio’s proprietary library, to derive metabolite identifications.</p>
<p id="Par17">The searched data matrix was uploaded to the MajorBio Cloud platform (cloud.majorbio.com) for analysis. Preprocessing of the data matrix was performed as follows: Missing values were removed using the 80% rule, retaining variables with non-zero values in at least 80% of samples. Missing values were then imputed (using the minimum value in the original matrix). To minimise errors from sample preparation and instrument instability, the response intensities of sample mass spectrometry peaks were normalised using sum-normalisation, yielding a normalised data matrix. Simultaneously, variables with QC sample relative standard deviations (RSD) exceeding 30% were excluded. Logarithmic transformation (log10) was applied to the data, yielding the final matrix for subsequent analysis. Subsequently, the ropls package (Version 1.6.2) in R was employed to perform three complementary multidimensional statistical models: (1) Unsupervised Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was first used to verify the natural separation trend of metabolic profiles between groups without prior grouping information, ensuring that group differences were not artificially induced; (2) Supervised Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was applied to enhance the extraction of differential information related to grouping, maximizing the distinction between the MASLD-PD and PD groups; (3) Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) was further used to separate the predictive variables (group-related variation) from orthogonal variables (non-group-related variation), reducing the interference of irrelevant noise on differential metabolite screening. Model stability and reliability were assessed using seven-fold cross-validation (excluding one sample in each fold for validation, repeating seven times) to avoid overfitting. Significantly different metabolites were strictly screened based on three criteria: VIP_pred_OPLS-DA &gt; 1 (reflecting the contribution of metabolites to group separation), FDR-adjusted <em>p</em>-value &lt; 0.05 (controlling for false discovery rate in multiple comparisons), and fold change (FC) ≥ 1 (upregulated) or ≤ 1 (downregulated) (reflecting the magnitude of expression differences). To further refine diagnostically valuable biomarkers, mitigate multicollinearity interference, and enhance model practicality, LASSO regression analysis was conducted on the 20 significantly upregulated differential metabolites using the glmnet package in R (<a href="https://glmnet.stanford.edu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://glmnet.stanford.edu/</a>). Five-fold cross-validation was applied to determine the optimal regularization parameters (lambda.min and lambda.1se), which balance model performance and parsimony. Metabolites with non-zero regression coefficients after regularization were defined as core diagnostic features. For the core feature panel, key model evaluation metrics including Kappa coefficient, F1 score, Precision, Recall, and Accuracy were calculated to assess diagnostic consistency, comprehensiveness, and correctness. Additionally, ROC curve analysis was performed on the core feature combination to verify its discriminatory efficacy between MASLD-PD and PD groups, with the area under the curve (AUC) and 95% confidence interval (CI) computed to quantify the diagnostic accuracy. Differential metabolites underwent metabolic pathway annotation via the KEGG database (<a href="https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html</a>), which is the most comprehensive public database for integrating genomic, metabolomic, and pathway information. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the scipy.stats package in Python with the hypergeometric test, a standard method for enrichment analysis that calculates the probability of differential metabolites being enriched in a specific pathway by chance. This method is particularly suitable for metabolomic data because it accounts for the background distribution of metabolites in the KEGG database, avoiding false positive enrichment results caused by random overlap.</p></section></section><section id="Sec9"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Result</h2>
<section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Multidimensional statistical analysis of metabolomic differences</h3>
<p id="Par18">Multidimensional statistical analysis was performed on their LC-MS metabolomics data to clarify the metabolic phenotype differences between the MASLD-PD group and the PD group. PCA revealed that PC1 and PC2 explained 39.80% and 6.35% of the variance, respectively, with a cumulative contribution rate of 46.15%. As shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>a (PCA score plot), the two groups clustered distinctly, exhibiting non-overlapping 95% confidence ellipses. Further analysis via PLS-DA revealed Component1 and Component2 explaining 43.40% and 4.36% of variance, respectively. Figure <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>b (PLS-DA score plot) shows that the two sample groups exhibited significant clustering and separation, with completely non-overlapping 95% confidence ellipses. Model parameter details in Table <a href="#Tab2" class="usa-link">2</a> (PLS-DA Model Parameter) show that the cumulative explanatory power of the model for the X matrix (R²X(cum)) reached 0.518, the cumulative explanatory power for the Y matrix (R²Y(cum)) was 0.997, and the cumulative predictive capability (Q²(cum)) was 0.941, all close to 1, indicating the model has good stability and reliability. Moreover, the PLS-DA permutation test results in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>d confirm that R<sup>2</sup>Y = 0.997 and Q<sup>2</sup> = 0.941 (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05, 0/200), which further verifies that the model has no overfitting issues and possesses stable discriminatory performance. In OPLS-DA, Component1 accounted for 40.10% variance, while orthogonal Component1 explained 17.67% variance. As presented in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>c (OPLS-DA score plot), the two sample groups exhibited clear clustering and separation, with no overlap in the 95% confidence ellipses. As shown in Table <a href="#Tab3" class="usa-link">3</a> (OPLS-DA Model Parameter), the model’s cumulative explanatory power for the X matrix (R²X(cum)) was 0.477, the cumulative explanatory power for the Y matrix (R²Y(cum)) was 0.98, and the cumulative predictive capability (Q²(cum)) was 0.906, meeting the criteria for a reliable model (Q² &gt; 0.5). The OPLS-DA permutation test in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>e further confirms the model’s reliability: R<sup>2</sup>Y = 0.98, Q<sup>2</sup> = 0.906 (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05, 0/200), ruling out overfitting and validating the stability of the group separation trend. These findings, supported by the score plots in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>a–c, the permutation test results in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>d, e, and the model parameters in Tables <a href="#Tab2" class="usa-link">2</a> and <a href="#Tab3" class="usa-link">3</a>, indicate significant metabolomic differences between the MASLD-PD and PD groups. The PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA models all demonstrate effective ability to distinguish the metabolic profiles of the two sample groups, laying a solid foundation for subsequent differential metabolite screening and pathway analysis.</p>

<p id="Par27">To further refine diagnostically valuable biomarkers, mitigate multicollinearity interference, and enhance model practicality, this study employed LASSO regression analysis on 20 significantly upregulated differential metabolites to screen core diagnostic features. Figure <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a> systematically presents the feature selection process and diagnostic efficacy validation results. The five-fold cross-validation curve in Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>a determines the optimal regularisation parameter through dual criteria of lambda.min and lambda.1se, ensuring model stability and parsimony; Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>b’s feature regression coefficient trajectory diagram demonstrates that as regularisation strength [log(λ)] increases, most metabolite coefficients are compressed to zero. Ultimately, only five metabolites retain non-zero coefficients, becoming core features distinguishing MASLD-PD from PD alone; Table <a href="#Tab5" class="usa-link">5</a> lists these five core metabolites and their regression coefficients, where 11-methyl-tetracosanoic acid contributes the highest weight (coefficient 2.1871), while 4-methyl-1 H-pyrrole-3-carboxamide (0.9069) and 5 diazo-uracil (0.8755) provide crucial diagnostic information for the model, whilst γ-glutamyl-β-cyanoalanine (0.2396) serves as an indispensable auxiliary feature. The only core metabolite with a negative coefficient is bupivacaine (−1.7109), which optimises discrimination accuracy through inverse weighting adjustment; The five key evaluation metrics in Table <a href="#Tab6" class="usa-link">6</a> (Kappa = 0.8, F1 = 0.9091, Precision = 0.8333, Recall = 1.0, Accuracy = 0.9) confirm the model’s outstanding performance with high consistency, zero missed diagnoses, and low misdiagnosis rates; ROC validation results in Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>c demonstrate that the core metabolite combination achieves an AUC of 1.0000, exhibiting perfect discriminatory efficacy for both disease categories. This identifies a preliminary core metabolite panel through exploratory LASSO regression, generating a testable hypothesis that these metabolites may serve as candidate discriminators between MASLD-PD and PD pending large-scale validation.</p>

<p id="Par33">This study utilised LC-MS metabolomics technology combined with multidimensional statistical analysis to systematically elucidate metabolic phenotype differences between patients with MASLD-PD and those with PD alone. It identified discriminative differential metabolites and deciphered their potential biological pathways, aiming to provide new perspectives for in-depth research into the co-morbidity mechanisms of MASLD-PD.</p>
<p id="Par34">Three multidimensional statistical models (PCA, PLS-DA, OPLS-DA) consistently showed distinct clustering and separation of metabolic profiles between the MASLD-PD and PD groups (no overlap in 95% confidence ellipses), confirming that MASLD coexistence induces stable, distinctive metabolic alterations in PD patients. The OPLS-DA model revealed that orthogonal Component 1 (explaining 17.67% variance) accounted for a larger proportion of group differences than PLS-DA Component 2 (4.36%), indicating that MASLD-associated metabolic perturbations are orthogonal to PD’s inherent metabolic characteristics. This metabolomic evidence supports that MASLD may drive a unique metabolic phenotype in co-morbid patients via pathways distinct from PD’s local pathological processes—consistent with the recognition that MASLD reflects systemic metabolic dysfunction (Chao et al., <a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">2024</a>). The consistent separation across unsupervised (PCA) and supervised (PLS-DA/OPLS-DA) models validates the robustness of metabolic differences between groups, laying a foundation for differential metabolite screening and pathway analysis.</p>
<p id="Par35">Based on the stringent screening criteria of “FDR-adjusted <em>p</em>-value &lt; 0.05, VIP_pred_OPLS-DA &gt; 1, and FC ≥ 1 (upregulated) or FC ≤ 1 (downregulated), this study identified 2,126 significantly differentially expressed metabolites. Compared to the PD group, 49 metabolites were significantly upregulated and 2,077 were significantly downregulated in the MASLD-PD group. Furthermore, the expression trends of the top 40 significantly differentially expressed metabolites further highlighted the directional differences in metabolic regulation between the two groups. These metabolites may serve as potential molecular markers of phenotypic divergence between the two groups and could be involved in the potential pathophysiological processes associated with the co-morbidity of MASLD and PD. Among significantly differential metabolites, 11-Methyltetracosanoylcarnitine (FDR-corrected <em>p</em>-value = 0.0001526, VIP_pred_OPLS-DA = 1.4389, FC = 1.2381) was significantly upregulated in the MASLD-PD group. As an acylcarnitine, this metabolite is functionally associated with fatty acid transport and β-oxidation pathways, both central to lipid metabolism. Its upregulation in MASLD-PD, coupled with KEGG enrichment analysis indicating activation of the steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway (a lipid metabolism-related pathway), suggests that dysregulation of fatty acid transport may represent a key metabolic hallmark of this comorbidity. This finding aligns with metabolomic evidence, where acylcarnitine accumulation may reflect alterations in lipid metabolic flux within MASLD (Zheng et al., <a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">2023</a>); Uric acid was significantly upregulated in the MASLD-PD group (FDR-corrected <em>p</em>-value = 0.0003948, VIP_pred_OPLS-DA = 1.3225, FC = 1.1621). At the metabolomic level, uric acids as purine metabolites correlate with metabolic dysfunction. Their elevation in MASLD-PD aligns with prior findings, where uric acid accumulation positively correlates with MASLD-associated metabolic disorders (Brennan et al., <a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">2020</a>; Li et al., <a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">2025</a>). Furthermore, uric acid’s association with metabolic and inflammatory pathways (its role in immune and metabolic pathways confirmed via KEGG enrichment analysis in this study) suggests it may serve as a metabolic link between MASLD and PD. However, metabolomics data alone cannot establish a direct causal relationship between uric acid, MASLD, and PD. Conversely, compared to the MASLD-PD group, the PD group exhibited significantly elevated levels of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) (FDR-corrected <em>p</em>-value = 0.0000406, VIP = 1.472, FC = 0.8481) and glutathione thiols (FDR-corrected <em>p</em>-value = 0.0003948, VIP = 1.3225, FC &lt; 1). As a metabolite associated with inflammatory responses, the isolated upregulation of PGE2 in the PD group aligns with its established role in periodontal inflammation (Båge et al., <a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2011</a>). The antioxidant metabolite glutathione thiol exhibited reduced levels in MASLD-PD, consistent with metabolomic evidence that decreased glutathione levels reflect oxidative stress-related metabolic dysregulation in MASLD (Martínez-Gili et al., <a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">2025</a>). These differential expressions indicate significant differences in the regulatory mechanisms of inflammatory and antioxidant metabolic pathways between MASLD-PD and PD alone.</p>
<p id="Par36">In this study, compared with the PD group, individual differentially upregulated metabolites in the MASLD-PD group (such as 2-Hydroxynevirapine, Naloxonazine, Gamma-Glutamyl-beta-cyanoalanine, etc.) showed preliminary discriminatory potential, with AUC values approaching or reaching 1.0000. This suggests specific metabolic reprogramming in MASLD-PD, potentially involving abnormalities in lipid, peptide, and other metabolic pathways, thereby providing metabolic-level clues for elucidating the molecular mechanisms of MASLD-PD comorbidity. More notably, the exploratory combined analysis of 20 differentially expressed metabolites achieved an AUC of 1.0000 in this small cohort, outperforming individual metabolites. This generates the hypothesis that disease progression may involve synergistic dysregulation of multiple metabolic pathways, such that a multi-metabolite panel could better capture complex metabolic disturbances than single biomarkers (Hu et al., <a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">2025</a>; Wang et al., <a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">2024</a>). However, this finding is preliminary and limited to the study cohort, requiring further validation to assess whether such a panel could enhance discriminatory performance in independent populations. This exploratory finding offers a hypothetical multi-metabolite panel that merits further investigation in large-scale, independent cohorts. The results generate testable hypotheses regarding the potential utility of metabolite combinations for distinguishing MASLD-PD from PD, but no clinical application is inferred at this stage.</p>
<p id="Par37">LASSO regression effectively eliminates redundant information through regularised screening, identifying five core diagnostic features from 20 differentially expressed metabolites. This approach circumvents multicollinearity interference while simplifying model structure, thereby enhancing clinical utility (Mao et al., <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">2025</a>). Among these, 11-methyl-tetracosanoic acid was identified as the key biomarker with the highest regression coefficient (2.1871). Its close association with lipid metabolism further corroborates the central role of lipid metabolism disorders in MASLD-PD comorbidity (Luo et al., <a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">2025</a>). This preliminary core panel exhibited exceptional discriminatory performance in the study cohort (Kappa = 0.8000, accuracy = 0.9000, AUC = 1.0000), supporting the hypothesis that these metabolites may contribute to precise distinction between MASLD-PD and PD. However, these results are exploratory and limited to the enrolled samples; they do not resolve clinical co-morbidity differentiation challenges nor imply clinical translatability. Instead, this screening strategy provides a framework for generating hypotheses regarding metabolomic biomarker development, with subsequent studies required to validate the panel in larger, diverse populations before any consideration of diagnostic tool development.</p>
<p id="Par38">KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that 49 upregulated metabolites in the MASLD-PD group were significantly enriched in three pathway categories: lipid metabolism (e.g., Steroid hormone biosynthesis), immune system (e.g., Th17 cell differentiation, T cell receptor signaling pathway), and signal transduction (e.g., ErbB signalling pathway, NF-κB signalling pathway). These metabolomic data indicate that MASLD-PD is characterised by coordinated dysregulation of lipid metabolic and immune pathways—consistent with Ye et al.’s (<a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">2025</a>) observation that lipid metabolism pathways mediate oral-health-related MASLD risk (Mousa et al. <a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">2025</a>). Notably, subgingival plaque metabolomics revealed that local metabolic dysregulation (e.g., upregulation of Cysteinyl-Gamma-glutamate, Leucyl-Glycine) may reflect systemic metabolic imbalance, providing metabolomic support for the “oral-gut-liver axis” theory (Ye et al., <a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">2025</a>). Lipid metabolism pathways (e.g., Steroid hormone biosynthesis) are central to the observed metabolic differences, as steroid hormone metabolism is a key component of systemic metabolic dysfunction in MASLD (Luo et al., <a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">2025</a>). Immune pathway enrichment (e.g., Th17 cell differentiation) further suggests that inflammatory metabolic reprogramming contributes to MASLD-PD comorbidity—aligning with metabolomic evidence that immune and lipid metabolic pathways are interconnected in systemic metabolic disorders (Torre et al., <a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">2021</a>). Core metabolites identified in this study (e.g., 11-Methyltetracosanoylcarnitine, cysteinyl-γ-glutamic acid) may serve as metabolic nodes linking these enriched pathways, though direct links to gut microbiota or “bidirectional regulation” (Ye et al., <a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">2023</a>) cannot be confirmed by metabolomic data alone and require further validation.</p>
<section id="Sec16"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Limitations</h3>
<p id="Par39">Whilst this study systematically elucidates the metabolic differences and potential mechanisms between MASLD-PD and PD alone, certain limitations remain: Firstly, the sample size was relatively small (15 cases per group), and this study did not establish internal or external validation cohorts. Although multidimensional statistical analyses (PCA, PLS-DA, OPLS-DA) demonstrated stable separation of metabolic phenotypes between the two groups, the small sample size may introduce bias during the screening of differential metabolites. More critically, the absence of internal validation (e.g., random division of the cohort into training and validation sets) and external validation (e.g., independent cohorts from different centres or populations) precludes verification of the reproducibility, robustness, and clinical applicability of the identified differential metabolites and core biomarker combinations. This limitation substantially constrains the reliability, generalisability, and clinical translation potential of the findings. Future studies should expand sample sizes (e.g., ≥ 50 cases per group), first establishing an internal validation framework by randomly allocating cohorts into training (60–70%) and validation (30–40%) sets to preliminarily assess biomarker discriminatory power. This should be followed by multicentre external validation involving populations with diverse geographical backgrounds, age stratification, and clinical characteristics. This multi-step validation strategy will help confirm the robustness of the core metabolite signature and enhance the credibility and generalisability of the findings. Secondly, the cross-sectional design only captures metabolic differences between groups at a specific time point, failing to establish causal relationships between altered metabolites and disease progression. Longitudinal cohort studies tracking metabolic dynamics and disease outcomes over time are required to clarify causal associations. Thirdly, Mechanistic exploration is currently limited to inferences based solely on metabolomic data, lacking validation through cellular experiments or animal studies (e.g., establishing a mouse model of high-fat diet-induced MASLD-PD pathogen infection to validate the regulatory role of Steroid hormone biosynthesis metabolic pathways in co-morbidity). Consequently, the conclusions regarding relevant mechanisms require further functional studies to provide substantiation. Fourthly, although the age distribution was balanced between the two groups (<em>p</em> = 0.90), the broad age range (30–60 years) may introduce age-related biological variation, and the lack of age as a covariate in multidimensional models might have failed to fully exclude the potential interference of age-associated metabolic decline on disease-specific metabolic signals. Fifth, this study did not stratify MASLD-PD patients by specific metabolic risk factors (such as type 2 diabetes, obesity alone, etc.). Given that different metabolic risk factors exert fundamentally distinct pathological effects on the “oral-systemic axis”, this omission may introduce confounding bias in the metabolic profiling analysis. Consequently, the study failed to precisely elucidate the specific associations between particular metabolic abnormalities and MASLD-PD co-morbidity. Future research will expand the sample size and employ subgroup designs based on specific metabolic risk factors to further clarify the specific metabolic associations between different subtypes of metabolic abnormalities and periodontal-liver co-morbidity.</p></section></section><section id="Sec17"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="Par40">This study employs metabolomics to systematically explore the metabolic phenotype differences between MASLD-PD and PD alone. It suggests the potential existence of distinguishable metabolic disorder patterns between the two groups, identifies a set of core differential metabolites with preliminary discriminatory potential, and suggests that imbalances in pathways such as immune regulation and lipid metabolism may be involved in the development of co-morbidity. The findings not only offer a novel metabolic perspective on the pathogenesis of MASLD-PD but also generate hypotheses regarding potential molecular targets and candidate biomarkers. These exploratory observations require further validation in large-scale, multi-center studies before any consideration of their utility for clinical diagnosis, treatment, or prognostic assessment. Future work should involve validation through expanded sample cohorts, functional experimental confirmation, and exploratory interventional studies to advance the clinical translation of these discoveries, ultimately improving diagnostic outcomes and long-term prognosis for MASLD-PD patients.</p></section><section id="Sec18"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h2>
