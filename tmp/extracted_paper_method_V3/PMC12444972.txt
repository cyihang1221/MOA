</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ABS1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="P3">A significant number of compounds in exposome databases and chemical inventories lack mass spectral data due to the nonavailability of reference standards. To address this limitation, computational chemistry methods can be utilized to extend mass spectral libraries for a set of chemicals. In this pilot study, we employed quantum-chemistry-based software QCxMS to generate collision-induced dissociation mass spectra for 121 compounds from the Blood Exposome Database. We developed a scalable computational framework that integrates QCxMS and additional tools, utilizing a grid-based parameter selection strategy and defined coverage criteria. Our approach systematically explored protomeric isomers and applied predefined parameter combination sets sequentially based on molecular structures. This workflow produced high-quality <em>in silico</em> spectra for 81 compounds that achieved entropy similarity scores ≥700 and at least two matching fragment ions against the NIST23 library, yielding 71% spectral coverage. These results highlight the importance of optimizing simulation parameters and accounting for protomeric diversity to enhance the spectral quality and computational efficiency. This workflow provides a practical strategy to add mass spectral data for most compounds in the Blood Exposome Database at reasonable computational cost, supporting the spectral library expansion for improved compound annotation in exposomics.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Metabolomics, Blood Exposome, QCxMS, QCCIDMS, Mass Spectra Simulation, Semiempirical methods</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="ABS2"><h2>Graphical Abstract</h2>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/0e26/12444972/45909af0fdaa/nihms-2108140-f0001.jpg" loading="lazy" height="352" width="640" alt="graphic file with name nihms-2108140-f0001.jpg"></p></section><section id="S1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p id="P5">Exposome is the sum of all exposures during a person’s lifetime.<sup><a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">1</a></sup> Exogenous and endogenous chemicals are the major component of the exposome.<sup><a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">2</a>,<a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a></sup> Endogenously, chemicals are generated during biochemical reactions that are regulated by metabolic, signaling, and gene regulatory pathways.<sup><a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a></sup> Exogenous chemicals include environmental pollutants, dietary components, and chemicals found in everyday consumer products.<sup><a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a>,<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a></sup> It has been suggested that unwanted exposure to these chemicals may increase the risk of chronic diseases,<sup><a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a>,<a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a></sup> warranting their biomonitoring, toxicology, and epidemiological investigations.<sup><a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a></sup></p>
<p id="P6">Human biomonitoring data sets that cover a large number of chemicals are key elements for studying the exposome.<sup><a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a></sup> These data sets are generated using targeted and untargeted chemical analysis assays that are developed using mass spectrometry instruments.<sup><a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">9</a></sup> Particularly, for the untargeted assays, high resolution mass spectrometry is often used to detect thousands of chemicals in human blood or urine specimens.<sup><a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>,<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup> The Blood Exposome Database<sup><a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a></sup> catalogues almost 50,000 chemicals that are detected or expected in a blood sample. The high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) analysis of human specimens is often conducted for exposomic projects. However, a large number of peaks detected by gas chromatography or liquid chromatography mass spectrometry instruments remain unannotated, mainly due to the lack of good quality fragmentation mass spectra from samples and/or in a reference mass spectral library.<sup><a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>,<a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">12</a>,<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">13</a></sup> Structural characterization of these detected compounds is crucial for understanding exposure-related health outcomes, detecting diseases and their progression, and predicting future health risks.</p>
<p id="P7">To address this issue, <em>in silico</em> methods, including machine learning (ML) and quantum chemistry (QC) for predicting mass spectra, have been used in recent years. These approaches focus on predicting collision-induced dissociation (CID) tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) data which are commonly utilized for exposomics and metabolomics.<sup><a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a>,<a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">15</a></sup> Among ML approaches, CFM-ID<sup><a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">16</a></sup> performs mass spectral and fragmentation pathway prediction, while SIRIUS with CSI:FingerID<sup><a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">17</a></sup> performs molecular structure prediction from experimental data. Recent models such as ICEBERG,<sup><a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">18</a></sup> MassFormer,<sup><a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a></sup> and FIORA<sup><a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">19</a></sup> use neural networks to predict spectra from the molecular graphs of chemical structures. While promising, these ML-based methods are limited by the dependence on the structure diversity of the training data sets.<sup><a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a>,<a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a></sup> Furthermore, they often lack insights into the fragmentation pathway and reaction mechanisms.</p>
<p id="P8">In contrast, quantum chemistry-based methods provide a more transparent, physics-based approach,<sup><a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a></sup> without any training data. These methods compute electronic structures and potential energy surfaces directly from molecular geometries, typically using density functional theory (DFT) or semiempirical approximations. Software tools such as QCxMS<sup><a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a>–<a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a></sup> and CIDMD (Collision-Induced Dissociation Molecular Dynamics)<sup><a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a>,<a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">28</a></sup> implement QC-based molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to model and simulate (de)-protonated species undergoing energetic collisions. This approach enables prediction of fragmentation pathways and generates mechanistically interpretable <em>in silico</em> CID spectra. QCxMS has been successfully demonstrated in simulating complex fragmentation processes for diverse chemical classes, including small organic metabolites,<sup><a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a></sup> pharmaceuticals,<sup><a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29">29</a>,<a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">30</a></sup> and environmental pollutants such as pesticides and industrial coolants.<sup><a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a></sup> QC-based mass spectral prediction is an emerging approach with significant potential in exposomics, where high chemical diversity and limited training data constrain ML methods. Such QC-predicted mass spectra of structurally diverse and analytically challenging compounds can aid in the identification of previously unknown exposure chemicals.</p>
<p id="P9">Here, we present a pilot study that applies a modular and extensible framework for generating <em>in silico</em> CID mass spectra of 121 compounds from the Blood Exposome Database. Built around QCxMS with supporting tools, the framework systematically samples protomeric isomers and applies sequential predefined QCxMS parameter combinations based on the molecular structure using a grid-based strategy. Using this protomer- and parameter-driven approach, we generated 3939 QCxMS-predicted spectra and found that careful control over the initial protomers and simulation parameters significantly improves the spectral quality and computational efficiency. Bt evaluation of spectral similarity and fragment ion matches against the NIST23 mass spectral library,<sup><a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">32</a></sup> we achieved 71% spectral coverage. This study provides a practical roadmap for scaling in QC-based CID mass spectral simulations into high-throughput workflows at a reasonable computing cost, offering a promising solution for compound identification in exposomics.</p></section><section id="S2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">MATERIALS AND METHODS</h2>
<p id="P10">We integrated multiple software tools into a multistep framework and ran QCxMS-based molecular dynamics simulations to generate <em>in silico</em> collision-induced dissociation (CID) tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) spectra for 121 exposome compounds (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>).</p>
<section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Blood Exposome Selection and Preparation.</h3>
<p id="P11">We selected a total of 121 compounds from the Blood Exposome Database. These chemicals were chosen based on the following three criteria: (i) the availability of their reference spectra in the NIST 2023 database with positive ionization mode ([M + H]<sup>+</sup>), (ii) their frequent occurrence in peer-reviewed literature related to metabolomics and exposomics, and (iii) a molecular weight range between 100 and 200 Da. Chemical details including, InChiKey, SMILES code, and 3D structure coordinates were retrieved from the PubChem database.<sup><a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">33</a></sup> We grouped the chemicals as cyclic or noncyclic for selecting QCxMS parameters.</p>
<p id="P12">Next, we converted 3D structure coordinate files to XYZ format using Open Babel.<sup><a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34">34</a></sup> Then, we utilized the xTB software’s (v 6.7.0)<sup><a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35">35</a>,<a href="#R36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R36">36</a></sup> build-in geometry optimizer called ANCopt (Approximate Normal Coordinate rational function optimizer) to optimize the chemical structures. Using the automatic protonation tool of CREST (Conformer-Rotamer Ensemble Sampling Tool, v 3.0.2)<sup><a href="#R37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R37">37</a></sup> with GFN2-xTB semiempirical quantum method,<sup><a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35">35</a></sup> we generated protonated molecules in the form of [M + H]<sup>+</sup> that are within the 30 kcal/mol energy window. CREST provided topologically unique protomer structures that are ready for QCxMS calculation. The protomers are labeled starting with 0 (i.e., protomer_0, protomer_1, etc.), where protomer_0 indicates the energetically most stable protomer (Δ<em>E</em> = 0).</p></section><section id="S4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Hierarchical Workflow of QCxMS Calculations Using Protomer-Parameter Grid.</h3>
<p id="P13">Because QCxMS-predicted mass spectra are sensitive to both input parameters and molecular structure, we developed a hierarchical workflow for this study. Rather than relying on a single configuration, we designed a “protomer-parameter grid” strategy in which each protomeric structure was simulated under multiple parameter combinations, reflecting diverse collisional MD conditions. Protomers are isobaric species that are indistinguishable by HRMS alone and often produce distinct fragmentation patterns due to structural differences. Therefore, to capture the full range of spectral outcomes and improve the matching confidence, all protomeric forms generated by CREST are included. This approach yields multiple spectra per protomer and provides a comprehensive data set for assessing QCxMS performance and spectral quality across varying simulation conditions and protomer structures.</p>
<p id="P14">For this study, the parameter grid includes a variation of parameters that describes the desired collision energy values in lab frame (elab), the type of collision events (fullauto, maxcoll, or setcoll, abbreviated as fullauto, maxcol, and setcol for naming) with the number of the collision (X), with or without electrospray ionization (esi), and the N<sub>2</sub> collision gas used (iatom). One of the most important aspects in mass spectra is collision energy values (elab). Noncyclic molecules, particularly those with polar and flexible structures, are more susceptible to fragmentation under lower collision energies due to heterolytic cleavage of labile bonds and favorable off-center interactions. In contrast, cyclic or aromatic structures are more rigid and stabilized by conjugation, often requiring higher collision energies to achieve fragmentation. Therefore, to account for different characteristics of molecular geometries, each molecule was categorized as cyclic (C) for molecules containing at least one ring and as acyclic (A) for the rest. For group A exposome compounds, relatively lower collision energy values (elab = 20, 40, and 45 eV) were assigned, whereas the higher energies (elab = 40, 60, and 80 eV) were set for group C compounds.</p>
<p id="P15">In addition to collision energy, we applied three types of collision events that may influence the collisional MD simulations and, sequentially, the QCxMS <em>in silico</em> spectra. The developer-recommended collision type is fullauto, which automates QCxMS calculations with default settings for chamber length, gas pressure, and a fixed temperature at 300 K. We also included two forced activation collision types, maxcoll and setcoll, in our parameter grid. These types allow for precise control over the number of precursor-gas collisions (maxcoll) or both precursor-gas and fragment-gas collisions (setcoll). For these two collision types, we limited the number of trajectories to 80 per parameter set, rather than the default setting of general activation with 25 trajectories per atom. The compounds in our study are small and have relatively simple structures with limited possibility of diverse fragmentation pathways. Consequently, the number of trajectories needed for statistical convergence is relatively low, and in our case, 80 trajectories were sufficient for exploring molecular dissociation pathways and capturing the CID spectral characteristics reliably.</p>
<p id="P16">The parameter sets in the parameter grid followed a naming convention of the form n2-[elab] for fullauto collision events, n2-[maxcol/setcol][X]-[elab] for specified collision types, and n2-esi-[maxcol/setcol][X]-[elab] for indication of ESI-mode enabled. Based on this naming convention, the complete parameter grid was constructed separately for cyclic and acyclic compounds as follows:
</p>
<ul id="L1" class="list" style="list-style-type:disc">
<li><p id="P17">Cyclic exposome compounds: n2-maxcol4–60, n2-setcol4–60, n2-maxcol4–80, n2-setcol4–80, n2-maxcol6–60, n2-setcol6–60, n2-maxcol6–80, n2-setcol6–80, n2–60, n2-esi-maxcol4–60, n2-esi-setcol4–60</p></li>
<li><p id="P18">Acyclic exposome compounds: n2-maxcol4–40, n2-setcol4–40, n2-maxcol4–25, n2-setcol4–25, n2-maxcol6–25, n2-setcol6–25, n2-maxcol6–40, n2-setcol6–40, n2–40, n2-esi-maxcol4–40, n2-esi-setcol4–40</p></li>
</ul>
<p id="P19">QCxMS allows for precollision internal energy adjustment via the esi keyword, which scales the internal energy automatically based on the molecular ion size. This thermal preactivation is applied prior to the collisional MD production step and can enhance fragmentation by increasing internal energy. In this study, we only applied esi fullauto or where esi was explicitly included in the parameter name. This distinction allowed us to evaluate the impact of preloading internal energy on fragmentation efficiency and the resulting spectral diversity.</p>
<p id="P20">We performed QCxMS simulations (v5.2.1) using the GFN2-xTB method<sup><a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a>,<a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">24</a>,<a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a></sup> with D4 dispersion corrections<sup><a href="#R38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R38">38</a></sup> to generate <em>in silico</em> CID mass spectra for all protomer structures obtained in the previous step. For each protomer, each unique input parameter set in the grid was applied for the three-stage QCxMS protocol: (1) an initial ground-state molecular dynamics (MD) simulation in the constant temperature (NVT, canonical) ensemble at 500 K with a 0.5 fs time step for equilibration, (2) a subsequent energy-conserving (NVE, microcanonical) ensemble MD simulation for sampling structural conformers, and (3) parallelized collisional MD production runs from the sampled conformers. In the production stage, QCxMS simulated 25 collisional trajectories per atom for fullauto collision types and 80 trajectories for maxcoll and setcoll collision types using a 0.5 fs time step and the leapfrog algorithm. The resulting <em>in silico</em> spectrum was compiled using PlotMS (v6.2.0) into JCAMP-DX (Joint Committee on Atomic and Molecular Physical data) format that reports <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values and relative intensities. Finally, JCMAP-DX files were converted into the NIST MSP (Mass Spectral Profile) format, a fixed structure for descriptive mass spectral information, from using in-house scripts.</p></section><section id="S5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">
<em>In Silico</em> Spectral Analyses and Coverage.</h3>
<p id="P21">QCxMS-simulated mass spectra for each protomer were compared to corresponding NIST23 reference spectra using in-house scripts and the IDSL.CSA R package,<sup><a href="#R39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R39">39</a></sup> which computes metrics including entropy similarity<sup><a href="#R40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R40">40</a></sup> and matched fragments. Entropy similarity, a widely used spectral similarity metric, ranges from 0 to 1000, with 1000 representing a perfect match. However, high entropy scores can be misleading in spectra with unfragmented precursor ions or minimal fragmentation by potentially inflating the scores. To mitigate this, we defined spectral coverage as was an entropy score ≥700 with at least two correctly predicted <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values (to two decimal place), regardless of intensity. This definition ensures overall similarity to the presence of chemically relevant fragmentation.</p></section><section id="S6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Computational Details.</h3>
<p id="P22">We conducted all calculations including QCxMS simulations using a combination of two cloud-based central processing units (CPU) with Intel Xeon Gold 6312U processors (24 cores, 48 threads, 2.4–3.6 GHz), and one local CPU server with an Intel Xeon E5–2687W v2 processor (16 cores, 32 threads, 3.40 GHz).</p>
<p id="P23">All in-house scripts are available on GitHub including: (a) installation of all software required, (b) project setup and preparation given a PubChem CID number input file, (c) main QCxMS calculation runs, and last (d) the spectral analysis (see <a href="#S17" class="usa-link">Data Availability Statement</a>). The user provided input for the IDSL.CSA R package is accessible at Zenodo (see <a href="#S17" class="usa-link">Data Availability Statement</a>).</p></section></section><section id="S7"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS</h2>

Using our grid, each compound was simulated across multiple protomers and QCxMS parameter combinations, including collision types (fullauto, maxcol, setcol), collision energies (25, 40, 60, 80 eV), internal energy settings (esi, noesi), and numbers of collision events (4, 6). We successfully generated 3939 <em>in silico</em> collision-induced dissociation (CID) mass spectra of 121 compounds, with 86 compounds meeting our spectral coverage criteria. <p id="P27">In untargeted metabolomics and exposomics experiments, MS/MS are coupled with a soft ionization method, typically electrospray ionization (ESI) that generates a range of ions but primarily protomers. During ESI, molecules undergo protonation at multiple sites, occasionally forming high-energy tautomers or conformers that would not normally exist at equilibrium in solution.<sup><a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">41</a>,<a href="#R42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R42">42</a></sup> Since the formation of gas phase ions is governed by a nonequilibrium desolvation process, certain high-energy protomers can be kinetically trapped during processes that introduce additional energy like collisional activation and an electric field. Therefore, multiple protomeric forms that deviate from Boltzmann-like populations may contribute to a single experimental MS/MS spectrum due to their distinct fragmentation pathways.<sup><a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43">43</a></sup> Unlike experimental conditions, QCxMS operates on a one-protomer–one-spectrum basis, accepting a single, protonated molecular structure as the input geometry. To more fully capture diverse protomers under experimental conditions, we included all protomers generated within a 30 kcal/mol energy window from the global minimum via CREST-based protonation screening for each molecule. While high-energy protomers may be thermodynamically disfavored, they remain chemically plausible due to the nonequilibrium nature of ESI.<sup><a href="#R44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R44">44</a>,<a href="#R45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R45">45</a></sup> Of the 121 blood exposome database compounds, 18 yielded only one protomeric form via CREST (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>) and 27 compounds had two, while 76 compounds had three or more. In one extreme case, up to ten distinct protonation states were generated for a single cyclic compound, sesamol (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S2</a>). Across all simulations, the lowest-energy protomer (protomer_0) accounted for the best-matching spectrum in 62 out of 121 compounds (51%) as displayed in <a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>. This trend is maintained for both cyclic and acyclic compounds. However, nearly half of the best-matching spectra arose from higher-energy protomers (protomer_1 and above), emphasizing that thermodynamic stability alone does not dictate fragmentation outcomes. Specifically, the second lowest energy protomer (protomer_1) contributed 30 best-matching spectra (25%), comprising 29% of the best acyclic spectra and 22% of the best cyclic spectra. The third protomer (protomer_2) yielded 13 best-matches with 6 from acyclic and 7 from cyclic structures. Notably, 16 spectra with the highest entropy similarity were generated from protomers ranked protomer_3–protomer_5, all of which were from cyclic compounds. These results demonstrate that the dominant fragmentation pathway often originates not from the most stable structure, but from the structure with the most reactive charge distribution.<sup><a href="#R46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R46">46</a>–<a href="#R48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R48">48</a></sup></p>
<p id="P28">Our systematic analysis reveals that 49% of best-matching spectra (59 out of 121 compounds) originated from higher-energy protomers (protomer_1+) rather than the ground state structure (protomer_0), providing quantitative confirmation of recent findings that thermodynamic stability does not always dictate fragmentation outcomes.<sup><a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a>,<a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">28</a></sup> Under collisional activation, even protonation states with relative energies up to 30 kcal/mol can become relevant, since deposition of cumulative energy can transiently overcome the barriers to proton transfer or rearrangement and create mobile protons.<sup><a href="#R47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R47">47</a></sup> Therefore, while the prevalence of protomer_0 in over half of the successful cases supports its relevance, the failure of this most stable structure to predict best-matching spectra in the remaining compounds demonstrates that a single-protomer assumption is insufficient for accurate and confident spectral prediction. CREST provides a thermodynamic ranking, but QCxMS outcomes often reflect kinetic or reactive accessibility rather than equilibrium distributions. These results emphasize the importance of including multiple protomeric forms in QCxMS simulations.</p></section><section id="S10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Effect of Parameters on QCxMS Spectral Match Quality.</h3>
<p id="P29">To evaluate the influence of QCxMS simulation settings on spectral quality, we analyzed which parameter combinations most frequently produced the best-matching <em>in silico</em> spectra across all 121 exposome compounds. Each protomer was subjected to a systematic grid of 11 distinct parameter combinations. These parameter sets varied by collision type (maxcol, setcol, or fullauto), number of collisions (4 or 6), collision energy (25, 40, 60, or 80 eV in lab frame), and the inclusion of precollision thermal activation via ESI. Collision energy settings in the parameter grid were tailored to compound structure with cyclic molecules evaluated at higher energies (60 and 80 eV) and acyclic compounds at lower energies (25 and 40 eV). (See <a href="#S7" class="usa-link">Materials and Methods</a> for full details.) The resulting spectra revealed that the effects of parameter settings on simulation accuracy varied with the molecular structure.</p></section><section id="S11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Cyclic Compounds.</h3>
<p id="P30">Among the 76 cyclic structures, the parameter set n2-maxcol4–60 (four maximum-energy precursor-only collisions at 60 eV with nitrogen gas) yielded the highest number of best-matching spectra, accounting for nearly 40% of the cases (30 compounds). A similar parameter set with precollision thermal activation, n2-esi-maxcol4–60, was the second most effective setting for 23 compounds (30%). Together, these two conditions accounted for nearly 70% of all of the best matches in the cyclic group. These were followed by the n2-maxcol6–60 parameter set, which accounted for 9 compounds (12%) yielding their best-matching spectra and n2-setcol6–60 accounting for 4 compounds (5%). The parameter sets n2-setcol6–80 and n2-esi-setcol4–60 had 3 matches, while n2-setcol4–60, n2-maxcol6–80, n2-maxcol4–80, and n2–60 yielded only 1 match each. The n2-setcol4–80 combination did not yield any best-matching QCxMS spectra.</p>
<p id="P31">When analyzed by collision type, cyclic compounds exhibited a strong preference for maxcol, with 64 of 76 (84%) best-matching spectra resulting from this precursor-only collision condition. In contrast, setcol, which enables additional fragment-gas collisions, accounted for only 14% (11 spectra). This preference for maxcol suggests that direct precursor activation is more effective in simulating spectra for small, rigid cyclic compounds (100–200 Da), where the number of accessible fragmentation pathways is inherently limited. For these compounds, setcol or fullauto collision types may deposit insufficient energy for effective distribution and/or bond cleavage. The explicit collision types in QCxMS parameters like maxcol and setcol require the collision number input since they can influence spectral quality. For cyclic blood exposome compounds, a large majority (76%) of their best-matching spectrum came from four- rather than six-collision setups. This result indicates that increasing collision numbers does not necessarily enhance spectral accuracy significantly for these relatively rigid structures. More collisions are likely to raise internal energy, but not always in a way that results in informative fragment ions. Collision energy for cyclic compounds had a strong effect. Among the best-matching spectra, 93% were produced at 60 eV, while only 7% favored the higher energy of 80 eV. Notably, even at 60 eV, many spectra retained a visible precursor ion peak alongside small fragment ions (often <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> &lt; 50). These patterns show that the collision energy applied is sufficiently energetic to induce informative fragmentations. Therefore, 60 eV likely presents an optimal collision energy for these small cyclic molecular structures.</p>
<p id="P32">Lastly, the effect of precollision thermal activation with ESI parameter appeared minor for this compound class. For example, n2-esi-maxcol4–60 and n2-maxcol4–60 yielded 23 and 30 best matches, respectively. The n2-maxcol4–60 condition slightly outperformed by producing 30 best-matching spectra compared to its ESI-enabled counterpart, n2-esi-maxcol4–60, which had 23 spectra. However, in the case of n2-setcol4–60 and n2-esi-setcol4–60, it was the opposite, where n2-esi-setcol4–60 outperformed by only a couple of spectra (3 to 1). This negligible effect of ESI suggests it has limited influence on spectral quality for these small cyclic blood exposome compounds relative to other key parameters like collision energy and collision types.</p></section><section id="S12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Acyclic Compounds.</h3>
<p id="P33">For the 45 acyclic compounds, we applied a separate grid of 11 parameter sets tailored to their typically more labile structure using lower collision energies (25 and 40 eV). The top-performing parameter combination setup was n2-maxcol6–25, which produced best-matching spectra for 9 compounds (20%), followed by n2-setcol4–25 generating 8 best-matching QCxMS spectra (18%), and n2-setcol6–25 with 7 best-matches (16%). Including the parameter set n2-maxcol4–25 that yielded 5 best-matching spectra (11%), these top four parameter settings accounted for 64% of all best-matching acyclic spectra.</p>
<p id="P34">Unlike the cyclic group, where strong preferences on most parameter selections were demonstrated, acyclic compounds exhibited diverse distribution of optimal parameter conditions. Acyclic compounds presented a relatively balanced split between maxcol 47% (21 spectra) and setcol 51% (23 spectra). Only one spectrum was produced with fullauto collision type. This broader distribution suggests that fragment-level collisions provided by setcol can be useful for noncyclic structures that are prone to secondary fragmentation. Similarity, 51% of acyclic compounds (23 best-matching spectra) favored a four-collision setup, while 47% (21 spectra) favored a six-collision setup. These results suggest that increasing the number of collisions often enhances fragmentation in compounds with flexible molecular backbones. The lower energy setting of 25 eV was optimal for 64% of acyclic compounds (29 spectra), while 40 eV was favored by the remaining 36% (16 spectra). Despite the relatively low energies, many spectra still exhibited low-mass fragment ions (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> &lt; 50) with precursor peaks, indicating that even mild activation often efficiently fragments acyclic structures. Compared to cyclic compounds of similar molecular weight, these systems appear inherently more prone to fragmentation and may be overfragmented at higher energies.</p>
<p id="P35">Overall, these findings highlight that optimal QCxMS parameter settings are highly structure-dependent. The cyclic compounds under 200 Da tend to yield more accurate simulated spectra under moderate collision energy (60 eV), precursor-only collision type with 4 collision events (maxcol4). In contrast, acyclic compounds with similar molecular weights benefit from a broader range of conditions, including lower collision energy (25 eV), multicollision schemes that involve both precursor and fragment ions (setcol). The comparable performance of setcol and maxcol for acyclic compounds suggests that multistep fragmentation pathways play a more significant role in reproducing experimental spectra for flexible molecular structures.</p></section><section id="S13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Computational Performance of <em>In Silico</em> MS/MS Simulations.</h3>
<p id="P36">To evaluate the computational cost of our QCxMS-based workflow, we analyzed CPU wall times for the most resource-intensive stage: the production run. This step involves multiple MD simulations of collision processes that cascade through fragmentation. We simulated 3939 <em>in silico</em> spectra across 121 compounds. The total cumulative CPU wall time required for the production runs was 13,557.42 h (single CPU), and individual production run times per system varied widely, from 5.19 min to 138.96 h. On average, QCxMS required 3.44 h per production run, with a median of 1.98 h. Notably, 93% of all systems (3667 out of 3939) completed in under 9 h, and half of them (1970) required 2 h or less (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S3</a>). No correlation was observed between protomer type and CPU wall time. Among parameter variations, systems simulated with maxcol4 were the most efficient, which was followed closely by maxcol6 (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S4</a>). To understand the computational requirements of generating high-quality QCxMS spectra, we focused on the 121 best-matching spectra, each representing the optimal protomeric form and parameter configuration for a given compound. These systems had production runtimes ranging from 20.74 min to 8.81 h. The median runtime was 1.89 h, with a mean of 2.39 h. Over half (64 out of 121) completed in under 2 h, and 82% (100 of 121) finished in less than 4 h. The most common runtime was 1–2 h, accounting for 30% (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S5</a>). These results demonstrate that longer simulation times do not necessarily produce better-matching spectra. Twenty-one systems that were simulated with maxcol4 were the most computationally efficient, with a mean runtime under 100 min. Overall, while certain parameter choices, particularly collision type and number of collisions, clearly influenced computational performance, the protomer types based on their relative energy values did not. Similarly, the QCxMS simulation time of the production runs did not correlate with <em>in silico</em> spectral quality. Many high-coverage, best-matching QCxMS spectra were often generated in less than a couple of hours, though some required over 8 h (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S6</a>). Our results demonstrated that QCxMS can generate useful MS/MS spectra at a reasonable computational cost.</p></section></section><section id="S14"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">DISCUSSION</h2>
<p id="P37">This pilot study demonstrates the feasibility of utilizing quantum chemistry-based MS/MS prediction,<sup><a href="#R49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R49">49</a></sup> specifically QCxMS(x = CID),<sup><a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a></sup> to generate spectra for compounds in the Blood Exposome Database.<sup><a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a></sup> The developed hierarchical workflow, incorporating a parameter grid and multiple protomers, achieved spectral coverage for a significant portion of the selected compounds. We advocate the use of these computational methods for a large-scale expansion of mass spectral libraries to cover the compounds that are not covered by the chemical space of known mass spectral libraries. Use of these methods for substance inventories such as The US Toxic Substances Control Act - Chemical Substance Inventory and the US Food and Drug Administration - Global Substance Registration System can aid in the biomonitoring of emerging concerning contaminants and toxic substances in human populations.</p>
<p id="P38">A key outcome is that our strategy yielded useful MS/MS spectra for approximately 71% of the tested compounds, addressing a crucial gap in compound identification for exposomics due to the frequent unavailability of reference standards.<sup><a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a></sup> The coverage can be further increased by simulated ESI negative mode ions (e.g., [M – H]<sup>−</sup>, [M + Cl]<sup>−</sup>).<sup><a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">25</a></sup> The simulated spectra demonstrated high similarity and matched fragment ions with NIST library data.</p>
<p id="P39">The study highlighted that relying solely on the lowest-energy protomer (protomer_0) is inadequate for accurate spectral prediction. While protomer_0 provided the best-matching spectrum in 51% of cases, higher-energy protomers were essential for nearly half of the compounds, especially cyclic ones. This aligns with the nonequilibrium nature of electrospray ionization, which can trap kinetically favored, higher-energy protomers that subsequently fragment. The varied fragmentation<sup><a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a></sup> from different protomers underscores the need to include multiple protomeric forms in simulations.</p>
<p id="P40">Systematic evaluation of QCxMS parameters revealed a structure-dependent optimization. Rigid cyclic compounds typically required higher collision energies (60 eV) and fewer precursor-only collision events (maxcol). In contrast, flexible acyclic compounds benefited from lower collision energies (25 eV) and a broader range of conditions, including setcol (allowing additional fragment-gas collisions) and varied collision numbers. This indicates that for acyclic molecules, multistep fragmentation pathways are more significant. These tailored parameter grids are vital for efficiently generating the quality spectra.</p>
<p id="P41">QCxMS can produce useful spectra at a reasonable cost. The median production runtime for the best-matching spectra was 1.89 h, with over 80% generated in under 4 h. This shows that lengthy simulations do not always yield better quality. Efficient parametrization, like using maxcol4 for cyclic compounds, offers good results without excessive demand. The total computational effort is manageable with modern computing resources.</p>
<p id="P42">Limitations include the pilot scale (121 compounds, 100–200 Da, [M + H]<sup>+</sup> only). The “spectral coverage” definition could be refined. Compounds not covered might need different QCxMS conditions or involve fragmentation mechanisms that are not fully captured. Future work should focus on expanding this approach to a larger and more diverse set of compounds from the Blood Exposome Database, including different adduct types (e.g., [M + Na]<sup>+</sup>, [M – H]<sup>−</sup>)<sup><a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">25</a></sup> and a wider range of molecular weights. Refining parameter grids and investigating uncovered cases will be beneficial. Integrating predicted spectra into public databases would be a valuable resource. There is also a need for systematic comparison between quantum chemistry-based methods (e.g., QCxMS) and machine learning models (e.g., CFM-ID or ICEBERG) for predicting CID mass spectra for small molecules.</p></section><section id="S15"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CONCLUSION</h2>
<p id="P43">We have established a scalable quantum chemistry-based framework centered on QCxMS, incorporating a protomer- and parameter-aware grid strategy for the <em>in silico</em> generation of mass spectra of blood exposome compounds. The approach yields high-quality <em>in silico</em> spectra with efficient use of computational resources, offering a practical and extensible path for significantly augmenting CID-MS/MS spectral libraries. This QC-based approach offers advantages over machine learning methods, particularly in the context of the highly diverse chemical space of the exposome. Unlike ML models, which are limited by the structural diversity of their training data, these physics-based QC methods require no prior experimental training data for the target compounds and provide mechanistic insights into fragmentation pathways. This makes QCxMS particularly well-suited for novel or understudied chemicals prevalent in exposome research. The modular design of this framework supports high-throughput scalability and integration into a broader workflow, providing a robust foundation for comprehensive spectral annotation and compound identification in exposomics. This work contributes to the broader goal of identifying previously uncharacterized chemicals in human biospecimens, thereby advancing our understanding of the exposome and its implications for human health.</p></section><section id="SM1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
