</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">OBJECTIVE:</h3>
<p id="p00005">To investigate the mechanisms of the effect of Shenji Guben (SJGB) decoction (参吉固本方) on chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">METHODS:</h3>
<p id="p00010">A murine model of COPD was established through lipopolysaccharide (LPS) nasal drops and passive smoke exposure, followed by evaluation of SJGB decoction efficacy<em> via</em> lung function tests and histological analysis. Non-targeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based metabolomics was used to explore the mechanisms of SJGB decoction in COPD.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS:</h3>
<p id="p00015">We found that the SJGB decoction effectively reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in the airways and lungs, and improved lung function in the COPD model mice. LC-MS-based metabolomics identified 86 biomarkers in COPD models. Compared to the model group, SJGB decoction significantly altered 34 metabolites. Prostaglandin E2 and DL-Citrulline were highlighted as two representative differential metabolites. MetaboAnalyst 5.0 highlighted glycerophospholipid and riboflavin metabolisms as key pathways affected by SJGB decoction.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">CONCLUSION:</h3>
<p id="p00020">This study evaluated the protective effect of SJGB decoction against COPD and provided insights into its potential mechanisms in COPD treatment.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> pulmonary disease, chronic obstructive; metabolomics; glycerophospholipids; dinoprostone; Shenji Guben decoction</p></section></section><section id="s1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p id="p00025">Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a heterogeneous pulmonary disorder marked by enduring respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, cough, and expectoration) aroused from persistent abnormalities of the airways (such as bronchitis and bronchiolitis) and/or alveoli (emphysema), leading to sustained and frequently progressive airflow obstruction.<sup><a href="#b1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b1">1</a></sup> Approximately 544.9 million individuals were estimated to suffer from chronic respiratory conditions in 2017, with COPD accounting for 55% of these cases.<sup><a href="#b2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b2">2</a></sup> COPD ranked the third leading cause of global death in 2019.<sup><a href="#b3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b3">3</a></sup> It's well-established that tobacco smoking is a major risk factor for COPD, and emerging evidence has also suggested the involvement of other environmental risks, such as exposure to harmful particles and gases from household and ambient air pollution.<sup><a href="#b4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b4">4</a></sup></p>
<p id="p00030">Bronchodilators remain the cornerstone of COPD treatment, which can widen airways and alleviate symptoms. Meanwhile, anti-inflammatory agents (such as corticosteroids) are commonly used to mitigate inflammation flare-ups.<sup><a href="#b5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b5">5</a></sup> Nonetheless, in addition to potential systemic side effects (such as bone fractures), some COPD patients may develop resistance to corticosteroid therapy. Recently, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has offered a promising alternative to managing chronic respiratory disorders, leveraging its fewer side effects. TCM has exhibited significant clinical efficacy in alleviating COPD symptoms, decreasing acute exacerbation rates, and improving life quality of patients.<sup><a href="#b6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b6">6</a></sup></p>
<p id="p00035">Professor CAO Shihong, a renowned TCM practitioner from Jiangsu Province, has emphasized the treatment of the “lung, spleen, and kidney” in managing stable COPD. In TCM, COPD is primarily attributed to the recurrent and persistent nature of various chronic lung disorders. The underlying cause is considered to be a deficiency of<em> Qi </em>in the lungs, spleen, and kidneys. In TCM theory, the lungs are the foundation of<em> Qi </em>and the principal organ responsible for respiration, the kidneys are the root of<em> Qi </em>and regulate the depth of breathing, and the spleen is vital for the production and circulation of<em> Qi </em>and blood. Therefore, the treatment of COPD in TCM necessitates a holistic approach that addresses the lungs, spleen, and kidneys. Professor CAO has meticulously developed the Shenji Guben (SJGB) decoction (参吉固本方) based on years of clinical experience and the principle of "nourishing the kidney to strengthen the foundation."<sup><a href="#b7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b7">7</a></sup> The primary aim of this formula is to fortify and protect kidney function. In TCM, the kidney is considered vital to overall health and is the foundation of the five organs and six viscera. Proper kidney function is crucial for maintaining normal respiratory processes and supporting the body’s positive<em> Qi</em>. Since COPD is often associated with deficiency syndromes, it is particularly important to nourish the heart and lungs or support the kidneys and spleen to bolster the body's defenses. The SJGB formula composed of nine herbs [Dangshen (<em>Radix Codonopsis</em>), Huangqi (<em>Radix Astragali Mongolici</em>), Bajitian (<em>Radix Morindae Officinalis</em>), Tusizi (<em>Semen Cuscutae</em>), Shudihuang (<em>Radix Rehmanniae Praeparata</em>), Baizhu (<em>Rhizoma Atractylodis Macrocephalae</em>), Guizhi (<em>Ramulus Cinnamomi</em>), Danshen (<em>Radix Salviae Miltiorrhizae</em>), and Zhimahuang (<em>Processing Herba Ephedra Sinica</em>)] has shown clinically verified efficacy in alleviating symptoms and improving quality of life in patients with stable COPD.<sup><a href="#b8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b8">8</a></sup> However, its action mechanism is still unclear.</p>
<p id="p00040">Metabolomics focuses on revealing the changes in endogenous metabolites within organisms under genetic or environmental stimuli and reflecting the pathological and physiological states.<sup><a href="#b9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b9">9</a></sup> This approach conforms to the holistic and dynamic perspectives of TCM and has been extensively applied to explore the essence, diagnosis, and therapeutic evaluation of COPD. Lung tissue metabolomics serves as a powerful tool for exploring the mechanisms underlying respiratory diseases and identifying potential biomarkers.<sup><a href="#b10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b10">10</a>,<a href="#b11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b11">11</a></sup> Metabolomics research indicates that disturbances in amino acid and lipid metabolisms, dysregulation of energy production pathways, and imbalances between oxidation and antioxidation can promote COPD progression.<sup><a href="#b12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b12">12</a></sup> Unsaturated fatty acid and phospholipid metabolisms have been identified as potential pathways related to COPD treatment with herbal formulations, particularly arachidonic acid and its metabolites, emerging as new therapeutic targets for COPD.<sup><a href="#b13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b13">13</a></sup></p>
<p id="p00045">The present study aimed to assess the efficacy of SJGB decoction in a murine model of COPD induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and passive smoking. Through metabolomics, the changes in lung metabolites in COPD mice treated with SJGB decoction were analyzed to identify potential targets and mechanisms underlying the therapeutic action of SJGB decoction against COPD.</p></section><section id="s2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. MATERIALS AND METHODS</h2>
<section id="s2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Preparation of SJGB decoction</h3>
<p id="p00050">All herbs were sourced from the Jiangsu Province Hospital of Chinese Medicine (Nanjing, China) and authenticated by Professor ZHU Yufeng (Department of Pharmacy, Jiangsu Province Chinese Medicine Hospital, Nanjing, China). The SJGB formula was composed of the following components: Dangshen (<em>Radix Codonopsis</em>) 20 g, Huangqi (<em>Radix Astragali Mongolici</em>) 15 g, Bajitian (<em>Radix Morindae Officinalis</em>) 10 g, Tusizi (<em>Semen Cuscutae</em>) 10 g, Shudihuang (<em>Radix Rehmanniae Praeparata</em>) 10 g, Baizhu (<em>Rhizoma Atractylodis Macrocephalae</em>) 10 g, Guizhi (<em>Ramulus Cinnamomi</em>) 6 g, Danshen (<em>Radix Salviae Miltiorrhizae</em>) 12 g, and Zhimahuang (<em>Processing Herba Ephedra Sinica</em>) 4 g. In short, 5 portions of SJGB decoction herbal mixtures were separately soaked in 3 liters of deionized water at 20 ℃ for 30 min. Subsequently, this mixture was heated to boiling and maintained for another 30 min for decoction preparation. This extraction process was conducted twice. The supernatant was obtained after combining the two decoctions and then concentrated to a density of 3 g/mL at 70 ℃ using a rotary evaporator (Eyela, Tokyo, Japan). Finally, the concentrate was lyophilized using a freeze-dryer (Biocool, Beijing, China) to prepare lyophilized powder.</p></section><section id="s2.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Characterization of the chemical compound profile of SJGB decoction</h3>
<p id="p00055">Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) analysis was conducted on SJGB decoction using a SHIMADZU-LC30 UHPLC system with an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 µm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) and a mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The sample injection volume was 10 µL, and the column temperature was maintained at 40 ℃ with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 100% acetonitrile (B). The gradient elution procedure was: 0-2 min, 0% B; 2-4 min, 25% B; 4-22 min, 25-100% B; 25-25.1 min, 100% B; 25.1-30 min, 100-0% B, followed by equilibration at 0% B.</p>
<p id="p00060">The separated molecules were analysed using a QE Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with electrospray ionization (ESI) in both positive and negative ionization modes. ESI source conditions were: Spray voltage: 3.8 kV (+) and 3.2 kV (－); Capillary Temperature: 320 (±); Sheath Gas: 30 (±); Aux Gas: (±); Probe Heater Temp: 350 (±); S-Lens RF Level: 50. Raw data were processed using MSDIAL software for peak detection, retention time correction, and peak area extraction. Compound identification was performed by BIOPRO-FILE Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).</p></section><section id="s2.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Establishment of a murine model of COPD</h3>
<p id="p00065">Passive smoking and intratracheal LPS (Cat: L2880-10MG, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) administration were adopted to establish a murine model of COPD{}.<sup><a href="#b14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b14">14</a></sup> The mice were exposed to cigarette smoke in an inhalation chamber (Cat: C-350-S, Yuyan Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) twice a day, 30 min each, with a minimum interval of 6 h between sessions for a continuous period of 16 weeks. For each smoking session, 10 cigarettes (Red Flag Canal, Anyang, China) were lit sequentially. On the 1st and 14th day of the experiment, LPS (7.5 μg in 50 μL of saline per mouse) was administered<em> via </em>droplets to the nostrils using a micropipette. On the days of receiving LPS administration, smoking exposure was omitted. Mice in the control group received an equal volume of sterile saline.</p></section><section id="s2.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. Mouse and treatment</h3>
<p id="p00070">A total of 60 male C57BL/6J mice (weighing 20-22 g) supplied by the Sibeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) [license No. SCXK (Su) 2022-0006; certification No. 202350597] were acclimated for one week in a specific pathogen-free animal facility with a standard diet and pure water. They were randomly allocated into 6 groups (<em>n =</em> 10): (a) control group; (b) COPD model group; (c) COPD plus low-dose SJGB decoction group (SJGB-l); (d) COPD plus medium-dose SJGB decoction group (SJGB-m); (e) COPD plus high-dose SJGB decoction group (SJGB-h); (f) COPD plus N-Acetylcysteine group (NAC; Cat: H20000472, Fluimucil, China). Starting from the 13th week of the experiment, mice in the treatment groups were orally administered SJGB decoction at low (7.5 g·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), medium (15 g·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), and high (30 g·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹) doses daily. The medium dose of SJGB decoction for mice was based on the equivalent dose conversion from the clinical dose for humans, which is 97 g/60 kg/d; the low and high doses were half and twice the medium dose, respectively. Mice in the positive control group were treated with NAC (78 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹) twice daily at 6-h intervals. Mice in the COPD model and control groups received an equal volume of distilled water<em> via </em>oral gavage. This study (2023DW-027-01) was approved by the Ethics Commission for Animal Tests of Jiangsu Province Hospital of Chinese Medicine.</p></section><section id="s2.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Detection of lung functions and sample collection</h3>
<p id="p00075">EMKA Small Animal Noninvasive Lung Function Test System (Emka Technologies, Paris, France) was used for respiratory function measurement in the 12th week and after the last oral administration. Enhanced pause (Penh) variations were evaluated using escalating doses of aerosolized methacholine (A2251, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), ranging from 0 to 50 mg/mL at intervals of 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, and 25 mg/mL.</p>
<p id="p00080">After lung function measurement for 24 h, animals were anesthetized using 1% sodium pentobarbital and euthanized by exsanguination<em> via </em>orbital exenteration. Blood samples were centrifuged (860 × <em>g</em>, 15 min, 4 ℃) to isolate the serum. The middle lobe of the left lung was preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent sectioning and histopathological examination.</p></section><section id="s2.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Histopathological analysis</h3>
<p id="p00085">Following paraffin embedding, lung tissue sections (4 μm) were prepared and subjected to hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining (GP1031, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The histopathological alterations of lung tissues were observed by microscopic examination.</p>
<p id="p00090">In HE-stained lung tissue sections, peribronchial inflammation severity was assessed on a scale from 0 to 5 (0, no cellular presence; 1, sparse cellular infiltration; 2, a single cell layer ring around bronchi; 3, a two-cell layer ring; 4, a three to four cell layer ring; 5, a ring exceeding four cell layers).<sup><a href="#b15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b15">15</a></sup> Pulmonary septal number (NS) at the microscope's crosshairs center and the total crosshairs length (L) were recorded. The mean linear intercept (MLI), representing average alveolar diameter, was determined as L/NS, based on three selected fields per section.<sup><a href="#b16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b16">16</a></sup></p></section><section id="s2.7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.7. Metabolomics analysis of lung samples</h3>
<p id="p00095">Lung tissue samples (50 mg) were homogenized for 3 min with ten grinding beads and 400 µL of extraction solvent (methanol: acetonitrile = 1∶1 (v/v)), containing 0.02 mg/mL of internal standard (L-2-chlorophenylalanine), followed by vortexing for 15 min. The samples were then incubated at －20 ℃ for 20 min and centrifuged at 18 000 ×<em>g</em> for 20 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant was transferred to a new glass vial for analysis. Equal volumes of supernatant were pooled from each sample to prepare quality control (QC) samples. Lung samples were analysed for metabolite profiles using a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water: acetonitrile (95∶5, v/v), while mobile phase B was composed of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile: isopropanol:water (47.5∶47.5, v/v). The elution conditions were as follows: (a) for positive ions: 0-3 min (0-20% B), 3-4.5 min (20-35% B), 4.5-5 min (35-100% B), 5-6.3 min (100% B), 6.3-6.4 min (100-0% B), 6.4-8 min (0% B); (b) for negative ions: 0-1.5 min (0-5% B), 1.5-2 min (5-10% B), 2-4.5 min (10-30% B), 4.5-5 min (30-100% B), 5-6.3 min (100% B), 6.3-6.4 min (100-0% B), 6.4-8 min (0% B). The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min and the column temperature was 40 ℃. The injection volume was 3 μL. The electrospray ionization source conditions were set as follows: source temperature, 425 ℃; sheath gas flow rate, 50 arb; Aux gas flow rate, 13 arb; ion-spray voltage floating at －3500V in negative mode and 3500V in positive mode, respectively; normalized collision energy, 20-40-60V rolling for MS/MS. Full MS resolution was 60000, and MS/MS resolution was 7500. Data acquisition was performed in the Data Dependent Acquisition mode. The detection was carried out over a mass range of 70-1050 m/z.</p></section><section id="s2.8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.8. Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="p00100">Data were represented as mean ± standard deviation and analysed using Student’s<em> t</em>-test (two-tailed) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test. A <em>P</em>-value &lt; 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.</p>
<p id="p00105">For metabolomics, raw LC/MS data were pre-processed using Progenesis Qi v3.0 software (Waters, Milford, MA, USA), resulting in a three-dimensional data matrix including sample details, metabolite names, and mass spectral intensities. Internal standard and known false positive peaks (e.g., noise, column bleed, and derivatized reagent peaks) were eliminated. The data were then deduplicated and peaks were pooled. Metabolites were identified by searching the Human Metabolome Database, METLIN database, and an in-house database.</p>
<p id="p00110">Missing values were imputed as one-tenth of the minimum detected amount of the relevant metabolite. Normalization involved dividing the peak area of each metabolite by the peak area of the total metabolites in a sample, then multiplying by 10 000. Unit variance scaling was used before partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) by SIMCA-P 14.1 (Umetrics, ‌Umea, Sweden). MetaboAnalyst 5.0 facilitated metabolic pathway analysis.</p></section></section><section id="s3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. RESULTS</h2>

<p id="p00115">UHPLC-MS/MS was employed to identify the active ingredients in SJGB decoction. The identified top 30 compounds by relative abundance in the decoction are listed in <a href="#T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>. According to the UHPLC-MS/MS analysis results, the five most abundant ingredients were (S)-Actinidine, Ephedrine, Pyroglutamic acid, Methy-lephedrine, and Coumarin.</p>

<div class="tw-foot p"><div class="fn" id="nt1"><p id="p00130">Notes: SJGB: Shenji Guben; RT: retention time.</p></div></div></section></section><section id="s3.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Effects of SJGB decoction in COPD model mice</h3>
<section id="s3.2.1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2.1. Body weight</h4>
<p id="p00135">In this study, the weight of the control mice steadily increased to (31.8 ± 1.0) g. In the first 12 weeks, the mice showed slow weight gain aside from the control mice. The weight of the mice significantly decreased one week after each LPS nasal drop and recovered in two weeks. A slight weight loss was observed one week after acetylcholine-induced lung function test at week 12. From weeks 13-16, all SJGB decoction-treated groups, except the low-dose group, outgained the model group in weight, showing a dose-dependent manner (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2A</a>). The final body weight of mice in the high-dose and medium-dose SJGB decoction groups was remarkably higher than that in the model group (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2B</a>).</p>

Lung function</h4>
<p id="p00145">At week 12, the model mice showed an increase in Penh values compared to the control mice, aligning with the COPD model profile (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2C</a>). Lung function assessments before tissue collection revealed that the Penh values in model mice were significantly elevated, while SJGB decoction treatment resulted in a dose-dependent reduction of Penh values compared to the model group (except for the low-dose group). Furthermore, the high-dose SJGB decoction group showed a significant reduction in Penh values compared to the model group (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.001) (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2D</a>).</p></section><section id="s3.2.3"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2.3. Lung tissue pathology</h4>
<p id="p00150">Mice in the control group mice presented normal bronchial lumina, intact mucosa, neatly arranged cilia, symmetrical alveoli of normal size and structure, complete alveolar septa, and minimal inflammatory cells.</p>
<p id="p00155">Compared to those in the control group, mice the model group showed narrowed bronchial lumina, mucosal epithelial detachment, disordered and collapsed cilia, alveolar wall thinning, alveolar space fusion and enlargement, and significant inflammatory cell infiltration in the bronchial lumina, vascular walls, and lung interstitium, accompanied by abundant desquamated necrotic epithelial cells and inflammatory exudates (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3A</a>). Relative to the model group, the middle-dose and high-dose SJGB decoction groups exhibited significant pathological improvements in lung tissues, with reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and decreased peribronchial inflammation score (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3B</a>). Additionally, in comparison to those in the control group, improved alveolar fusion, an increased number of alveoli per field of view, thinner alveolar septa, and a significant reduction in the MLI were observed in the SJGB-m and SJGB-h groups (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3C</a>).</p>

DISCUSSION</h2>
<p id="p00195">COPD is a common respiratory condition characterized by progressive airflow limitation. Currently, the treatment options for COPD can manage disease severe exacerbation, primarily including bronchodilators, corticosteroids, and phosphodiesterase 4 inhibitors.<sup><a href="#b17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b17">17</a></sup> However, these treatments can also trigger significant side effects and drug tolerance, highlighting the critical need to develop novel therapeutic approaches. Against this backdrop, TCM or a combination of TCM and Western medicine holds great promise for alleviating symptoms, reducing acute exacerbation frequency, enhancing exercise capacity, and improving the quality of life of COPD patients.<sup><a href="#b13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b13">13</a></sup></p>
<p id="p00200">The SJGB formula, meticulously developed by Professor CAO Shihong based on the principle of "nourishing the kidney and solidifying the foundation" and years of clinical experience, has shown favorable efficacy in strengthening the spleen, augmenting<em> Qi</em>, enriching the kidney, replenishing essence, and consolidating the body's foundation. The results of this study indicated that the SJGB formula increased the body weight, effectively reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in the airways and lungs, and improved the alveolar fusion and lung function of the COPD model mice.</p>
<p id="p00205">Metabolomics focuses on reflecting the intrinsic mechanisms of diseases or drugs by detecting changes in endogenous components of organisms under external stimuli.<sup><a href="#b18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b18">18</a>,<a href="#b19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b19">19</a></sup> In our study, the administration of the TCM formula SJGB decoction in a murine model of COPD yielded notable alterations in the metabolomics of lung tissues. Metabolomics results showed that glycerophospholipid metabolism and riboflavin metabolism were the two pathways most relevant to the mechanisms of SJGB formula in treating COPD. Specifically, a decrease in phosphatidylcholine (PC) levels and an increase in riboflavin levels in SJGB decoction-treated mice were observed compared to the model mice. Interestingly, in a previous study by Li <em>et al</em>
<sup><a href="#b20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b20">20</a></sup> on the differentiation in TCM patterns of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease through comprehensive metabolomic and lipidomic characterization, compared to healthy subjects, patients with lung<em> Qi </em>deficiency and lung-kidney<em> Qi </em>deficiency exhibit higher levels of PC. In contrast, our findings showed that after treatment with SJGB formula, PC levels in mouse lung tissue were decreased. Given the significant role of PC in surfactant composition, reduced PC levels after treatment might indicate a change in surfactant metabolism.<sup><a href="#b21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b21">21</a></sup> This discrepancy may highlight the complexity of metabolite alterations in different COPD phenotypes and warrant further investigation. Riboflavin, or vitamin B2, plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular redox balance by participating in the electron transport chain and functioning as a precursor for the metabolic coenzymes flavin adenine dinucleotide and flavin mononucleotide, which are vital for the activity of glutathione reductase, an enzyme crucial for glutathione regeneration and, consequently, antioxidant defense.<sup><a href="#b22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b22">22</a></sup> Given that COPD pathophysiology is primarily driven by oxidative stress and inflammation, the elevation of riboflavin may be a mechanistic pathway for SJGB decoction to exert its protective effects.<sup><a href="#b23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b23">23</a></sup></p>
<p id="p00210">In our study, elevated PGE2 and Prostaglandin D2 levels were observed in the COPD model, consistent with the inflammatory nature of COPD.<sup><a href="#b24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b24">24</a></sup> Although the administration of SJGB decoction did not cause statistically significant differences in the levels of these prostaglandins compared to the model group, there was a decreasing trend. This decreasing trend, coupled with histopathological evidence suggesting that SJGB treatment alleviated inflammatory responses, provides valuable insights into the potential mechanisms by which SJGB decoction plays a therapeutic role in COPD. Additionally, it was noteworthy that SJGB decoction treatment significantly upregulated citrulline in the COPD model group, particularly considering the downregulation of citrulline in the COPD model group. A previous study has found that macrophage citrulline levels are rapidly decreased following stimulation with interferon-gamma and/or LPS, which is an essential process for efficient pro-inflammatory signaling activation. Conversely, increased citrulline can directly bind to Janus kinase 2 (JAK2), inhibiting the JAK2-signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 signaling pathway.<sup><a href="#b25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b25">25</a></sup> Citrulline also performs crucial functions in the production of nitric oxide (NO), a molecule vital for maintaining vascular tone, reducing inflammation, and ensuring efficient blood flow within the pulmonary circulation.<sup><a href="#b26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b26">26</a></sup> In the context of COPD, a decrease in citrulline levels may impair NO synthesis, thereby exacerbating pulmonary hypertension, vascular remodeling, airway constriction, and inflammatory responses.<sup><a href="#b27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b27">27</a>,<a href="#b28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b28">28</a></sup> SJGB decoction may reduce pulmonary hypertension and mitigate inflammation by enhancing citrulline availability.</p>
<p id="p00215">Nevertheless, this study also has several limitations. Firstly, unlike targeted metabolomics, non-targeted approaches do not use reference standards. Instead, they depend on database matching, which can result in false positives. Further targeted metabolomics or quantitative experiments are warranted for validation. Although more than 450 metabolites were covered in this study, many significant metabolites are still undetected, affecting the analysis of metabolic pathways. Secondly, the COPD modeling methods employed in this study (including LPS-induced and passive smoking methods) may not fully reflect the complexity of human COPD, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the inherent limitations of these modeling methods related to TCM syndrome classification may influence the interpretation of SJGB decoction's therapeutic effects. Furthermore, while our results are encouraging, further rigorous clinical trials are needed to validate the efficacy and safety of SJGB decoction in COPD patients.</p>
<p id="p00220">To conclude, our data evaluated the protective effects of SJGB decoction against COPD in LPS and passive smoking-induced mouse models. Metabolomics analysis results indicated that SJGB decoction may exert its effects<em> via </em>modulation of glycerophospholipid and riboflavin metabolisms. Moreover, SJGB decoction can decrease inflammatory mediators (such as prostaglandins) while increasing DL-citrulline. These findings provide novel insights into the potential mechanisms of SJGB decoction in COPD treatment.</p></section><section id="s5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">5. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS</h2>
