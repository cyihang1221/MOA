</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>ABSTRACT</h2>
<p>Basal macroautophagy/autophagy has recently been found in anucleate platelets. Platelet autophagy is involved in platelet activation and thrombus formation. However, the mechanism underlying autophagy in anucleate platelets require further clarification. Our data revealed that LC3-II formation and SQSTM1/p62 degradation were noted in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-activated human platelets, which could be blocked by 3-methyladenine and bafilomycin A<sub>1</sub>, indicating that platelet activation may cause platelet autophagy. AMPK phosphorylation and MTOR dephosphorylation were also detected, and block of AMPK activity by the AMPK inhibitor dorsomorphin reversed SQSTM1 degradation and LC3-II formation. Moreover, autophagosome formation was observed through transmission electron microscopy and deconvolution microscopy. These findings suggest that platelet autophagy was induced partly through the AMPK-MTOR pathway. In addition, increased LC3-II expression occurred only in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-treated <em>Atg5</em><sup>f/f</sup> platelets, but not in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-treated <em>atg5</em><sup>−/−</sup> platelets, suggesting that platelet autophagy occurs during platelet activation. <em>atg5</em><sup>−/−</sup> platelets also exhibited a lower aggregation in response to agonists, and platelet-specific <em>atg5</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice exhibited delayed thrombus formation in mesenteric microvessles and decreased mortality rate due to pulmonary thrombosis. Notably, metabolic analysis revealed that sphingolipid metabolism is involved in platelet activation, as evidenced by observed several altered metabolites, which could be reversed by dorsomorphin. Therefore, platelet autophagy and platelet activation are positively correlated, partly through the interconnected network of sphingolipid metabolism. In conclusion, this study for the first time demonstrated that AMPK-MTOR signaling could regulate platelet autophagy. A novel linkage between AMPK-MTOR and sphingolipid metabolism in anucleate platelet autophagy was also identified: platelet autophagy and platelet activation are positively correlated.</p>

<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>KEYWORDS:</strong> AMPK, autophagy, hydrogen peroxide, platelets, sphingolipid metabolism</p></section></section><section id="s0001"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Platelets are the flowing first responders to a blood vessel injury and they assist in hemostasis. Compromised platelet function can induce bleeding diatheses. Moreover, incongruous platelet activation can lead to thrombosis that causes acute vascular obstruction, triggering strokes, heart attacks, and other ischemic conditions [<a href="#cit0001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0001">1</a>,<a href="#cit0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0002">2</a>]. Platelets are produced from megakaryocytes under shear conditions as anucleate cellular fragments and remain in circulation for approximately 7 to 10 days in humans [<a href="#cit0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0003">3</a>,<a href="#cit0004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0004">4</a>]. Because their function declines over time, mature platelets are removed by the liver and spleen [<a href="#cit0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0005">5</a>].</p>
<p>Autophagy, the cellular process that facilitates lysosomal degradation of long-lived cytoplasmic proteins, is initiated during passage of differentiation, nutrient starvation, or cellular stress, including oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and the gathering of protein aggregates [<a href="#cit0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0006">6</a>,<a href="#cit0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0007">7</a>]. Autophagy also occurs in anucleate platelets [<a href="#cit0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0008">8–11</a>]. Ouseph et al reported that basally active autophagy continuously proceeded in an unstimulated (resting) condition [<a href="#cit0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0009">9</a>]. Nutrient deprivation induces MAP1LC3/LC3 (microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3)-II formation, SQSTM1/p62 (sequestosome 1) degradation, and double-membrane vesicle formation in platelets [<a href="#cit0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0008">8</a>]. By contrast, the LC3-II level can decrease by 1.5 to 2.5-fold in response to platelet agonists [<a href="#cit0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0009">9</a>]. This may be attributed to more rapid autolysosomal proteolysis, which could lead to a higher turnover of LC3-II in agonist-induced platelets than in starved platelets. In addition, platelet autophagy is involved in platelet activation [<a href="#cit0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0008">8</a>,<a href="#cit0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0009">9</a>]. Exposure to the pharmacological autophagy inhibitors 3-methyladenine (3-MA) and bafilomycin A<sub>1</sub> (Baf-A1) and the deletion of genes (such as the platelet-specific <em>atg5</em><sup>−/−</sup> and <em>atg7</em><sup>−/−</sup> or <em>Becn</em><sup>+/−</sup>) can impair platelet aggregation, hemostasis, and thrombosis [<a href="#cit0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0008">8</a>,<a href="#cit0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0009">9</a>,<a href="#cit0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0011">11</a>], suggesting that basal autophagy is essential to platelet function. By contrast, a substantial mitophagy induction (above the basal autophagy level) in diabetic platelets has been reported to protect against severe oxidative stress [<a href="#cit0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0010">10</a>]. Furthermore, hypoxic preconditioning has been reported to induce platelet mitophagy, which could reduce ischemia/reperfusion-induced heart injury [<a href="#cit0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0011">11</a>]. These findings indicate that platelet mitophagy may protect platelets from oxidative stress–induced damage, which can prevent platelet activation and thrombosis. Taken together, these findings suggest that basal platelet autophagy supports platelet activation and platelet mitophagy prevents pathological platelet activation. Notably, PIK3C3/VPS34 (phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase catalytic subunit type 3), a class-III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase, has been reported to catalyze phosphatidylinositol phosphorylation to form phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PtdIns3P). The PtdIns3P on autophagic membranes is essential for the expansion of phagophores and completion of autophagosomes [<a href="#cit0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0012">12</a>]. However, Liu et al reported that platelets that underwent deletion of <em>Pik3c3</em> exhibits impaired platelet activation, but the absence of PIK3C3 does not significantly affect the agonist-induced reduction of the LC3-II level, suggesting that the inhibition of platelet function caused by <em>pik3c3</em> deficiency may not be autophagy dependent [<a href="#cit0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0013">13</a>]. PIK3C3 was also reported to play a crucial role in platelet autophagy in patients with immune thrombocytopenia purpura/ITP, and enhancing platelet autophagy might alleviate platelet destruction by improving platelet viability [<a href="#cit0014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0014">14</a>]. Based on these findings, the relationship between platelet activation and platelet autophagy should be clarified.</p>
<p>Reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydrogen peroxide (H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>), can induce autophagy in various cell types [<a href="#cit0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0015">15–17</a>]. In platelets, H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> can promote platelet activity and participate in platelet activation [<a href="#cit0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0018">18</a>,<a href="#cit0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0019">19</a>]. H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> can increase by as much as 50 to 100 µM in certain disease states or during chronic inflammation [<a href="#cit0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0020">20–22</a>]. Although relative high concentration of H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (1 mM) was used to study platelet autophagy [<a href="#cit0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0010">10</a>], it may be detrimental to platelets. Therefore, a series of experiments were performed to determine the role of platelet autophagy, which potentially plays a role in the treatment of occlusive thrombotic diseases.</p></section><section id="s0002"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="s0002-s2001"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Autophagy induction in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-activated human platelets</h3>
<p>Before this study, we first observed its effect on platelet function. The data revealed that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> at 1 mM, but not 100 µM, led to completely impaired platelet aggregation in response to collagen (data not shown), suggesting that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> at high concentration (1 mM) may cause platelet damage. Therefore, 50 and 100 µM H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> was used to investigate the relationship between platelet activation and autophagy in the current study. As shown in <a href="#f0001" class="usa-link">Figure 1A</a>, the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images indicated apparent morphological changes in platelets with H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (100 µM) treatment, revealing that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> converts discoid platelets to rounded cells with extended pseudopods and irregular shape. This indicated that the concentration (100 µM) was sufficient to trigger platelet activation. Moreover, LC3-I to LC3-II conversion was observed in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-activated platelets in a concentration-dependent manner (50–100 µM; <a href="#f0001" class="usa-link">Figure 1B</a>). SQSTM1 degradation was also observed in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-activated platelets at 100 µM (<a href="#f0001" class="usa-link">Figure 1C</a>), suggesting that platelet autophagy occurred in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-activated human platelets. Thus, H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> at 100 µM was used as an agonist for platelet autophagy in subsequent experiments. In addition, H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-induced LC3-I to LC3-II conversion and SQSTM1 degradation occurred in a time-dependent manner, in which the effect was the maximum from 60 to 120 min (<a href="#f0001" class="usa-link">Figure 1D</a>). The accumulation of LC3-II and SQSTM1 was observed after treatment with Baf-A1 (<a href="#f0001" class="usa-link">Figure 1B-C</a>), which blocks the autophagic flux by inhibiting autolysosome acidification and autophagosome–lysosome fusion <strong>[</strong><a href="#cit0023" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0023">23</a>,<a href="#cit0024" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0024">24</a><strong>]</strong>, suggesting that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> stimulation increases the autophagic flux. In addition, 3-MA, the class-III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase inhibitor that interferes with the early steps of autophagy, could reverse LC3-II formation and SQSTM1 degradation (<a href="#f0001" class="usa-link">Figure 1B-C</a>).</p>

<p>The formation of double-membrane autophagosomes is a critical characteristic of autophagy <strong>[</strong><a href="#cit0023" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0023">23</a>,<a href="#cit0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0025">25</a><strong>]</strong>. In this study, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed the presence of double-membrane autophagosomes (arrowhead) in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-activated platelets but not in the resting control (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2A, I</a> and ii). Moreover, the process of degradation of mitochondria by autolysosomes (mitophagy) was observed in activated platelets (arrows; <a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2A</a>, iii and iv). In addition, 3-dimensional autolysosomes (autophagosome-lysosome fusion) were clearly observed through deconvolution microscopy (3-dimensional; Video S1). LC3 (green fluorescence) and lysosome (red fluorescence) were labeled with anti-LC3 and anti-LAMP1 (lysosomal associated membrane protein 1) antibodies, respectively. Fluorescence images were captured from 2-dimensional (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2B</a>), z-axis (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2C</a>), and varying 3-dimensional angles (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2D</a>). LC3 and lysosome colocalization could also be found in activated platelets (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2B</a>, arrowhead). Moreover, z-section images revealed that the colocalization of LC3 and lysosomes only occurred between sections 4 and 6 but not above or below these sections (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2C</a>, white arrowheads), indicating that green and red signal colocalization was a specific event. The 3-dimensional reconstructed video, with rotation at different angles, also displayed that both red and green signals colocalized at the same spatial location, further supporting that the colocalization event was not a coincidence (Video S1). These data clearly demonstrated autophagy induction in activated platelets.</p>

<p>Previously, glucose deprivation has been reported to lead to ROS accumulation, which in turn activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) [<a href="#cit0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0026">26–28</a>]. AMPK can activate ULK1 (unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1), which plays a critical role in autophagy initiation, by directly phosphorylating ULK1 at Ser317 and Ser777 and by indirectly inactivating MTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase), which results in the dephosphorylation of ULK1 at Ser757 [<a href="#cit0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0028">28</a>]. Moreover, in response to cellular stress, the phosphorylation of MTOR at Ser2448 is reduced and the brake imposed on autophagy is released [<a href="#cit0029" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0029">29</a>]. Thus, the role of AMPK-MTOR in regulating platelet autophagy in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-stimulated human platelets was investigated next. AMPK phosphorylation at PRKAA Thr172 and ULK1 phosphorylation at Ser317 occurred at 60 min in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-treated human platelets, respectively; a reduction in MTOR phosphorylation at Ser2448 occurred at 30 and 60 min (<a href="#f0003" class="usa-link">Figure 3A</a>). H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (50–100 µM) significantly induced AMPK and ULK1 phosphorylation and attenuated MTOR phosphorylation which could be reversed by the AMPK inhibitor compound C (<a href="#f0003" class="usa-link">Figure 3B-D</a>). Compound C also prevented LC3 accumulation and SQSTM1 degradation in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-stimulated human platelets (<a href="#f0003" class="usa-link">Figure 3E</a>). Another, other phospho-sites (Ser777 and Ser757) of ULK were detected. The data revealed that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> could induce the phosphorylation and dephosphorylation of ULK1 at Ser777 and Ser757, respectively, which could be reversed by compound C, suggesting that the activation of ULK1 was regulated by AMPK and MTOR in response to H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> in platelets (Fig. S1). Taken together, these findings suggest that the AMPK-MTOR pathway is crucial in platelet autophagy.</p>

<p>Two types of mouse thrombotic model were used to determine the role of platelet autophagy in thrombotic events. In the mouse model of fluorescein sodium-induced platelet thrombus formation in the mesenteric microvessels, vascular occlusion (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Figure 5B</a>, left panel, arrow) occurred at approximately 102.1 ± 3.7 s (<em>n</em> = 8) in <em>Atg5</em><sup>f/f</sup> mice, but vascular occlusion did not occur in platelet-specific <em>atg5</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice in the same time frame (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Figure 5B</a>, left panel). The occlusion time in platelet-specific <em>atg5</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice was approximately 151.7 s ± 6.1 s (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Figure 5B</a>, right panel, <em>n</em> = 8). In the mouse model of adenosine diphosphate (ADP)-induced pulmonary thrombosis, acute pulmonary thromboembolic death could be prevented in platelet-specific <em>atg5</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice, which displayed an increased survival rate of 25%, compared with platelet-specific <em>Atg5</em><sup>f/f</sup> mice (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Figure 5C</a>, <em>N</em> = 12). Therefore, impaired platelet autophagy may cause defective thrombus formation. Moreover, the effect of platelet autophagy on hemostasis was determined using the tail bleeding time assay. The data revealed that the bleeding time of <em>Atg5</em><sup>f/f</sup> mice was approximately 62.2 ± 4.0 s (<em>n</em> = 15). No significant difference was observed between <em>Atg5</em><sup>f/f</sup> and platelet-specific <em>atg5</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Figure 5D</a>), implying that platelet autophagy mainly regulates thrombosis, but not hemostasis. Taken together, these findings suggest that platelet autophagy is involved in platelet activation and thrombosis without affecting hemostasis.</p></section><section id="s0002-s2006"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Novel linkage of AMPK-MTOR to sphingolipid metabolism in platelet autophagy</h3>
<p>Autophagy can regulate cellular metabolic capability [<a href="#cit0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0032">32–34</a>]. However, whether autophagy-mediated metabolism regulates platelet function remains unclear. Hence, in this study, we determined metabolic changes between resting and H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-treated human platelets by performing a metabolic analysis (<em>n</em> = 7). Metabolites with significant changes (<em>P &lt;</em> 0.05 and fold change &gt; 1.5) were selected and subjected to pathway analysis. A partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) score plot revealed that the clustering significantly differed between control (resting) and H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-treated groups, suggesting that metabolic changes occurred in activated platelets (<a href="#f0006" class="usa-link">Figure 6A</a>). The heat map (top 50) also presented differential patterns in the control and H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-treated groups (<a href="#f0006" class="usa-link">Figure 6B</a>). These metabolites were subjected to pathway analysis by using MetaboAnalyst and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) metabolic pathways database. Human metabolome database (HMDB) numbers were subjected to MetaboAnalyst for a pathway analysis. This analysis revealed the top-5 pathways ranked by their p value (<a href="#f0006" class="usa-link">Figure 6C</a>) and the percentage of metabolite hits (<a href="#f0006" class="usa-link">Figure 6D</a>): (1) sphingolipid metabolism, (2) glycerophospholipid metabolism, (3) glycerolipid metabolism, (4) linoleic acid metabolism, and (5) primary bile acid biosynthesis pathways. Among these pathways, sphingolipid metabolism had the highest score. The identified metabolites were imported into the KEGG database to identify their potential interactions. The data also revealed that one of the active networks was sphingolipid metabolism in which several metabolic changes, including an increase in sphingomyelin (SM), ceramide, phosphatidylcholine (PC), diacylglycerol (DAG), galactosylceramide, sulfatide, glucosylceramide, and lactosylceramide levels, were found in the H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-treated group (<a href="#f0006" class="usa-link">Figure 6E</a>). Moreover, the increase in the SM level in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-treated platelets could be prevented by the AMPK inhibitor compound C, as evidenced by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis (<a href="#f0007" class="usa-link">Figure 7A-D</a>). In addition, sphingomyelin (SM 16:0 and SM 18:0) could increase AMPK phosphorylation (Fig. S3A). Moreover, the SMPD3 (sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase 3) has been reported to be activated by H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> or by nutrient starvation, and in turn to induce autophagy [<a href="#cit0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0035">35–37</a>]. Thus, here, the SMPD3 inhibitor GW4869 was used to determine the role sphingolipid metabolism on autophagy in platelets. The data revealed that GW4869 inhibited H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-induced AMPK phosphorylation (Fig. S3B). Collectively, these findings strongly indicate a novel linkage of AMPK-MTOR to sphingolipid metabolism in anucleate platelet autophagy.</p>

<p>Platelets contain the autophagy apparatus, and a basal autophagy in resting platelets is required for platelet activation [<a href="#cit0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0008">8</a>,<a href="#cit0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0009">9</a>]. Notably, starvation-mediated autophagy and agonist-mediated autophagy seem to play different roles in platelets. A study reported that the inhibition of platelet activation by prostaglandin E1 did not affect starvation-mediated autophagy, indicating that this autophagy type is independent of platelet activation [<a href="#cit0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0008">8</a>]. By contrast, several inhibitors of platelet activation have been shown to block agonist-mediated autophagy, suggesting that platelet activation signaling cascades, including PLC (phospholipase C) and PRKC (protein kinase C), are required for agonist-mediated autophagy [<a href="#cit0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0009">9</a>]. The present study also revealed that platelet activation can induce platelet autophagy. These observations indicate the complex regulation of autophagy in platelets. However, Ouseph et al reported that SQSTM1 was not affected during agonist-induced platelet autophagy and in <em>atg7</em>-deficient platelets, suggesting that alternative receptors other than SQSTM1 are utilized in agonist-induced platelet autophagy [<a href="#cit0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0009">9</a>]. By contrast, our study revealed that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> could induce platelet autophagy (LC3-II formation) accompanied with SQSTM1 degradation. This suggests that SQSTM1 is used as a receptor in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-mediated platelet autophagy in contrast to autophagy induced by platelet agonists, or that the level of platelet activation induced by H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> is weaker than that induced by other agonists; thus, SQSTM1 turnover could be observed in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-treated platelets. Similarly, in this study, LC3-II accumulation was found in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-treated platelets, whereas LC3-II loss was noted in agonist-treated platelets. These diverse results may be attributed to the rapid autophagic flux in agonist-treated platelets [<a href="#cit0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0009">9</a>]. By contrast, in diabetic platelets or under the hypoxic preconditioning condition, a substantial mitophagy has been reported to be induced to protect against severe oxidative stress and ischemia/reperfusion-induced heart injury [<a href="#cit0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0010">10</a>,<a href="#cit0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0011">11</a>]. These findings demonstrate that platelet mitophagy might serve as a protective mechanism under pathological conditions [<a href="#cit0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0010">10</a>,<a href="#cit0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0011">11</a>]. Thus, evidence suggests that platelet autophagy plays a critical role in protective mechanisms against oxidative stress and in physiological function, supporting platelet activation. Actually, we also found that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> might induce mitopahgy in platelets (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2A</a>, iii and iv). Moreover, H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> led to mitochondrial depolarization, the decrease of ATP content, and the loss of mitochondrial mass in platelets (Figs. S4 and S5). In addition, we also found that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> could cause the phosphorylation of ULK1 at Ser555 (Fig. S5C), which has been reported to translocate to mitochondria and mediate mitophagy [<a href="#cit0027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0027">27</a>,<a href="#cit0038" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0038">38</a>,<a href="#cit0039" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0039">39</a>]. Collectively, these findings evidently support that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> might concomitantly induce mitochondrial damage and platelet mitophagy, which themselves are responsible for pathological and physiological platelet activation, respectively [<a href="#cit0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0010">10</a>]. However, the detailed regulatory mechanisms of mitophagy in platelets need to be further clarified.</p>
<p>The relationship between platelet autophagy and platelet activation remains unclear. Our data revealed that AMPK-MTOR was involved in platelet autophagy in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-activated human platelets. Wang et al. also reported that ROS promotes human platelet activation by upregulating autophagy through the PI3K-AKT-MTOR pathway [<a href="#cit0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0040">40</a>]. They observed that the MTOR inhibitor rapamycin could enhance oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL)-mediated platelet aggregates and LC3-II formation. ox-LDL also reduced AKT and MTOR phosphorylation in human platelets [<a href="#cit0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0040">40</a>]. These observations suggest that MTOR inhibition activates platelet autophagy and promotes platelet activation under oxidative stress. By contrast, Aslan et al reported that the pharmacological inhibition of MTOR blocked RAC1 activation and subsequent platelet spreading on fibrinogen and considerably prevented collagen-induced platelet aggregation and platelet aggregate formation under the shear stress condition [<a href="#cit0041" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0041">41</a>]. However, the concentration of rapamycin (1 µM) used was relatively high for human platelets [<a href="#cit0041" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0041">41</a>]. We confirmed that rapamycin at a concentration of 1 µM, but not 200 nM, could inhibit collagen-induced human platelet aggregation (data not shown). Moreover, Liu et al reported that rapamycin (200 nM) did not affect mouse platelet aggregation [<a href="#cit0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0013">13</a>]. These observations possibly relate to the off-target effect of rapamycin at a high concentration of 1 µM on the inhibition of platelet activation. However, Yang et al reported that MTORC1 promoted aging-related venous thrombosis through the elevation of the MPV and through platelet activation [<a href="#cit0042" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0042">42</a>]. They reported that 2-month intragastric administration of rapamycin or deletion of Raptor could reduce mouse platelet aggregation and venous thrombosis. These findings suggest that MTOR activity is positively correlated with platelet activation in aging mouse platelets. They also reported that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (50 µM) induced the phosphorylation of RPS6KB/p70S6K (ribosomal protein S6 kinase B) and RPS6 (a downstream readout of MTORC1) in platelets from 4- and 16-month-old mice; thus, they proposed that ROS contribute to MTORC1-dependent platelet activation during aging [<a href="#cit0042" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0042">42</a>]. However, the present study revealed that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (50 and 100 µM) could reduce MTOR phosphorylation and promote autophagy and activation in human platelets. These inconsistent findings thus warrant further research.</p>
<p>PRKAA2/AMPKα2 may be involved in platelet signaling and thrombus stability [<a href="#cit0043" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0043">43</a>]. Moreover, the AMPK inhibitor compound C (10 μM) significantly attenuated platelet aggregation induced by thrombin [<a href="#cit0043" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0043">43</a>]. Platelets from <em>prkaa1</em> knockout mice also exhibited decreased thrombin-induced aggregation, and PRKAA1/AMPKα1 was activated in platelets on thrombin or collagen stimulation [<a href="#cit0044" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0044">44</a>,<a href="#cit0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0045">45</a>]. In the present study, AMPK activation and LC3-II formation were found in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-activated human platelets, which were significantly reversed by compound C. Therefore, AMPK may be involved in both platelet activation and platelet autophagy. By contrast, Liu et al reported that AMPK mediates the antiplatelet effects of thiazolidinediones, a class of antidiabetic drugs [<a href="#cit0046" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0046">46</a>]. They showed that compound C reduced rosiglitazone-attenuated platelet aggregation induced by collagen, but compound C alone did not affect collagen-induced platelet aggregation [<a href="#cit0046" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0046">46</a>]. However, similar to our results, Randriamboavonjy et al reported that compound C alone could inhibit platelet aggregation. This discrepancy requires further clarification [<a href="#cit0043" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0043">43</a>].</p>
<p>Notably, our study is the first to use metabolic analysis to investigate the relationship between platelet autophagy and metabolism. In H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-activated human platelets, the levels of several metabolites considerably increased, including sphingomyelin (SM), ceramide, phosphatidylcholine (PC), diacylglycerol (DAG), galactosylceramide/sulfatide, and glucosylceramide/lactosylceramide. Sphingomyelin, galactosylceramide/sulfatide, and glucosylceramide/lactosylceramide are originated from ceramide through conversion by SGMS (sphingomyelin synthase) [<a href="#cit0047" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0047">47</a>,<a href="#cit0048" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0048">48</a>], UGT8/CGT (UDP glycosyltransferase 8) [<a href="#cit0049" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0049">49–51</a>], and UGCG/GCS (UDP-glucose ceramide glucosyltransferase)-B4GALT/LacCS (beta-1,4-galactosyltransferase) [<a href="#cit0052" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0052">52–54</a>], respectively, By contrast, sphingomyelin (SM), galactosylceramide, and glucosylceramide can be converted into ceramide, increasing ceramide levels, through SMPD/SMase (sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase), GALC/GCDase (galactosylceramidase), and GBA/GluSDase (glucosylceramidase beta), respectively [<a href="#cit0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0055">55</a>]. Ceramide has been reported to be involved in autophagy [<a href="#cit0056" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0056">56</a>,<a href="#cit0057" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0057">57</a>], and our previous study demonstrated that ceramide participates in F2RL3/protease-activated receptor 4–mediated platelet activation [<a href="#cit0058" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0058">58</a>]. Moreover, sulfatides have been reported to serve as ligands of SELP, and they are involved in the activation of platelets and the formation of stable platelet aggregates [<a href="#cit0059" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0059">59</a>,<a href="#cit0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0060">60</a>]. Glucosylceramide was also reported to contribute to atherosclerotic plaque inflammation [<a href="#cit0061" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0061">61</a>]. The present data revealed an increase in the DAG level on H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> treatment. It can lead to PRKC activation in various cell types [<a href="#cit0047" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0047">47</a>,<a href="#cit0048" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0048">48</a>]. These observations suggest that sphingolipid metabolism is involved in platelet autophagy, platelet activation, and thrombus formation. Thus, the metabolic analysis in this study provides insights into the possible mechanism through which sphingolipid metabolism may regulate platelet autophagy. Moreover, in this study, compound C significantly reduced the SM level. SM (16:0 and 18:0) could induce AMPK phosphorylation. Moreover, the SMPD3 inhibitor GW4869 inhibited H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-induced AMPK phosphorylation, suggesting that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-induced platelet autophagy is partly mediated by SMPD3. Previous studies have also reported that H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> can activate SMPD3, which induces autophagy during nutrient starvation [<a href="#cit0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0035">35–37</a>]. Therefore, these findings support that sphingolipid metabolism is involved in H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>-induced autophagy activation and that sphingolipid metabolism may provide a linkage between platelet activation and platelet autophagy.</p>
<p>In conclusion, our findings indicate that platelet activation could lead to platelet autophagy through the AMPK-MTOR pathway (<a href="#f0008" class="usa-link">Figure 8</a>). We also confirmed the positive correlation between platelet autophagy and platelet activation and thrombus formation. Moreover, the most crucial finding of this study is the discovery of a novel linkage of AMPK-MTOR to sphingolipid metabolism in anucleate platelet autophagy.</p>

<section id="s0004-s2001"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Chemicals and reagents</h3>
<p>H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (31642), luciferase/luciferin (FLE250), fluorescein sodium (F6377), ADP (A2754), and anti-LC3B polyclonal antibodies (pAbs) (L7543) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. 3-MA (13242) and Baf-A1 (11038) were purchased from Cayman Chemical. Anti-ULK1 (ab128859), anti-ATG5/APG5L (ab108327), anti-PRKAA1/AMPK alpha 1 (ab32047), and anti-SQSTM1/p62 (ab109012) mAbs and Sphingomyelin Assay Kit (ab138877) were purchased from Abcam. The horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) (111–035-003) and anti-mouse IgG (115–035-003) were purchased from Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories. CF<sup>TM</sup> 405 M-conjugated goat anti-mouse (20180) and CF<sup>TM</sup> 488A-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary Abs (20012) were purchased from Biotium.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2002"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Human platelet preparation</h3>
<p>This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shin Kong Wu Ho-Su Memorial Hospital (Approval No. 20151203 R) and conformed to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All volunteers provided informed consent. Human platelet suspensions were prepared as described previously [<a href="#cit0062" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0062">62</a>]. In brief, blood was drawn from healthy volunteers who had taken no medication during the preceding 2 weeks and then mixed with an acid-citrate-dextrose (A.C.D.; 56 mM sodium citrate [J.T.Baker, 6132–04-3], 65 mM citric acid [J.T.Baker, 5949–29-1], and 14 mM glucose [Sigma Aldrich, P5767]) solution (9:1, v:v). After centrifugation (120 × g, 10 min), the supernatant (platelet-rich plasma, PRP) was supplemented with prostaglandin E<sub>1</sub> (PGE<sub>1</sub>, 0.5 mM; Sigma Aldrich, P5515) and heparin (6.4 IU/mL; Sigma Aldrich, H3149), and then incubated for 10 min at 37°C. After centrifugation (500 × g for 10 min), the platelet pellets were suspended in Tyrode’s solution (pH 7.3; 137 mM NaCl [Bionovas, AS0480], 2.4 mM KCl [J.T.Baker, 7447–40-7], 1 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub> [Sigma Aldrich, M2393], 0.2 mM Na<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub> [J.T.Baker, 7558–79-4], 12 mM NaHCO<sub>3</sub> [Sigma Aldrich, S5761], 5.5 mM glucose, 3.5 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA) [Bionovas, AA0530]), and then PGE<sub>1</sub> (0.5 µM), and heparin (6.4 IU/ml) were added. The mixture was incubated for 10 min at 37°C. After centrifugation of the suspensions at 500 × g for 10 min, washed platelets were suspended in Tyrode’s solution (containing 1 mM Ca<sup>2+</sup>; J.T.Baker, 10035–04-8).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2003"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunoblotting study</h3>
<p>Washed platelets (1.2 × 10<sup>9</sup>/mL) were preincubated with the inhibitors (3-MA, Baf-A1, or compound C), and H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> was added subsequently for the indicated times (30–120 min). The reaction was stopped, and the platelets were immediately resuspended in 200 μL of a lysis buffer. Lysates were centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min. Samples containing 80 μg of protein were separated through 8% to 14% sodium dodecylsulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis; proteins were electrotransferred through semidry transfer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Blots were blocked with TBST (pH 7.6; 10 mM Tris-base [Bionovas, AT0890], 100 mM NaCl [Bionovas, AS0480], 0.01% Tween 20 [Sigma Aldrich, P1379]) containing 5% BSA for 1 h and then probed with various primary antibodies. The membranes were then incubated with the HRP-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG (diluted 1:3000 in TBST) for 1 h. Immunoreactive bands were detected using the ECL system. The ratios of semiquantitative results obtained by scanning reactive bands and quantifying optical density by using videodensitometry (Bio-Profil; Biolight Windows Application V2000.01, Vilber Lourmat, France).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2004"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Electron microscopy analysis</h3>
<p>Human platelets were poured on microscopy slides and fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 19200) and 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Sigma Aldrich, 1.04239) in 0.1 M cacodylate (Sigma Aldrich, C0250) for 2 h. Samples were washed 3 times with 0.1 M cacodylate buffer. Platelets were postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (Sigma Aldrich, 75633) for 1 h. After being washed with 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, platelets were dehydrated with 70% to 100% alcohol (Supelco, 1.00983). After the platelets were embedded, they were visualized on a scanning electron microscope (S-2400; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) and a transmission electron microscope (HT7700; Hitachi).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2005"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Deconvolution microscopy</h3>
<p>For autophagy image acquisition, cells were imaged with a wide-field DeltaVision deconvolution microscope (GE Healthcare Life Science, Pittsburgh, USA). Both the microscope and camera were controlled by SoftWorX application suite. Platelets (3 × 10<sup>7</sup>/mL), attached to a poly-L-Lysine (Sigma Aldrich, P8920)-coated coverslip, were treated with or without H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (100 µM) for 1 h. After treatment, the platelets were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and washed 3 times with PBS buffer (pH 7.4; 1.37 M NaCl, 27 mM KCl, 18 mM KH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, and 100 mM Na<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub>). The platelets were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma Aldrich, X100) for 10 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. The platelets were labeled with anti-LC3 pAbs or anti-LAMP1 mAbs. Stacks of fluorescence images were deconvolved using SoftWorX (Applied Precision) and analyzed on VoloCITY (PerkinElmer).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2006"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Platelet-specific <em>atg5</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice</h3>
<p>C57BL/6-Tg(Pf4-cre)Q3Rsko/J (JAX 008535) and C57BL/6 mice were obtained from BioLasco (Taipei, Taiwan). B6.129S-Atg5&lt; tm1Myok&gt; (<em>Atg5</em><sup>flox/flox</sup>, RBRC02975) mice were obtained from RIKEN BioResource Center (Ibaraki, Japan). Mice were maintained in the Laboratory Animal Center at Taipei Medical University, Taipei, Taiwan. All procedures were approved by the Affidavit of Approval of Animal Use Protocol-Taipei Medical University (LAC-2016-0276) and -Shin Kong Wu Ho-Su Memorial Hospital (Approval No. Most1060006), and were in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition, 2011). Platelet-specific <em>atg5</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice were generated by crossing <em>Atg5</em><sup>flox/flox</sup> (<em>Atg5</em><sup>f/f</sup>) mice (B6. 129S) with Pf4-Cre mice (C57BL/6) [<a href="#cit0063" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0063">63</a>,<a href="#cit0064" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0064">64</a>]. PCR was employed to genotype the Pf4-Cre mice, and <em>Atg5</em> deficiency in the platelets was further confirmed through western blotting. The following primers were used for PCR identification: Primer 1, 5′-GAA TAT GAA GGC ACA CCC CTG AAA TG-3′; Primer 2, 5′-GTA CTG CAT AAT GGT TTA ACT CTT GC-3′; Primer 3, 5′-ACA ACG TCG AGC ACA GCT GCG CAA GG-3′; Primer 4, 5′-CCC ATA CAG CAC ACC TTT TG-3′; and Primer 5, 5′-TGC ACA GTC AGC AGG TT-3′.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2007"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Mice hematology analysis</h3>
<p>WT, <em>Atg5</em><sup>f/f</sup>, and <em>atg5</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice (25–30 g, 8–10 weeks old) were anesthetized using a mixture containing 75% air and 3% isoflurane (PANION &amp; BF Biotech Inc., Attane<sup>TM</sup>) maintained in 25% oxygen; 200 μL of blood was then drawn through cardiac puncture into heparinized capillary tubes containing ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA, 10 mM; Sigma Aldrich, E5134). Blood parameters were analyzed with an IDEXX Procyte Dx (IDEXX Laboratories; Westbrook, ME, USA).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2008"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Washed mouse platelet preparation and aggregation study</h3>
<p>Mouse blood was collected through cardiac puncture also into a tube containing 100 µL of sodium citrate, followed by gentle mixing. After centrifugation at 180 × g for 5 min, PRP was obtained and mixed with A.C.D. (9:1, v:v). The platelet pellet was obtained after centrifugation at 1300 × g for 15 min, and it was resuspended in Tyrode’s solution. A Lumi-Aggregometer (Payton Associates, Scarborough, ON, Canada) was used to measure platelet aggregation, as described previously [<a href="#cit0062" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0062">62</a>]. Platelet suspensions (3.6 × 10<sup>8</sup>/mL) were stimulated using collagen (1 µg/mL) or α thrombin (0.02 U/mL) for 10 min; the extent of aggregation is expressed in light transmission units.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2009"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Fluorescein sodium-induced platelet thrombus formation in mesenteric microvessels of mice</h3>
<p>Thrombus formation was assessed as described previously [<a href="#cit0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0065">65</a>]. Mice were anesthetized and the external jugular vein was cannulated with a polyethylene-10 tube for intravenously administering the dye. Venules (30–40 mm) were selected for irradiation at a wavelength of &lt;520 nm to produce a microthrombus. After the administration of sodium fluorescein (15 mg/kg), the time required to occlude the microvessel through thrombus formation (occlusion time) was recorded.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2010"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">ADP-induced acute pulmonary thrombosis in mice</h3>
<p>This experiment was performed as described previously [<a href="#cit0066" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0066">66</a>]. In brief, mice were injected with ADP (1.4 g/kg) through the tail vein to induce acute pulmonary thrombosis. In each group, the mouse survival rate was determined at 1 h after injection. Mice that survived were euthanized in a CO<sub>2</sub> chamber.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2011"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Tail bleeding time</h3>
<p>Mice were anesthetized and bleeding was induced by severing the tail 3 mm from the tail tip, and the bleeding tail stump was immersed in saline. Successively, the bleeding time was continuously recorded until no sign of bleeding was observed for at least 10 s.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2012"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolomics</h3>
<section id="s0004-s2012-s3001"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample preparation</h4>
<p>Human platelets were extracted with 100 μL of methanol (Macron chemicals, 67–56-1):H<sub>2</sub>O (Sigma Aldrich, W4502) (7:3, v:v); 2 freeze–thaw steps were applied, and then the samples were vortexed. After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min at 4°C) the supernatant was recovered, dried using a speed vacuum, and reconstituted in 50 μL of acetonitrile/H<sub>2</sub>O (1:1, v:v).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2012-s3002"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Chromatography</h4>
<p>Chromatography analysis was performed on a Waters Acquity UPLC (ultra-performance liquid chromatography) system. A BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters<sup>TM</sup>, 186004801) was used for compound separation. The column temperature was maintained at 45°C and elution was performed at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min; the injection volume was 3.5 μL. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water with 0.1% formic acid (Honeywell, 56302) and (B) acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient profile used for positive ionization detection was as follows: 0 to 0.1 min, 99% B; 0.1 to 7 min, 99% to 30% B; 7 to 7.1 min, 30% to 99% B; 7.1 to 10 min, 99% B. The eluent was directly introduced to the mass spectrometer without a split.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2012-s3003"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Mass spectrometry</h4>
<p>Mass spectrometry was performed on a SYNAPT G2 Q-TOF (Waters MS Technologies, Manchester, UK). The parameters of the mass spectrometer for positive ionization mode detection were as follows: desolvation gas, 900 L/h; desolvation temperature, 550°C; cone gas, 15 L/h; source temperature, 120°C; capillary voltage, 2.8 kV; cone voltage, 40 V and TOF MS scan range, 50 to 1000 <em>m/z</em>. The data acquisition rate was set to 1.2 s, with a 0.02-s interscan delay using Waters MS<sup>E</sup> acquisition mode, with full exact masses collected simultaneously by rapidly alternating between 2 functions. Function 1 acquired data with a low collision energy of 4 and 2 eV for trap and transfer collision cell, respectively, and function 2 acquired data with a transfer collision energy ramp of 15 to 25 eV. All analyses were acquired using the lockspray to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. Leucine-enkephalin (<em>m/z</em> 556.2771) was used as the lockmass at a concentration of 1 ng/μL and flow rate of 5 μL/min. Data were collected in continuum mode, and the lockspray frequency was set at 20 s. All data acquisition was controlled by Waters MassLynx (version 4.1).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2012-s3004"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Determination of sphingomyelin levels by ELISA kit and LC-MS/MS analysis</h4>
<p>For the ELISA assay, the levels of sphingomyelin (SM) were determined by using a Sphingomyelin Assay Kit (Abcam, ab138877). Washed platelets were treated with H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (100 µM) for 1 h in the absence or presence of compound C. After the reactions, the platelets were resuspended in 100 μL of a lysis buffer, and lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min to obtain the supernatant. The levels of SM in supernatants were measured according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. Fluorescence intensity was detected at Ex/Em = 540/590 nm using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).</p>
<p>For the LC-MS/MS analysis, samples were extracted by using a Bligh and Dyer method for lipid extraction with slightly modification [<a href="#cit0067" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0067">67</a>,<a href="#cit0068" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0068">68</a>]. Washed platelets (1 × 10<sup>6</sup>/mL) were treated with H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (100 µM) for 1 h in the absence or presence of compound C. After the reactions, the platelets were resuspended in 80 μL of water, and then extracted with chloroform (Bionovas, AC0750)/methanol mixture (1:2, by volume; 300 μL) containing butylate hydrooxytoluene (BHT, 50 μg/Ml; Sigma Aldrich, B1378) and the internal standards (Ceramide C17; Avanti Polar Lipids, <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/AV860517" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">AV860517</a>). After vortexing for 20 min, chloroform (100 μL) and water (100 μL) containing 0.9% NaCl were added. After mixing thoroughly, the samples were then centrifuged for 5 min at 2000 × g. At the end of the process, the organic (lower) phase was collected and dried under a stream of N<sub>2</sub>. The residues were re-dissolved in chloroform/methanol mixture (1:1, by volume; 200 μL). After mixing, the samples were transferred to glass vials for analyses.</p>
<p>The analyses of sphingomyelin (SM) were carried out on a Waters Acquity UPLC system coupled with a Waters Xevo TQ-XS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an Acquity UPLC CSH C<sub>18</sub> column (130 Å, 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) with an Acquity UPLC CSH C<sub>18</sub> VanGuard pre-column (130 Å, 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 5 mm) eluted at a flow rate of 400 μL/min. The mobile phase consisted of 30 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile/water (60:40, v:v) as solvent A and 30 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in 2-propanol/acetonitrile (90:10, v:v) as solvent B. The gradient profile was as follows: 0 to 1 min, 50% B; 1 to 4.5 min, 50% to 70% B; 4.5 to 8 min, 70% to 100% B; 8 to 8.1 min, 100% to 50% B; 8.1 to 10 min, 50% B. The total run time for analysis was 10 min, and the injection volume was 5 μL.</p>
<p>The mass spectrometer was operated in the positive ESI mode. The parameters were as follows: desolvation gas, 1000 L/h; desolvation temperature, 500°C; cone gas, 150 L/h; source temperature, 150°C; capillary voltage, 2.5 kV; cone voltage, 20 V for SM 16:0 (d18:1/16:0) and SM 18:0 (d18:1/18:0) and 30 V for SM 24:0 (d18:1/24:0). Data were acquired by Waters MassLynx (version 4.2). Precursor-to-product ion transitions of <em>m/z</em> 703.42 → 184.01 for SM 16:0 (d18:1/16:0), of <em>m/z</em> 731.44 to 184.01 for SM18:0 (d18:1/18:0), of <em>m/z</em> 815.53 to 184.01 for SM 24:0 (d18:1/24:0), and of <em>m/z</em> 552.60→264.28 for Ceramide C17 were used for multiple reaction monitoring. Concentrations of the calibration standards, quality controls, and unknowns were evaluated by Waters MassLynx software (version 4.2).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2012-s3005"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Data analysis</h4>
<p>The experimental results are expressed as means ± standard error of the mean (S.E.M.) and are accompanied by the number of observations (n).
