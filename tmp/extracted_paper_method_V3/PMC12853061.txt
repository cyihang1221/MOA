</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ab0005"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>To systematically explore how processing regulates the volatile metabolites and flavor of Hawk Tea (Litsea coreana), this study used non-targeted volatilomics, HS-SPME-GC–MS, and multivariate analysis (including K-means clustering) to analyze four samples with varying fermentation degrees (non-fermented green, slightly fermented white, semi-fermented oolong, deeply fermented black). A total of 379 volatile metabolites were identified, with terpenoids (28.0%) and esters (18.5%) forming the “floral-fruity” aroma foundation. K-means clustering (k = 6) grouped differential volatiles into fermentation-responsive subgroups. Fermentation reduced terpenoids/aldehydes (grassy aroma) and increased esters/heterocyclic compounds (sweet/caramel aroma); semi- and deeply fermented samples had 3.4- and 2.9-fold higher total volatiles than non-fermented ones. The rOAV ≥1 identified 90 key aroma compounds (10 core compounds). Sensory evaluation showed fermentation darkened soup color (yellow-green→orange-red), shifted aroma (camphor→fruity→sweet), and reduced astringency. KEGG analysis enriched aroma-related pathways (e.g., phenylpropanoid biosynthesis). The “processing-volatile-flavor” model provides a basis for standardizing Hawk Tea processing and improving quality.</p>

<span class="label">•</span><p class="display-inline" id="p0005">Terpenoids and esters form the core aroma among 379 identified volatiles.</p>
</li>
<li id="li0010">
<span class="label">•</span><p class="display-inline" id="p0010">A “semi-fermentation threshold effect” triggers the prominent “floral-fruity” flavor.</p>
</li>
<li id="li0015">
<span class="label">•</span><p class="display-inline" id="p0015">Fermentation regulates flavor via specific metabolic pathways.</p>
</li>
</ul></section><section id="s0005"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0025">Hawk tea, derived from the buds, leaves, or tender shoots of <em>Litsea coreana</em>, is a traditional beverage highly cherished in the southwestern region of China, renowned for its uniquely charming aroma, mellow and sweet taste with a faint camphor note, and containing no caffeine(<a href="#bb0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0020">Bai et al., 2022</a>; <a href="#bb0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0030">Chen et al., 2019</a>). In the long-term tea-making practice, people have developed a rich variety of processing techniques, leading to multiple categories of hawk tea. Its production typically involves elaborate processes including withering, fixation, rolling (omitted in some processes), fermentation (covering both fermented and unfermented types) and drying—these unique raw materials and processing techniques jointly endow hawk tea with distinctive flavor attributes(<a href="#bb0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0045">Feng et al., 2019</a>). Hawk tea is widely distributed in Guizhou, with Zunyi City (including Meitan, Tongzi, Chishui, Suiyang, etc.) as the main production area. Currently, the industry in Guizhou is still in the development stage. Affected by issues such as “small scale, scattered distribution”, lack of processing standards and non-standard technology, the tea quality is unstable, which restricts the industrial development.</p>
<p id="p0030">In tea quality evaluation, taste and aroma are core indicators, and volatile metabolites are the key factors determining tea quality and flavor (<a href="#bb0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0085">Liu et al., 2022</a>; <a href="#bb0175" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0175">Zhang et al., 2021</a>; <a href="#bb0185" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0185">Zhao et al., 2024</a>). Different tea varieties exhibit distinct aroma characteristics due to the unique combination of volatile metabolites(<a href="#bb0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0195">Zhou et al., 2022</a>). For instance, post-fermented teas like Liupao tea develop “red, strong, stale, mellow” quality through fermentation, with volatile metabolites such as terpenoids, heterocyclic compounds, and esters forming its characteristic aroma(<a href="#bb0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0075">Liang et al., 2024</a>; <a href="#bb0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0095">Ma et al., 2023</a>). Similarly, the flavor formation of hawk tea is closely associated with changes in volatile metabolites during processing, but the specific mechanisms remain to be clarified.</p>
<p id="p0035">In tea volatile metabolites analysis, headspace solid-phase microextraction-gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) has become an important tool due to its advantages of integrating extraction, concentration, and sampling, along with simplicity, time efficiency, and environmental friendliness(<a href="#bb0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0075">Liang et al., 2024</a>). This technique has been successfully applied in analyzing volatile metabolites of various teas, such as Liupao tea(<a href="#bb0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0075">Liang et al., 2024</a>) and Pu'er tea(<a href="#bb0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0145">Wang et al., 2022</a>), enabling accurate identification of key aroma compounds. For example, Liang et al. used HS-SPME-GC–MS combined with multivariate statistical analysis to identify 1009 volatile metabolites in Liupao tea during fermentation, clarifying the dynamic changes of key flavor-related components.</p>
<p id="p0040">Although hawk tea has gained market recognition, significant flavor differences exist among products processed by different methods, and the underlying metabolic mechanisms have not been fully elucidated. Existing studies on hawk tea mostly focus on the basic components of fresh leaves or single processing links(<a href="#bb0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0030">Chen et al., 2019</a>), and have not systematically compared the dynamic effects of full-gradient processing methods (non-fermentation, light fermentation, medium fermentation, heavy fermentation) on volatile metabolites; meanwhile, the analysis of metabolic mechanisms for flavor formation is insufficient, and the association between processing parameters and key aroma components remains unclear. Field research shows that the processing of hawk tea is mainly based on white tea processing technology, with various other processing technologies such as green tea, oolong tea, and black tea coexisting. In contrast, for other tea types like Liupao tea and black tea, researchers have revealed the correlation between fermentation degree and volatile metabolites change through metabolomic approaches—for example, increased fermentation promotes the accumulation of aldehydes, esters, and heterocyclic compounds, while reducing terpenoids related to “green” notes(<a href="#bb0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0075">Liang et al., 2024</a>; <a href="#bb0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0155">Yao et al., 2023</a>). As a caffeine - free tea, the demand for hawk tea in the healthy beverage market is on the rise(<a href="#bb0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0040">Feng et al., 2024</a>). This research provides valuable references for exploring the formation of its unique flavor, which can increase the added value of the Laoying tea industry and contribute to the rural revitalization in Southwest China.</p>
<p id="p0045">This study used HS-SPME-GC–MS to analyze volatile metabolites in four hawk tea types (green, white, oolong, black) with increasing fermentation(<a href="#bb0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0090">Longo et al., 2018</a>). Combined with non-targeted volatilomics(VOCs), k-Means Clustering(K-means), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes(KEGG), relative Odor Activity Value(rOAV), Principal Component Analysis(PCA),Hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA)(<a href="#bb0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0075">Liang et al., 2024</a>), it identified key differential metabolites, explored how processing affects flavor, provided a basis for hawk tea processing standardization and southwest industrial high-value development, and established a systematic flavor regulation framework.</p></section><section id="s0010"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="s0015"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Materials: Hawk tea samples, raw materials, and reagents</h3>
<p id="p0050">The <em>Litsea coreana</em> used in this study was taxonomically identified by Researcher Wang Jihong (Guizhou Academy of Sciences), based on morphological characteristics and molecular markers. The voucher specimen (No. HGAS 00356) was deposited in the Herbarium of Guizhou Academy of Sciences (HGAS) for future taxonomic verification. <em>Litsea coreana</em> plants were grown in a tea garden located in Meitan County, Zunyi City, Guizhou Province (geographical coordinates of 27°39′N and 107°38′E). The tea garden implemented unified management (e.g., consistent fertilization, irrigation, and pest control) to ensure uniform growth conditions of the plants. In April 2024, when the tender shoots reached the optimal maturity stage (with fully expanded young leaves and bright green appearance), one bud and two leaves were randomly collected from 30 to 35 tea trees (evenly distributed in the tea garden) to ensure sample representativeness. Based on the existing product categories, <em>Litsea coreana</em> was processed into four types of hawk tea using different processes, namely green tea type (GHT, non-fermented), white tea type (TCHT, slightly fermented), oolong tea type (OHT, semi-fermented) and black tea type (BHT, deeply fermented), with the fermentation degree increasing in the above order. Three replicate samples were prepared for each type, and the detailed processing procedures were as follows:</p>
<p id="p0055">GHT: Prepared via fixation, rolling and drying;</p>
<p id="p0060">TCHT: Prepared via natural withering (3 days, during which slight fermentation occurred);</p>
<p id="p0065">OHT: Prepared via sun withering, multiple shaking (3 times, 10 min each), static fermentation (2 h), and drying;</p>
<p id="p0070">BHT: Prepared via withering, rolling, fermentation (20–25 °C, relative humidity &gt;90%, 3 h), and drying.</p>
<p id="p0075">After preparation, all samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen (to inhibit enzyme activity and prevent metabolite degradation) and stored at −80 °C until subsequent analysis. As shown in <a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>, it presents the complete process starting from the collection of <em>Litsea coreana</em> raw materials, the processing of various types of hawk tea, and the subsequent analytical detection stage.</p>

Sample preparation and treatment</h3>
<p id="p0085">To ensure the stability of the chemical properties of the samples, the collected hawk tea samples were rapidly ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen using an agate mortar. Exactly 500 mg (±1 mg) of the powder was accurately weighed and transferred to a 20 mL headspace vial, ensuring the powder settled completely at the bottom. Subsequently, 2 mL of saturated sodium chloride solution and 20 μL of the internal standard solution (3-Hexanone, 10 μg/mL in methanol) were added to the vial. The saturated sodium chloride solution exerted a salting-out effect, which reduced the solubility of volatile metabolites in the aqueous phase and promoted their partition into the headspace, thereby enhancing the subsequent adsorption efficiency of the SPME fiber(<a href="#bb0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0050">Guan et al., 2024</a>). 3-hexanone was selected as the internal standard because it is not detected in hawk tea samples (avoiding interference) and exhibits good thermal stability (no degradation during desorption at 250 °C), which meets the selection criteria for internal standards in the semi-quantitative analysis of volatile metabolites(<a href="#bb0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0150">Wang et al., 2023</a>). DVB/CAR/PDMS exhibits high adsorption efficiency for both polar and non-polar components, and it is commonly used as an extraction fiber for VOCs detection(<a href="#bb0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0010">Antonova-Nedeltcheva et al., 2025</a>). After adding the reagents, the headspace vial was sealed with a crimp cap (equipped with a tetrafluoroethylene gasket) to prevent the loss of volatile metabolites. Volatile metabolite extraction was performed using an automated HS-SPME system, with the following operation steps: First, the sealed headspace vial was placed in a headspace sampler module at 60 °C for 5 min to achieve phase equilibrium of volatile metabolites between the aqueous phase and the headspace. Then, the pre-activated 120 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS extraction fiber was inserted into the vial's headspace and maintained at 60 °C for 15 min for full adsorption of volatile metabolites. This coating exhibits high adsorption efficiency for both polar (e.g., esters) and non-polar (e.g., terpenoids) components, and has been verified to be suitable for the analysis of volatile metabolites in tea(<a href="#bb0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0075">Liang et al., 2024</a>). After adsorption, the fiber was immediately transferred to the gas chromatography (GC) injection port, where thermal desorption was conducted at 250 °C for 5 min. The desorbed volatile metabolites were then introduced into the GC-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) system for separation and analysis.</p></section><section id="s0025"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Sensory evaluation</h3>
<p id="p0090">The sensory evaluation panel consisted of 10 trained evaluators (5 males and 5 females, aged 20–30 years). Evaluations were conducted in accordance with the national standard “Tea Sensory Evaluation Method” (GB/T 23776–2018) and the local standard sensory evaluation specifications (DB52/T 1484–2020), focusing on three key attributes of tea soup: the color, aroma, and taste. The specific evaluation procedure was as follows: Accurately weigh 3.0 g of the tea sample and place it in a special teacup, pour 150 mL of boiling water into it, soak at 95 ± 1 °C for 5 min and then filter it into a tea evaluation bowl. After the tea soup cools down to an appropriate temperature, the evaluators conduct a comprehensive evaluation of the color, aroma, and taste of the tea soup from multiple aspects such as vision, smell and taste(<a href="#bb0190" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0190">Zheng et al., 2024</a>).</p>
<p id="p0095">Ethical compliance: Sensory evaluation was conducted involving ten healthy volunteers who provided written informed consent. The study was categorized as non-invasive and posed no foreseeable risks, thus aligning with the IFST Guidelines for Sensory Analysis (2020, <a href="#s0090" class="usa-link">Section 4.2</a>), which exempt non-medical sensory research from ethical review.</p></section><section id="s0030"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. GC–MS conditions and data processing</h3>
<p id="p0100">VOCs in the samples were analyzed using an Agilent 8890 gas chromatograph (GC) coupled with an Agilent 7000E mass spectrometer (MS) system.</p>
<p id="p0105">GC conditions: The inlet temperature was set at 250 °C, with a spitless injection mode maintained for 5 min, followed by a split ratio of 10:1. It is equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column of 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, and the stationary phase is 5% phenylpolymethylsiloxane. The carrier gas is helium, with a linear velocity of 1.2 mL/min.</p>
<p id="p0110">Column oven temperature program: Initial temperature of 40 °C (held for 3.5 min), ramped to 100 °C at 10 °C/min; then to 180 °C at 7 °C/min, and finally to 280 °C at 25 °C/min (held for 5 min).</p>
<p id="p0115">MS conditions: The MS was a quadrupole type, equipped with an electron impact ion source (EI) operated at 70 eV. The temperatures of the quadrupole detector, ion source, and MS interface were 150 °C, 230 °C and 280 °C respectively. Analysis was performed in the selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode.</p>
<p id="p0120">Qualification and Semi-quantification of VOCs: Metabolites were identified using a custom-built database, which integrates multi-species data, literature references, standard substance information and retention indices. This database details the retention time (RT), qualitative ions and quantitative ions information of each compound, ensuring accurate metabolite annotation (<a href="#bb0160" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0160">Yuan et al., 2022</a>). The relative content analysis of the metabolites is carried out using a semi-quantitative method, and 3-hexanone is used as the internal standard for determination(<a href="#bb0165" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0165">Zeng et al., 2024</a>).</p></section><section id="s0035"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Analysis of metabolite rOAV</h3>
<p id="p0125">The rOAV is a method used to determine the key flavor compounds in food. It combines the sensory threshold of the compounds and can clarify the contribution of each aroma compound to the overall aroma characteristics of the sample(<a href="#bb0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0060">Huang et al., 2022</a>). The calculation formula of rOAV is as follows:</p>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="fo0005"><tr><td class="formula"><math id="M1" display="block" altimg="si1.svg"><msub><mtext>rOAV</mtext><mi mathvariant="normal">i</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">C</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">i</mi></msub><mo>/</mo><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">T</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">i</mi></msub></math></td></tr></table>
<p>where C<sub>i</sub> is the relative content (μg/g) of the compound, and T<sub>i</sub> is the threshold (μg/g) of the compound. By calculating the rOAV, it is possible to identify which aroma compounds make a significant contribution to the overall flavor of the food. Generally, rOAV≥1 indicates that the compound makes a direct contribution to the flavor of the sample because its actual concentrations exceed their sensory thresholds, thus having a significant impact on the flavor(<a href="#bb0140" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0140">Van Gemert, 2011</a>).</p></section><section id="s0040"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Multivariate statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="p0130">All multivariate statistical analyses were performed on the relative content data of VOCs, with preprocessing steps adjusted according to different analysis models. PCA is a dimensionality reduction technique that simplifies complex datasets into several principal components, enabling visualization of sample differences and the overall structure in a low-dimensional space(<a href="#bb0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0005">Abdelhafez et al., 2020</a>). Before analysis, data were preprocessed with unit variance scaling. PCA can visually display the overall changes of volatile metabolites in each sample and detect the trends and patterns within the data.</p>
<p id="p0135">HCA groups samples or variables according to their similarity(<a href="#bb0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0170">Zhang et al., 2022</a>). The results are presented as heat maps, highlighting the similarities and differences in sample clustering. OPLS-DA is used to identify specific volatile metabolites that distinguish different sample categories, providing an in-depth understanding of the factors influencing the changes in the volatile metabolite profiles(<a href="#bb0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0070">Kang et al., 2022</a>). When analyzing the differences between two groups of samples, differential metabolites are determined using the thresholds of Variable Importance in the Projection (VIP) ≥ 1 and |Log2FC| ≥ 1. Before conducting the OPLS-DA analysis, the data was subjected to mean centering. To avoid overfitting, 200 permutation tests were carried out. The identified metabolites were annotated through the KEGG Compound Database, and these metabolites were mapped to the KEGG Pathway Database. The pathways related to significantly regulated metabolites were input into the Metabolite Set Enrichment Analysis (MSEA), and their significance was determined by the <em>p</em>-value obtained from the hypergeometric test. The Good Scents Company Information System platform was utilized to determine whether the metabolites possess aroma properties (URL: <a href="https://thegoodscentscompany.com" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://thegoodscentscompany.com</a>). The data processing was completed using Excel software, and the figures were drawn using the free online data analysis platform Majorbio Cloud (URL: <a href="https://cloud.metware.cn" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://cloud.metware.cn</a>). The images in this paper can be downloaded via the URL: <a href="https://data.mendeley.com/drafts/4skgtkncf9" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://data.mendeley.com/drafts/4skgtkncf9</a>.</p></section></section><section id="s0045"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results and discussion</h2>
<section id="s0050"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Quality control (QC) analysis of samples</h3>
<p id="p0140">To ensure the reliability and stability of the experimental data, quality control (QC) samples were prepared, and all samples (including QC samples) were processed and analyzed under the same conditions. Specifically, the QC samples were prepared by pooling all the research samples (i.e., samples to be tested) and homogenizing the mixture. These QC samples were then analyzed using the same processing procedures and detection methods as the research samples, to verify the reproducibility of the entire analytical workflow.</p>
<p id="p0145">Using the HS-SPME-GC/MS technique, TIC chromatograms of all samples were acquired under SIM mode. As shown in Fig. S1 A, the TIC chromatograms of the three QC samples showed significant overlap, with a peak matching rate of &gt;95% (based on retention time, RT deviation &lt;0.05 min) and a relative standard deviation (RSD) of peak areas for major volatile metabolites &lt;12%. This indicated that the metabolite extraction and detection methods established in this study had good reproducibility, which could provide stable and reliable data for subsequent experiments.</p>
<p id="p0150">To further assess data dispersion, the coefficient of variation (CV)-defined as the ratio of the standard deviation (SD) to the meaning of the original data-was calculated for all quantifiable volatile metabolites. The experimental results showed that over 90% of these metabolites had a CV &lt; 0.5, and more than 85% had a CV &lt; 0.3 (Fig. S1 B). Consistent with the general criterion for QC sample stability (CV &lt; 0.3 for reliable data in volatilities), this low CV distribution indicated minimal data dispersion and high stability of the experimental data, demonstrating the excellent signal stability of the analytical method. These CV results were also comparable to or even superior to those reported in similar studies. For instance, Tranchida observed that only 88% of their quantitative precision data had a CV &lt; 0.3 (<a href="#bb0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0135">Tranchida et al., 2013</a>). In contrast, our study achieved a higher proportion of metabolites with CV &lt; 0.3, which further underscores the advantages of the analytical method employed herein.</p>
<p id="p0155">Furthermore, this high stability not only guaranteed the reliability of the research results and the credibility of the data but also ensured that the subsequent comparisons of volatile metabolite profiles among hawk tea samples with different processing techniques would reflect true differences induced by processing, rather than random experimental errors. This laid a solid foundation for in-depth analysis of the relationship between processing techniques, volatile metabolite composition, and flavor characteristics of hawk tea.</p></section><section id="s0055"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Sensory evaluation</h3>
<p id="p0160">The evaluation team conducted a standardized sensory evaluation of hawk tea prepared by four different processing methods from three aspects (color, aroma and taste), in accordance with the tea sensory evaluation method and the local standard for sensory evaluation(<a href="#bb0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0105">Moreira et al., 2024</a>). Each type of tea exhibited unique characteristics in terms of soup color, aroma, and taste (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a> A). Specifically, the tea soup characteristics of the four samples are as follows (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a> B): GHT exhibited a yellowish green tea soup, with a fresh and elegant aroma and a gentle taste; TCHT had an orange tea soup, characterized by a distinct camphor wood aroma and faint astringency; OHT showed an apricot-colored tea soup, accompanied by a fruity aroma and moderate sweetness; BHT presented an orange-red tea soup, with a rich sweet aroma and mellow taste. With the increase in fermentation degree (from low to high), the tea soup color gradually deepened, the aroma shifted from camphor wood to fruity and sweet notes, the astringency gradually weakened, and the sweetness gradually increased. These sensory differences were closely related to the changes in sensory-related components induced by processing: For example, the sweet aroma of BHT was consistent with the higher content of phenethyl alcohol and ethyl acetate compared to GHT; the weakened astringency in fermented hawk teas (OHT, BHT) might be attributed to the reduction of catechins via oxidation during the fermentation process(<a href="#bb0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0035">Feng et al., 2025</a>).</p>

(C) The proportion of the quantity of volatile metabolites.</p></figcaption></figure></section><section id="s0060"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3. Classification of volatile metabolites</h3>
<p id="p0165">In this study, a total of 379 volatile metabolites were identified from hawk tea prepared by four different processing techniques. Detailed information of these metabolites, including compound name, molecular weight, qualitative ions, quantitative ions, and class in samples, is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Based on their chemical structures and properties, the 379 metabolites were accurately categorized into 15 classes. The number of species and percentage of each class relative to the total identified metabolites are presented in <a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a> C. Among the 15 classes, the top five in terms of species count were as follows: terpenoids (106 species, accounting for 28.0% of total metabolites), esters (70 species, 18.5%), ketones (33 species, 8.7%), alcohols (28 species, 7.4%), aldehydes (28 species, 7.4%), and heterocyclic compounds (28 species, 7.4%). The remaining classes included acids (25 species, 6.6%), amines (14 species, 3.7%, a subclass of nitrogen-containing compounds), hydrocarbons (14 species, 3.7%), phenols (10 species, 2.6%), ethers (9 species, 2.4%), aromatic hydrocarbons (7 species, 1.8%, specifically benzene and its derivatives, excluding phenols and heterocyclic aromatic compounds), other nitrogen-containing compounds (5 species, 1.3%, nitrogen-containing substances excluding amines), halogenated hydrocarbons (1 species, 0.3%), and sulfur-containing compounds (1 species, 0.3%).</p>
<p id="p0170">As the most abundant class, terpenoids exhibited a high abundance consistent with their biological characteristics of wide distribution and structural diversity in natural plants(<a href="#bb0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0065">Jin et al., 2020</a>). Typical aroma-active substances were identified in this class, such as linalool (floral note) and limonene (citrus note). Most terpenoids had a relatively low odor threshold, playing a dominant role in endowing hawk tea with a “fresh” overall aroma. Esters (18.5%), the second most abundant class, further enriched the sweet and fruity layers of hawk tea aroma due to the detection of Acetic acid, phenyl-, isopentyl ester (honey-sweet note) and Butanoic acid, 3-methyl-, propyl ester (fruity note). Together, terpenoids and esters formed the aroma foundation of hawk tea, characterized by “floral and fruity notes as the main tone”—this directly corresponded to the sensory characteristics reported in <a href="#s0055" class="usa-link">Section 3.2</a>, such as “fresh and elegant aroma of GHT” and “prominent fruity aroma of OHT”. In addition, although heterocyclic compounds accounted for only 7.4% of total species, substances in this class (e.g., dimethylpyrazine and 2-Hexanoylfuran) were largely generated via the Maillard reaction and caramelization during fermentation. Therefore, its content in black tea-type hawk tea (BHT) is significantly higher than that in hawk tea prepared by other processing methods, making it a key substance that endows BHT with a “caramel-sweet aroma”. Meanwhile, aldehydes (7.4%) contained hexanal (grassy note), which was more abundant in OHT and primarily responsible for its “grassy aroma”.</p>
<p id="p0175">While specific relative abundances of volatile metabolites varied across different studies (due to differences in extraction techniques, analytical methods, and processing protocols), the types and variation trends of volatile metabolite classes identified in this study were consistent with prior research on hawk tea(<a href="#bb0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0030">Chen et al., 2019</a>). This consistency further verifies the scientific rigor and reliability of the results obtained in this study.</p></section><section id="s0065"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.4. Multivariate analysis of volatile metabolites</h3>
<p id="p0180">To gain an in-depth understanding of the impact of different processing methods on the volatile metabolites in hawk tea, multivariate statistical analysis methods such as PCA, HCA and OPLS-DA were adopted in this study.</p>
<p id="p0185">PCA was used to reduce the dimensionality of volatile metabolite data, enabling intuitive visualization of sample differences and data trends in low-dimensional space(<a href="#bb0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0025">Ben Salem &amp; Ben Abdelaziz, 2021</a>). Prior to analysis, the data were preprocessed with unit variance scaling to eliminate the influence of dimensional differences among variables; abnormal samples were further identified and excluded using a threshold of ±3 standard deviations to avoid distorting the clustering results. After performing unit variance scaling on the data, it was found that the QC samples clustered together in the PCA score plot (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a> A), indicating that the analysis process had good robustness and reliability (<a href="#bb0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0120">Pan et al., 2022</a>). The cumulative contribution rate of the first two principal components (PC1 + PC2) reached 66.79%, among which PC1 accounted for 43.59% and PC2 accounted for 23.20%. Notably, the four research samples (GHT, TCHT, OHT, BHT) showed distinct separation: GHT (non-fermented) and TCHT (slightly fermented) were distributed in the left region of PC1, while OHT (semi-fermented) and BHT (deeply fermented) were in the right region, reflecting a clear correlation between sample clustering and fermentation degree.</p>

The analysis used Euclidean distance to calculate the similarity between samples and Ward's minimum variance method for cluster linkage. The HCA results showed that the samples were clustered into two major branches: one consisting of GHT and TCHT (non-fermented or slightly fermented) and the other comprising OHT and BHT (semi-fermented or deeply fermented) (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a> B). Quantitatively, the total relative content of volatile metabolites in BHT and OHT was 2.9-fold and 3.4-fold higher than that in GHT, respectively, consistent with the PCA clustering trend and further confirming that fermentation degree is a key driver of volatile metabolite variation.</p>
<p id="p0195">OPLS-DA was applied to screen differential volatile metabolites by separating the X matrix (volatile metabolite content) into two parts: variation related to the Y matrix (sample processing type) and orthogonal (unrelated) variation. This effectively eliminates noise and enhances the interpretability of group differences. Three pairwise comparisons were conducted: TCHT vs. GHT, OHT vs. GHT, and BHT vs. GHT (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a> A–C). K-means clustering analysis of differential volatile metabolites</h3>
<p id="p0210">K-means clustering was performed on standardized differential metabolites using Euclidean distance as the similarity metric. The optimal number of clusters (k = 6) was determined via the elbow method, where the within-cluster sum of squares stabilized. The algorithm iterated until centroids changed by &lt;0.001 or reached a maximum of 1000 iterations (<a href="#bb0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0075">Liang et al., 2024</a>). When analyzing the volatile metabolites in hawk tea processed by four different methods, we carried out K-means clustering analysis on the standardized differential metabolites and drew the corresponding clustering analysis diagram. As can be seen from the diagram (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>), Subclass 1 contains 47 volatile metabolites, and the contents of these components are mainly high in fermented tea, indicating that they may be produced during the fermentation process (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a> A). Subclass 2 contains 93 volatile metabolites, and with the increase of the fermentation degree, the contents of these components increase significantly (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a> B). Subclass 3 contains 97 volatile metabolites, which also have high contents in fermented tea, especially in OHT (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a> C). Subclass 4 contains 27 volatile metabolites, and the contents of these components are high in unfermented tea, indicating that they may be transformed or degraded during the fermentation process (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a> D). Subclass 5 contains 46 volatile metabolites, which mainly have high contents in slightly fermented tea (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a> E). Subclass 6 contains 69 volatile metabolites, and the contents of these components are the highest in OHT, indicating that they may be related to specific oxidation processes (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a> F).</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="f0025" title="Fig. 5"><h4 class="obj_head">Fig. 5.</h4>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=12853061_gr5.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/7877/12853061/dee41d3c31b7/gr5.jpg" loading="lazy" id="lk0025" height="551" width="787" alt="Fig. 5"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/f0025/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>K-means clustering analysis chart of volatile component differences in hawk tea with different types.</p></figcaption></figure><p id="p0215">The 6 subclasses of differential volatile metabolites exhibit distinct responses to hawk tea fermentation. Subclass 1 (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a> A) exhibits a fermentation-dependent characteristic. The formation and accumulation mechanism of its representative component, Copaene, is as follows: On one hand, fermentation can promote the degradation of glycosidically bound precursors in tea leaves, releasing free Copaene; on the other hand, microbial metabolism or enzymatic reactions (such as oxidation and rearrangement reactions) may also directly synthesize this component. However, it should be noted that Copaene decomposes further during deep fermentation (e.g., BHT), so its content is relatively more stable in TCHT (slightly fermented tea). This component can effectively enhance the overall aroma richness of hawk tea and neutralize the residual astringent note of TCHT caused by slight fermentation, improving flavor harmony. Subclass 2 (deep fermentation-specific) sees linear value increases, dominated by terpenoids/esters—α,α,4-trimethyl-3-cyclohexene-1-methanethiol (<em>Saccharomyces cerevisiae</em> metabolism)(<a href="#bb0200" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0200">Zhou et al., 2024</a>)and trans-β-ionone (carotenoid degradation) drive BHT's caramel-sweet aroma(<a href="#bb0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0055">Hong et al., 2023</a>; <a href="#bb0115" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0115">Ouyang et al., 2024</a>; <a href="#bb0125" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0125">Qi et al., 2024</a>). Subclass 3 exhibits a “semi-fermentation enrichment” pattern, with 2-undecanone (possessing a fruity aroma with fatty undertones) as a key component. The “withering-turning” process activates the degradation and oxidation of lipids, facilitating the synthesis of 2-undecanone(<a href="#bb0180" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0180">Zhang et al., 2025</a>). This compound is prone to oxidative degradation. However, the accumulation of 2-undecanone enhances the “fruity aroma” characteristic of OHT observed in sensory evaluation. As can be seen from Subclasses 2 and 3, semi-fermentation and fermentation can increase the content of some esters and ketones by more than double. Subclass 4 (non-fermentation-retained) retains high values only in GHT; monoterpenoids (e.g., β-farnesene) and C6 aldehydes (e.g., (E)-2-hexenal) degrade via fermentative PPO/alcohol dehydrogenase, their loss eliminating GHT's fresh grassy aroma in fermented teas. Subclass 5 reaches its peak content in TCHT, with β-damascenone and (E,E)-2,4-undecadienal as its representative components. These components may exist in the form of glycoside conjugates in tea leaves. The “light rolling-short fermentation” process of TCHT promotes their formation, endowing TCHT with a faint fruity aroma and a meaty note, which caters to young consumers' preference for low-astringency tea beverages. However, during fermentation, their content gradually decreases due to oxidation, volatilization, and microbial activities. Subclass 6 is dominated by semi-fermentation. Its four representative compounds—decanal, (E,E)-2,4-nonadienal, 2-methylisoborneol, and nonanal—show a consistent content variation pattern during tea fermentation: their levels increase in the early stage, driven by glycoside hydrolysis, lipid oxidation, or microbial metabolism; in the later stage, their contents decrease due to volatilization, oxidative degradation, or microbial transformation. These compounds contribute distinct aroma notes respectively: citrusy fatty aroma, waxy note, earthy moldy note, and rose-like fruity aroma. Collectively, they shape the layered complexity of the fermented tea's aroma profile.</p>
<p id="p0220">K-means clustering clarified fermentation response thresholds for each subclass (e.g., Subclass 3 is inferred that there exists a “semi-fermentation threshold effect” in Hawk Tea—when the degree of fermentation reaches 30%–50%, the contents of esters and heterocyclic compounds increase sharply and become the dominant flavor components. This effect can serve as a regulatory target for the “floral-fruity” quality of Hawk Tea.) and established a “cluster-compound-sensory” logical chain. This confirms fermentation as the core factor regulating hawk tea flavor, providing theoretical support for targeted processing optimization—such as controlling GHT fermentation to preserve freshness and enhancing BHT fermentation to boost sweet aroma—and the development of characteristic hawk tea products.</p></section><section id="s0075"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.6. The rOAV analysis of key aroma components in hawk tea</h3>
<p id="p0225">In order to accurately identify the key compounds that affect the flavor of hawk tea, this study meticulously calculated the rOAV of all volatile compounds. Generally, rOAV≥1 indicates that the compound makes a significant contribution to the flavor(<a href="#bb0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0060">Huang et al., 2022</a>). The study found that a total of 90 key volatile compounds were identified in the four types of hawk tea, covering terpenoids, esters, heterocyclic compounds, aldehydes, ketones and alcohols (Table S7). They have a significant impact on the fruity, floral, sweet and other aroma characteristics of hawk tea: for example, the terpenoid <span class="font-variant-small-caps">d</span>-limonene brings a citrus aroma and enhances the fruity aroma(<a href="#bb0080" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0080">Liu et al., 2024</a>); α-pinene endows a pine wood aroma and adds a natural atmosphere(<a href="#bb0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0110">Nothnagel et al., 2023</a>); the phenolic compound ethyl maltol has a caramel and strawberry aroma, enriching the flavor(<a href="#bb0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0100">Mao et al., 2024</a>); butanoic acid, 3-methyl-, propyl ester contributes an apple and fruity aroma, enhancing the fruity aroma tone(<a href="#bb0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0015">Api et al., 2022</a>).</p>
<p id="p0230">Fermentation promotes biochemical reactions, causing changes in the types and contents of key aroma components, and the relative contents and rOAV values of many components change significantly (See <a href="#t0005" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). Combined with the K-means analysis, the copaene compound is in subclass 1. With the deepening of fermentation, its content first increases and then decreases; the two compounds α,α,4-trimethyl-3-cyclohexene-1-methanethiol and trans-β-ionone are in subclass 2, and their aromas increase successively; the 2-undecanone compound is in subclass 3, and its content gradually increases with fermentation, reaching the highest in OHT and slightly decreasing in BHT; the compounds (E)-1-(2,6,6-trimethyl-1,3-cyclohexadien-1-yl)-2-buten-1-one and (E,E)-2,4-undecadienal are in subclass 4, and their contents are only prominent in slightly fermented tea; the four compounds decanal, (E,E)-2,4-nonadienal, 2-methylisoborneol and nonanal are in subclass 6, and the odors of these compounds are clearly distinguishable at different fermentation stages. Fermentation is crucial for the formation and changes of the aroma components in hawk tea. The key aroma components determined in this study are similar to the important aromatic components in hawk tea reported in the literature, such as α-Pinene, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">d</span>-Limonene, trans-β-Ionone, etc. At the same time, some compounds that are rarely reported in the literature, such as α,α,4-trimethyl-3-cyclohexene-1-methanethiol and (E)-1-(2,6,6-trimethyl-1,3-cyclohexadien-1-yl)-2-buten-1-one, etc., were discovered, providing new clues and directions for subsequent research.</p>

For example, in <a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>C (BHT vs. GHT), the “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” pathway (DA score = 1.82, <em>P</em> = 0.003) has 12 differential metabolites, which is the most significant pathway related to aroma formation. The DA score of the “Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon degradation” pathway are right-skewed (Sig_compound: Hydrocoumarin), suggesting that the metabolites in this pathway accumulate more significantly in TCHT, which may be related to the formation of the unique flavor of TCHT; the change in the “Phenylalanine metabolism” pathway may affect the synthesis of Benzeneacetic acid, 2-hydroxy-, and thus affect the aroma. In OHT vs. GHT (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a> B), The DA scores of the pathways “Toluene degradation” (Sig_compound: Benzyl alcohol)and “Aromatic compound degradation”(Sig_compound: Benzaldehyde) are prominent and the P-values are significant, indicating that the abundances of metabolites in these pathways have obviously changed during the processing of oolong tea. The “Toluene degradation” pathway may be involved in the generation or transformation of volatile aromatic compounds, contributing to the fruity aroma of oolong tea; the change in the pathway “Microbial metabolism in diverse environments” reflects the influence of the role of microorganisms on the flavor of oolong tea. In BHT vs. GHT (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a> C), The DA scores of pathways such as “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis”(Sig_compound: Phenol, 4-(3-hydroxy-1-propenyl)-2-methoxy-), “Biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites” (Sig_compound: trans-Cinnamic acid)and “Sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis”(Sig_compound: α-Farnesene) have significantly changed and the P-values are low, indicating that the synthesis of secondary metabolites has changed greatly during the processing of black tea. The change in the “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” pathway may lead to the accumulation of phenylpropanoid compounds in black tea, forming characteristic aromas such as the sweet aroma of black tea.</p>
<p id="p0250">Overall, with the change of processing methods from TCHT to BHT, the trends of changes in metabolic pathways are different, and the metabolic pathways related to aroma are more significant in OHT and BHT with higher fermentation degrees. These changes provide a basis for analyzing the flavor formation mechanism during the processing of hawk tea, highlighting the crucial role of processing techniques in the quality of tea.</p></section><section id="s0085"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.8. Flavoromics analysis of volatile differential metabolites in hawk tea</h3>
<p id="p0255">This study deeply explored the influence of different processing methods on the flavor characteristics of hawk tea. By analyzing whether each differential volatile metabolite has an aroma attribute, it was found that their combination can create a unique tea flavor. The flavor characteristics of the differential volatile metabolites in each group are summarized in Tables S9-S11. In the comparison group of TCHT and GHT (<a href="#f0035" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a> A), there were 39 differential metabolites with flavor characteristics. Among them, the differential metabolites labeled with the top 5 flavor characteristics were “berry, foliage, peanut, bitter and citrus” respectively; there were 50 differential metabolites between the OHT and GHT groups (<a href="#f0035" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a> B), and the differential metabolites labeled with the top 5 flavor characteristics were “soapy, fatty, peony, camphor and cherry” respectively; there were 56 differential metabolites between the BHT and GHT groups (<a href="#f0035" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a> C), and the differential metabolites labeled with the top 5 flavor characteristics were “green, fresh, rose, woody and citrus” respectively. This indicates that as the fermentation degree deepens, the number of flavor characteristics of hawk tea gradually increases, and its aroma also shows a transition from fruity aroma to floral aroma and then to the aroma of grass and plants.</p>

The results showed that among the slightly fermented teas (Fig. S4 A), the flavor characteristics with a relatively large number of labels were “herbal(18 metabolites), woody (15 metabolites), citrus (12 metabolites) and waxy (10 metabolites)”; among the weakly fermented teas (Fig. S4 B), the sensory flavors of “sweet (22 metabolites), floral (19 metabolites) and woody (14 metabolites)” had particularly prominent labeling numbers; while among the strongly fermented teas (Fig. S4 C), the sensory flavors of “sweet (25 metabolites), floral (21 metabolites), woody (18 metabolites), citrus (16 metabolites) and green (13 metabolites)” had the most considerable labeling numbers. As the fermentation degree increases, the influence of fermentation on the flavor of hawk tea shows a strong correlation. The “woody aroma” flavor gradually weakens, while the differences in the labeling of the “green aroma and sweetness” are significantly enhanced. This indicates that the woody aroma, green flavor and sweetness have particularly prominent characteristics during the processing of hawk tea, and the changes in flavor characteristics under different fermentation degrees provide an important basis for the quality evaluation and flavor improvement of hawk tea, and also provide a scientific reference for consumers to choose diverse flavors.</p>
<p id="p0265">It should be noted that this study has the following limitations: Samples were collected only from Meitan County, Zunyi, Guizhou Province, and did not cover other major hawk-tea-producing regions such as Sichuan, Yunnan, Hunan, or Guangxi; thus, the conclusions may not fully represent the characteristics of the entire industry. Only four traditional processing methods were selected, while emerging technologies such as microwave enzyme-inactivation and freeze-drying were not included. Microbiome data were not integrated to elucidate the regulatory role of microorganisms in volatile synthesis during fermentation, especially the metabolism of glycosides. Future research should expand the sampling scope and incorporate metagenomic approaches to further clarify the mechanisms underlying flavor formation.</p></section></section><section id="s0090"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0270">Using HS-SPME-GC–MS and non-targeted volatilomics, this study clarified how processing (especially fermentation) affects hawk tea's volatiles, aroma, and metabolic pathways, with key conclusions:</p>
<p id="p0275">Among the 379 identified volatile metabolites (covering 15 categories), terpenoids (accounting for 28.0%) and esters (accounting for 18.5%) are the key components that form the core aroma of hawk tea. Using the screening criterion of rOAV ≥1, a total of 90 flavor compounds were obtained, 10 of which are key aroma components. Sensory evaluation results showed that fermentation darkens the soup color of hawk tea, changes its aroma, and reduces its astringency, with semi-fermented OHT showing particularly prominent “floral-fruity” characteristics. Based on the compositional variation pattern of Subclass 3 (the subclass corresponding to the content peak in semi-fermented tea), it can be inferred that hawk tea exhibits a “semi-fermentation threshold effect”. When the fermentation degree reaches the semi-fermentation level, the contents of esters and heterocyclic compounds increase sharply and become the dominant flavor components. This effect can serve as a key target for regulating the “flower and fruit aroma” quality of Litsea coreana var. lanuginosa. KEGG analysis indicated that fermentation can affect flavor by regulating specific metabolic pathways, such as the “polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon degradation” pathway associated with camphor aroma in TCHT, the “toluene degradation” pathway associated with fruity aroma in OHT, and the “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” pathway associated with sweet taste in BHT.</p>
<p id="p0280">The “processing-volatile metabolite-flavor” model can provide guidance for standard formulation in the industrial upgrading of hawk tea. For instance, GHT products are recommended and promoted for young consumers who prefer a fresh and brisk flavor, while BHT products are recommended and promoted for middle-aged and elderly consumers who favor a sweet aroma. The samples in this study were only collected from Guizhou. The content of terpenoid precursors in hawk tea from this region is relatively high, which may result in a stronger correlation between “terpenoids and floral-fruity aroma” compared to other producing areas. Subsequently, metagenomics will be used to analyze the regulatory role of microorganisms in the degradation of terpenoids, and a new processing technology, ‘microwave blanching + low-temperature fermentation’, will be developed to specifically enhance the flavor diversity of hawk tea.</p></section><section id="s0100"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CRediT authorship contribution statement</h2>
