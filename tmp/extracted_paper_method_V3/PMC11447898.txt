<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/bf39/11447898/b4d5cd7356db/es4c02833_0004.jpg" loading="lazy" id="ab-tgr1" height="356" width="666" alt="graphic file with name es4c02833_0004.jpg"></p>
<p>The machine-learning tool MS2Tox can prioritize hazardous
nontargeted
molecular features in environmental waters, by predicting acute fish
lethality of unknown molecules based on their MS<sup>2</sup> spectra,
prior to structural annotation. It has yet to be investigated how
the extent of molecular coverage, MS<sup>2</sup> spectra quality,
and toxicity prediction confidence depend on sample complexity and
MS<sup>2</sup> data acquisition strategies. We compared two common
nontargeted MS<sup>2</sup> acquisition strategies with liquid chromatography
high-resolution mass spectrometry for structural annotation accuracy
by SIRIUS+CSI:FingerID and MS2Tox toxicity prediction of 191 reference
chemicals spiked to LC-MS water, groundwater, surface water, and wastewater.
Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) resulted in higher rates (19–62%)
of correct structural annotations among reference chemicals in all
matrices except wastewaters, compared to data-independent acquisition
(DIA, 19–50%). However, DIA resulted in higher MS<sup>2</sup> detection rates (59–84% DIA, 37–82% DDA), leading
to higher true positive rates for spectral library matching, 40–73%
compared to 34–72%. DDA resulted in higher MS2Tox toxicity
prediction accuracy than DIA, with root-mean-square errors of 0.62
and 0.71 log-mM, respectively. Given the importance of MS<sup>2</sup> spectral quality, we introduce a “CombinedConfidence”
score to convey relative confidence in MS2Tox predictions and apply
this approach to prioritize potentially ecotoxic nontargeted features
in environmental waters.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> nontargeted analysis, nontargeted screening, high-resolution mass spectrometry, machine-learning, toxicity prediction, MS/MS data acquisition methods, LC-HRMS, LC<sub>50</sub></p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><h2>Short abstract</h2>
<p>Two common
MS<sup>2</sup> data acquisition strategies for
nontargeted analysis of water were compared with respect to molecular
coverage, structural annotation, and performance in MS2Tox, a machine-learning
tool for prediction of acute fish toxicity.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>A wide variety of anthropogenic substances
have been identified
in groundwaters, surface waters, and wastewaters, including pharmaceuticals,
personal care products, industrial additives, per- and polyfluoroalkyl
substances (PFAS), and pesticides,<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a>−<a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> with a range of toxicities to humans and wildlife.<sup><a href="#ref9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref9">9</a>−<a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a></sup> While some of these are priority substances that are routinely monitored
according to national or international programs and directives, 95%
of chemicals in commerce have never been monitored in the environment
and may have multiple transformation products for which exposure and
hazard have not yet been assessed.<sup><a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a></sup> In
fact, more than 370,000 chemicals or chemical mixtures are registered
for production and use globally, many of which have unknown or ambiguous
structures.<sup><a href="#ref13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref13">13</a></sup> Thus, new tools and approaches
are needed to monitor chemical pollution that can identify unknown
toxic substances in the environment.</p>
<p>Nontargeted analysis of
environmental samples by liquid chromatography
high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) has evolved as a powerful
tool for acquiring comprehensive molecular information from environmental
samples. Thousands, or even tens of thousands, of unknown molecular
features can be detected in environmental samples,<sup><a href="#ref14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref14">14</a>,<a href="#ref15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref15">15</a></sup> with each feature having a chromatographic retention time and peak
area(s), full scan mass spectrum (i.e., MS<sup>1</sup>), and possibly
a fragmentation spectrum (i.e., MS<sup>2</sup>).<sup><a href="#ref16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref16">16</a>,<a href="#ref17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref17">17</a></sup> Structural annotation of unknown features can to some extent be
accomplished by MS<sup>2</sup> spectral matching to reference libraries
(e.g., MassBank<sup><a href="#ref18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref18">18</a></sup>), or use of <em>in
silico</em> structural annotation tools.<sup><a href="#ref19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref19">19</a>,<a href="#ref20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref20">20</a></sup> In both cases the annotation rate and accuracy depend on the availability
of reference MS<sup>2</sup> spectra, as well as the quality of acquired
MS<sup>2</sup>. Even with quality experimental MS<sup>2</sup> spectra,
toxic chemicals may remain unannotated if reference spectra are not
available, or if the analytical standard cannot be purchased or synthesized.
As a result, only a small fraction of features detected in water samples
are confidently annotated or confirmed at the structural level by
nontarget workflows (e.g., &lt;1%<sup><a href="#ref14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref14">14</a>,<a href="#ref21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref21">21</a></sup>), thereby
still limiting the practical use of nontargeted analysis for rapid
screening of toxic substances.</p>
<p>Various strategies have been
employed to prioritize features of
interest in nontargeted data sets, including through study design,
such as temporal and spatial trends analysis,<sup><a href="#ref22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref22">22</a>−<a href="#ref26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref26">26</a></sup> case-control comparisons,<sup><a href="#ref27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref27">27</a></sup> or with mass spectral information, such as empirical formula assignment,<sup><a href="#ref28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref28">28</a></sup> mass-defect, or fragmentation flagging.<sup><a href="#ref29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref29">29</a></sup> Since most environmental samples contain thousands
of detectable molecular features by nontargeted LC-HRMS, a rapid toxicity-based
feature prioritization strategy is desirable. In this direction, Meekel
et al.<sup><a href="#ref30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref30">30</a></sup> used structural alerts to trigger
high-quality MS<sup>2</sup> data acquisition with multiple collision
energies for presumed toxic compounds based on MS<sup>2</sup> prescans.
Recently, we introduced MS2Tox, a machine-learning tool for prediction
of ecotoxicity (i.e., 50% lethal concentration, LC<sub>50</sub>, to
aquatic organisms including fish) based only on MS<sup>1</sup> and
MS<sup>2</sup> spectral data of unknown molecular feature, leveraging
on <em>in silico</em> tools (i.e., SIRIUS<sup><a href="#ref19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref19">19</a></sup>). Molecular fingerprints predicted from mass spectrometric
data using SIRIUS software contain structural information. However,
toxicity predictions in MS2Tox do not require unequivocal structural
annotation or confirmation, and thus MS2Tox enables rapid flagging
of the most toxic molecules in complex samples. A similar approach,
also using SIRIUS fingerprints,<sup><a href="#ref19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref19">19</a></sup> to prioritize
compounds based on their toxicity was presented by Arturi et al.<sup><a href="#ref31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref31">31</a></sup> for toxicological end points in ToxCast/Tox21.
Such rapid flagging of potentially toxic substances can be used as
a first step to prioritize molecular features for subsequent identification
or biological testing,<sup><a href="#ref32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref32">32</a></sup> and could complement
traditional approaches such as effect-directed analysis<sup><a href="#ref33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref33">33</a>,<a href="#ref34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref34">34</a></sup> which may be more accurate but are slower, low-throughput, and more
resource intensive.</p>
<p>The choice of HRMS data acquisition method
is an important consideration
for effectively detecting and characterizing chemicals by nontargeted
analysis.<sup><a href="#ref35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref35">35</a></sup> For MS<sup>2</sup> spectral
acquisition by LC-HRMS, two general approaches are used: data-dependent
acquisition (DDA) and data-independent acquisition (DIA). In DDA,
MS<sup>2</sup> spectra are either acquired based on predefined user
criteria or dynamically based on MS<sup>1</sup> full scan data. One
popular approach is suspect screening, whereby MS<sup>2</sup> spectra
are specifically acquired for a defined list of MS<sup>1</sup> precursor <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values selected <em>a priori</em>.<sup><a href="#ref36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref36">36</a>,<a href="#ref37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref37">37</a></sup> A dynamic form of DDA acquisition is the
TopN scan, whereby the <em>N</em> most intense precursor ions
(e.g., <em>N</em> = 5, 10, or 20) in an MS<sup>1</sup> scan
are selectively fragmented one at a time and the MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum
of each is recorded.<sup><a href="#ref28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref28">28</a>,<a href="#ref38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref38">38</a>,<a href="#ref39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref39">39</a></sup> An advantage of DDA is that it produces relatively clean MS<sup>2</sup> spectra; however, spectral interferences from cofragmentation
of isobaric substances (e.g., <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> ±0.2 Da<sup><a href="#ref40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref40">40</a></sup>) may still occur.<sup><a href="#ref41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref41">41</a>,<a href="#ref42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref42">42</a></sup> Additionally, low-abundance MS<sup>1</sup> peaks are inherently
unlikely to yield corresponding MS<sup>2</sup> spectra in the TopN
approach, particularly in complex samples.<sup><a href="#ref41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref41">41</a>,<a href="#ref43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref43">43</a>−<a href="#ref45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref45">45</a></sup> Due to differences in electrospray ionization efficiency
among different chemicals, low peak intensities do not always represent
low concentrations in the sample,<sup><a href="#ref46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref46">46</a></sup> and
low concentrations of highly toxic compounds may be relevant. Thus,
an alternative approach for MS<sup>2</sup> spectral acquisition is
DIA, whereby all precursor ions in a specified mass range(s) are fragmented
simultaneously,<sup><a href="#ref47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref47">47</a></sup> and deconvolution software
can group MS<sup>2</sup> fragments together with their MS<sup>1</sup> precursor ion. DIA data can be acquired by (1) <em>full-scan
DIA</em>, fragmenting all precursor ions within one MS<sup>2</sup> scan, (2) <em>windowed DIA</em>, fragmenting all precursor
ions within specified isolation windows (fixed or variable, narrow
or wide, overlapping or not), or (3) various <em>unconventional
DIA methods</em>.<sup><a href="#ref48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref48">48</a></sup> Although DIA can
be more comprehensive for molecular coverage, in practice a limitation
may be the utter complexity of the resulting raw MS<sup>2</sup> data
which may not be adequately deconvoluted in data-processing stages.<sup><a href="#ref45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref45">45</a>,<a href="#ref49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref49">49</a></sup></p>
<p>In this work, we compare the performance of DDA (<em>N</em> = 10) and DIA (five variable isolation windows) for detection and
accurate annotation of a diverse set of 191 reference chemicals spiked
to increasingly complex water samples, and examine the subsequent
performance of these data sets for MS2Tox LC<sub>50</sub> prediction
in environmental samples. Based on the findings, we furthermore introduce
a new parameter in MS2Tox, the CombinedConfidence score, as a relative
measure of confidence in LC<sub>50</sub> predictions for unknown molecular
features. Finally, we apply MS2Tox and CombinedConfidence scores to
prioritize potentially ecotoxic substances in environmental samples
analyzed by both DDA and DIA.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Water Samples and Analytical Standards</h3>
<p>Four water types
were spiked with the same mixture of 216 analytical reference chemicals,
including e.g. PFASs, surfactants, pharmaceuticals, industrial chemicals,
and pesticides, with a wide range of physical properties (e.g., predicted
log <em>P</em> values from −4.8 to 7.9<sup><a href="#ref50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref50">50</a>,<a href="#ref51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref51">51</a></sup>). The detailed list of reference chemicals, sampling locations,
and detailed sampling procedures are given in the Supporting Information
(SI; <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Tables S1 and S2</a>). The water types
included LC-MS water (Optima grade, Fisher Chemical), groundwater,
surface water, and wastewater collected in Sweden. Samples were collected
in solvent-cleaned borosilicate flasks in volumes ranging from 40
to 80 mL. The groundwater consisted of pooled grab samples collected
in Kalmar, Uppsala, and Västmanlands counties during the spring
2021. Surface water was collected as a grab sample in Lake Mälaren
in Stockholm county in autumn 2022. Incoming wastewater was sampled
upstream of a major wastewater treatment plant in Stockholm county,
representative of household wastewater, and was collected as a weekly
composite sample in summer 2021. Transport blank water was 80 mL of
the LC-MS water in a precleaned borosilicate flask, taken to the field
during sampling and returned and stored with the environmental samples.</p></section><section id="sec2.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample Preparation</h3>
<p>Briefly, transport blank and environmental
samples were spiked with 28 isotopically labeled internal standards
(200 ng/L, <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S2</a>), filtered (0.2 μm,
Sartorius Minisart, 15 mm), and transferred to 10 mL glass injection
vials. To create spiked environmental samples, a mixture of 216 reference
chemicals was added to the samples (100 ng/L each, <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S1</a>), and a corresponding unspiked sample was also kept
for each water type. Finally, all samples were spiked with two isotopically
labeled injection internal standards (200 ng/L, <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S2</a>). The LC-MS water was prepared in the same way as
environmental water types, but without the filtering step. A corresponding
instrumental blank sample was prepared with 10 mL of LC-MS water in
an injection vial.</p></section><section id="sec2.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Instrumental Analysis</h3>
<p>Water analysis was by online
solid-phase extraction (SPE)-LC-HRMS by a modified protocol of Bonnefille
et al.<sup><a href="#ref14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref14">14</a></sup> (detailed procedures in the <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">SI</a>). Each sample was injected three times in
each ionization mode, in randomized order, except wastewater samples,
which were injected at the end of the sequence to avoid any possible
cross contamination. Samples were first injected (1 mL) to the online
SPE column (Waters Oasis HLB, 15 μm, 2.1 × 20 mm, 80 Å)
and subsequently backflushed to a Waters Acquity UPLC BEH C18 analytical
column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm, 130 Å) with corresponding
guard column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 5 mm, 130 Å). Analytical
separation was achieved by gradient elution ultrahigh-pressure liquid
chromatography (Ultimate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a flow
rate of 0.4 mL/min using mobile phases (A) 1 mM ammonium fluoride
in water and (B) 100% methanol.</p>
<p>HRMS data were acquired by a
Q Exactive Orbitrap HF-X (Thermo Fischer Scientific) operating in
electrospray ionization mode. Samples were analyzed in positive and
negative ionization modes in separate injections. For the DIA method,
acquisition alternated between six scan events, first an MS<sup>1</sup> full-scan (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 90–1050
Da; 120,000 resolution, fwhm at 200 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>), followed by five MS<sup>2</sup> DIA scans with isolation windows
of variable precursor <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> ranges
(30,000 resolution, fwhm at 200 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>). Variable sized DIA windows account for uneven precursor ion densities,<sup><a href="#ref52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref52">52</a></sup> and a similar number of windows has shown improved
annotation rates in similar water matrices compared to a larger number
of DIA acquisition windows.<sup><a href="#ref53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref53">53</a></sup> In DDA, the
HRMS alternated between an MS<sup>1</sup> full-scan (identical to
DIA method), and a Top10 MS<sup>2</sup> program (30,000 resolution,
fwhm at 200 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>, 10 s dynamic exclusion).
A retention time index solution was injected three times per ionization
mode for each acquisition method batch.<sup><a href="#ref54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref54">54</a></sup></p></section><section id="sec2.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data Processing with MS-DIAL</h3>
<p>For peak picking, data
alignment, and blank filtering in DIA and DDA, in addition to MS<sup>2</sup> deconvolution for DIA data, all raw data were processed using
MS-DIAL v4.80.<sup><a href="#ref49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref49">49</a>,<a href="#ref55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref55">55</a></sup> Similar parameters were applied
for both DDA and DIA (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S3</a>). Individual
sample types (injected in triplicate), along with blank samples (injected
in triplicate), were preprocessed in separate batches to minimize
false positives in gap-filling steps. To minimize false positives,
only features detected in each triplicate injection per sample type
were kept in subsequent data analysis. For further data processing,
only those ions assigned as molecular ions ([M + H]<sup>+</sup>, [M
– H]<sup>−</sup>) or sodium adducts ([M + Na]<sup>+</sup>) in MS-DIAL were used for comparison of the methods. Retention times
of the spiked reference standards were recorded by manual inspection
of spiked LC-MS grade water chromatograms. Features representing the
spiked chemicals were extracted from MS-DIAL output using R (v4.2.2)
based on criteria of retention time (±30 s) and MS<sup>1</sup> precursor <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> error (&lt;5 ppm).</p></section><section id="sec2.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data Processing with SIRIUS+CSI:FingerID and R</h3>
<p>To evaluate
MS<sup>2</sup> spectral quality, MS<sup>2</sup> data for spiked reference
standards were structurally annotated using SIRIUS+CSI:FingerID (v5.6.2
and v5.6.3).<sup><a href="#ref19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref19">19</a>,<a href="#ref56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref56">56</a></sup> This software was also used to
output molecular fingerprints from the MS<sup>2</sup> spectra for
LC<sub>50</sub> predictions. LC<sub>50</sub> values were predicted
using the function <em>FishLC50Prediction(LC50mode=”static”)</em> in R-package MS2Tox version 0.3.2.<sup><a href="#ref32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref32">32</a></sup> With
this function, LC<sub>50</sub> values are predicted in log-mM units
and were converted to mg/L using the molecular mass of the predicted
formula. A new confidence score, CombinedConfidence, was calculated
by averaging scaled values (range 1–5) of absoluteMassErrorPrecursor,
TopCSIDcore, and ConfidenceScore from SIRIUS+CSI:FingerID; this was
used to describe the confidence of predicted LC<sub>50</sub>s. Predicted
molecular formulas without the scores (e.g. ConfidenceScore is assigned
only to first rank structure in SIRIUS+CSI:FingerID) were assigned
a score equal to the lowest score in this data set.</p></section><section id="sec2.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Annotation of Prioritized Features</h3>
<p>For the case study,
high-confidence potentially toxic molecular features in the spiked
environmental samples having a MS2Tox predicted LC<sub>50</sub> &lt;
−2 log-mM, and a CombinedConfidence score &gt;3, were prioritized
for annotation. As a comparison, high-confidence features predicted
to have low toxicity were also selected using criteria of MS2Tox predicted
LC<sub>50</sub> &gt; 0 log-mM and CombinedConfidence &gt;3. After
prioritization,
the features were manually investigated, and integrated noise (i.e.,
false peaks) or impurities of spiked chemicals were removed from the
prioritization list by comparison to corresponding unspiked samples
of the same water type. MS2Tox predictions for target analytes (<em>N</em> = 216; <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S1</a>) were additionally
performed in the unspiked samples for the case study, following the
same procedure as described for the spiked samples. For feature annotations
at confidence level 2a, according to confidence levels of Schymanski
et al.,<sup><a href="#ref57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref57">57</a></sup> spectral library matching to
MassBank Europe<sup><a href="#ref18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref18">18</a>,<a href="#ref58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref58">58</a></sup> was performed in MS-DIAL (v.
4.80, Mass tolerance 0.002 Da in MS<sup>1</sup> and 0.005 in MS<sup>2</sup>, 60% identification score cutoff). All probable structures
(level 2a) were further evaluated for supporting evidence using the
retention time index, and only structures falling within box1 (”candidate
accepted”)<sup><a href="#ref54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref54">54</a></sup> were considered for
the case study. For evaluating MS2Tox predicted toxicities for level
2a annotated structures, 96 h LC<sub>50</sub> values of fathead minnow
were predicted from the structure with the CompTox Chemicals Dashboard
prediction tool (v2.2.1).</p></section><section id="sec2.7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data and Code Availability</h3>
<p>R-code for MS2Tox, using
command line for SIRIUS+CSI:FingerID calculations, and ConfidenceScore
are available in MS2Tox GitHub (<a href="https://github.com/kruvelab/MS2Tox" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://github.com/kruvelab/MS2Tox</a>). Tables with predicted toxicities and formulas for acquired features
are presented in the <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">SI</a>.</p></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results and Discussion</h2>
<section id="sec3.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">MS<sup>1</sup> and MS<sup>2</sup> Feature Detection Rates in
DDA and DIA</h3>
<p>Detection rates by DDA and DIA MS<sup>2</sup> acquisition methods were evaluated based on detection of spiked
chemicals in MS<sup>1</sup> and MS<sup>2</sup>, as well as the total
number of nontargeted features detected in water types of increasing
complexity. Of the 216 spiked chemicals, 191 were recovered by the
online SPE, and thus only this subset of reference chemicals was considered
in the comparison of DDA and DIA (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S1</a>). After preprocessing, data filtering, and blank filtering in MS-DIAL,
161 MS<sup>1</sup> precursor ions out of the subset of 191 reference
chemicals were detected at the correct retention times by both DDA
and DIA methods for the clean LC-MS water matrix. Thus, some analytes
were lost in the data processing steps, such as by removal through
blank filtering or incorrect adduct assignment by MS-DIAL. For example,
in positive ionization mode the [M + H]<sup>+</sup> precursor ion
of memantine was detected but was assigned as the [M + NH<sub>4</sub>]<sup>+</sup> adduct in MS-DIAL, in both DIA and DDA acquisition
methods.</p>
<p>In spiked LC-MS water, groundwater, surface water,
and wastewater we detected 1889, 5453, 5709, and 19166 MS<sup>1</sup> features, respectively, with the DIA method (both ionization modes).
With DDA, we detected 2349, 4681, 5129, and 14530 MS<sup>1</sup> features,
respectively. Considering spiked chemicals, fewer reference chemicals
were detected in MS<sup>1</sup> with increased sample complexity by
both DDA and DIA (i.e., clean water &gt; groundwater &gt; surface
water
&gt; wastewater), but the diminished performance was more pronounced
for DDA, and DIA resulted in more detections in MS<sup>1</sup> in
the environmental samples (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>). Considering all nontarget features and spiked reference
chemicals together, overall a lower number of MS<sup>1</sup> features
was detected in environmental samples by DDA compared to DIA (75.6%
to 90.0% of total feature count in DIA; <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S4</a>). One potential explanation for this was the inherently
longer scan cycle time of DDA (550 ms) than DIA (300 ms); however,
the number of scans per peak for spiked chemicals were satisfactory
for both MS<sup>2</sup> acquisition methods in all water matrices,
generally exceeding 12 scans per peak for a 12 s wide peak.</p>

chemicals (59.2–74.9%) by DIA (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>B), depending on sample complexity. In comparison

water matrix studied, the LC-MS water, we observed that for the reference
chemicals with an MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum acquired only by DIA (<em>N</em> = 10), these had two orders of magnitude lower median
peak area (4.5 × 10<sup>6</sup>) compared to reference chemicals
with an MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum acquired by both approaches (<em>N</em> = 151; 3.1 × 10<sup>8</sup>) or only DDA (<em>N</em> = 5; 6.7 × 10<sup>8</sup>). In surface water, however,
the observed median peak areas were comparable. On the other hand,
we observed a shorter median retention time for the chemicals having
an MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum that was only acquired by DIA (<em>N</em> = 47; 7.5 min) compared to those with MS<sup>2</sup> acquired
by both methods (<em>N</em> = 88; 10.9 min).</p>
<p>Considering
MS<sup>1</sup> and MS<sup>2</sup> data overall, our
results confirm that DIA gave higher detection rates than DDA for
nontargeted acquisition in complex environmental samples. Although
direct comparison with previously published nontargeted water analysis
is impossible, similar trends have been observed in the metabolomes
and exposomes. Barbier Saint Hilaire et al.<sup><a href="#ref60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref60">60</a></sup> reported a similar trend comparing DDA and DIA, whereby DIA resulted
in fragmentation spectra of all 72 targeted low-mass metabolites in
human serum, but only 61% of these had fragmentation spectra by DDA.
Additionally, Wu et al.<sup><a href="#ref61" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref61">61</a></sup> could identify
66 of 68 veterinary drug residues in aquacultured eel with more than
one product ion with DIA, compared to only 48 with target DDA, due
to the absence of recorded MS<sup>2</sup> spectra.</p></section><section id="sec3.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Performance of DDA and DIA for Annotation of Structure and Formula</h3>
<p>To compare the performance of DDA and DIA in formula and structural
annotations, we evaluated the true positive rates (TPRs) of the first
ranked formula as well as structural annotations using SIRIUS+CSI:FingerID
and spectral library matching to MassBank Europe. The TPRs were calculated
as the proportion of the 191 spiked reference chemicals with a correct
first-ranked formula and first-ranked structure annotated.</p>
<p>Considering
first-ranked formula assignments (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>, orange bars named CorrectFormulaRank1),
DDA and DIA resulted in TPRs of 59.2% and 46.1% in LC-MS water, respectively,
suggesting a higher quality of MS<sup>2</sup> spectra by DDA. However,
higher TPRs were evident for DIA than for DDA in all environmental
waters. Specifically, TPRs in DIA were 33.0%, 29.3%, and 27.7% in
groundwater, surface water, and wastewater, respectively, compared
to 29.3%, 23.0%, and 20.9% in DDA (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>C). Additionally, we observed lower TPRs
among halogenated chemicals in formula predictions with SIRIUS. Here,
20 out of 36 reference chemicals that never yielded correct first-ranked
formula predictions in both DDA and DIA were halogenated, including
12 that contained fluorine, such as the PFASs (<em>N</em> =
9) and fluorinated pharmaceuticals (<em>N</em> = 3).</p>
<p>For accurate <em>in silico</em> structural annotations
with SIRIUS+CSI:FingerID, DDA generally had higher TPRs than DIA despite
the fact that DDA yielded lower numbers of detected reference chemicals
with MS<sup>2</sup>, suggesting increased MS<sup>2</sup> spectral
quality by DDA (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>D). More specifically, with DDA data from analysis of LC-MS water,
groundwater, surface water, and wastewater, the correct first-ranked
structures were annotated for 119 (TPR 62.3%), 63 (33.0%), 56 (29.3%),
and 37 (19.4%) reference chemicals, respectively. Correspondingly
in DIA, correct first-ranked structures were annotated for 95 (TPR
49.7%), 56 (29.3%), 51 (26.7%), and 37 (19.4%) with the DIA data (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>D). Nijssen et al.<sup><a href="#ref62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref62">62</a></sup> obtained similar results, attributed to higher
MS<sup>2</sup> spectra quality for DDA data, when comparing true positive
annotations of 32 veterinary drugs and pesticides spiked in solvent
and animal feed extract testing four different annotation tools (one
mass spectral library, three <em>in silico</em> tools). Nevertheless,
in wastewater, the most complex water samples analyzed here, the TPR
was the same (19.4%) for DDA and DIA, owing to the trade-offs of method
detection sensitivity and MS<sup>2</sup> spectral quality by the two
protocols. Comparison of structural annotation accuracy by SIRIUS
for positive and negative ionization modes reveal only minor differences
for LC-MS water, independent of the MS<sup>2</sup> acquisition mode.
However, a tendency of lower TPRs were observed for the spiked chemicals
in the environmental samples in the negative ionization mode, compared
to the positive ionization mode. The divergence is especially noticeable
for the DDA method. For example, in surface water, an MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum was acquired for 56 compounds in positive mode and 51 in
negative mode, while correct rank 1 structure annotations were observed
for 42 and 21 compounds, respectively.</p>
<p>Regarding the performance
of MS<sup>2</sup> spectral library matching,
higher TPRs were observed for DIA in all water sample types, with
decreasing TPRs with increasing sample complexities. In LC-MS water,
groundwater, surface water, and wastewater, 138 (TPR 72.3%), 88 (46.1%),
81 (42.2%), and 65 (34.0%) spiked reference chemicals were correctly
matched to MassBank Europe by DDA, respectively (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S5</a>). In DIA, the corresponding numbers were 139 (TPR
72.8%), 98 (51.3%), 96 (50.3%), and 77 (40.3%). Higher TPRs in <em>in silico</em> formula assignments and empirical spectral library
matching by DIA in environmental matrices suggest that these approaches
are less sensitive to MS<sup>2</sup> spectral interference than <em>in silico</em> structural assignment, and that overall DIA performance
benefited from the higher detection rates of the spiked reference
chemicals. However, spectral libraries are biased toward well-known
and frequently monitored chemicals, such as the spiked reference chemicals,
and contain many fewer candidates for matching compared to the more
comprehensive <em>in silico</em> structure libraries, potentially
explaining the differences in TPRs by these two complementary approaches.</p></section><section id="sec3.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Assessment of DDA and DIA MS<sup>2</sup> Spectral Quality</h3>
<p>For evaluation of MS<sup>2</sup> spectral quality, we assessed <em>correct annotation rate</em> (CAR%), comparing the ratio between
correct rank1 structural annotations and the number of spiked chemicals
with an acquired MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum in the samples (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>, dark green bars
named MS<sup>2</sup>), rather than TPR, which considered all 191 detectable
reference chemicals. The DDA method had higher CARs for <em>in
silico</em> structural annotation in SIRIUS, ranging from 52.1%
to 76.3%, compared to 32.7% to 59.0% with DIA (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>D). Additionally, in spectral library matching
the DDA method yielded higher CARs than DIA: 88.5%, 86.3%, 90.0%,
and 91.5% for LC-MS water, groundwater, surface water, and wastewater,
respectively, compared to 86.3%, 68.5%, 71.1% and 68.1%. These data
suggest a higher spectral quality for MS<sup>2</sup> spectra acquired
by DDA, and the same has been suggested earlier by Barbier Saint Hilaire
et al.<sup><a href="#ref60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref60">60</a></sup> based on higher dot products for
DDA spectra matched with in-house reference spectra compared to DIA
spectra. However, it should be kept in mind, as discussed above, higher
TPRs were observed for the DIA method due to the superior detection
rates of MS<sup>2</sup> features, suggesting a balanced performance
in MS<sup>2</sup> acquisition and spectral quality also by DIA.</p></section><section id="sec3.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Comparative Performance of MS2Tox with DDA and DIA</h3>
<p>MS2Tox predicts fish LC<sub>50</sub> for detected features based
on MS<sup>1</sup> and MS<sup>2</sup> spectral data, independent of
structural identification. Nevertheless, MS2Tox depends on input of
accurate molecular fingerprints predicted from formula and MS<sup>2</sup> spectra with SIRIUS+CSI:FingerID. Thus, here we compared
predicted LC<sub>50</sub> values from MS2Tox based on MS<sup>2</sup> data acquired here by DIA and DDA to the LC<sub>50</sub> values
predicted by MS2Tox based on the correct structures. Note that the
predicted LC<sub>50</sub> values were not compared to experimental
LC<sub>50</sub> values because only 35 of 191 spiked reference chemicals
had experimental LC<sub>50</sub> values in CompTox<sup><a href="#ref63" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref63">63</a></sup> databases. The accuracy of MS2Tox has been previously shown
by Peets et al.<sup><a href="#ref32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref32">32</a></sup> and thus the usage of
predicted toxicity values from known structures allowed us to utilize
other reference compounds besides the 35 in the comparative analysis
of DDA and DIA. Figures comparing predicted toxicity values and experimental
values are shown in SI <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.est.4c02833/suppl_file/es4c02833_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figures S2 and S3</a>.</p>
<p>Due to the higher MS<sup>2</sup> detection rates with DIA,
LC<sub>50</sub> values were predicted by MS2Tox for more LC-HRMS features
with DIA, compared to DDA (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>); note that the number of extracted features was higher
than the number of detectable spiked reference standards, due to some
chemicals being detected in both positive and negative modes as well
as detection as adducts. The LC<sub>50</sub> values predicted from
DDA spectra had somewhat lower RMSE (0.62 to 0.76 log-mM depending
on the matrix) compared to DIA (0.71–0.80 log-mM), suggesting
higher fingerprint accuracy in DDA. Manually checking spiked reference
chemicals detected in groundwater in positive ionization mode showed
that precursor ion peak areas were lower for those only detected by
DIA (<em>N</em> = 17, median 1.38 × 10<sup>7</sup>) compared
to those detected only by the DDA method (<em>N</em> = 7,
median 1.38 × 10<sup>9</sup>). Lower precursor ion intensities
could lead to poorer MS<sup>2</sup> quality<sup><a href="#ref47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref47">47</a></sup> and might explain the somewhat higher RMSE by DIA. RMSE values for
LC<sub>50</sub> predicted from MS<sup>2</sup> spectra were below 0.97
log-mM for both correctly and incorrectly predicted formulas with
both acquisition methods (<a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>). However, we observed a lower correlation between
LC<sub>50</sub> values predicted based on MS<sup>2</sup> and structure
when incorrect formula was assigned, compared to when the correct
formula was assigned (<a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>). This was expected because molecular formula annotation
affects the fragmentation tree, and thus the molecular fingerprint
predicted with SIRIUS+CSI:FingerID. The issue of significant errors
in toxicity predictions, despite correct chemical formula annotations,
can be attributed to the incomplete MS<sup>2</sup> (tandem mass spectrometry)
data, which can lead to inaccurate fragmentation trees and, consequently,
molecular fingerprints. For instance, one of the highest errors observed
was for the spiked chemical 2,6-di-<em>tert</em>-butyl-4-(hydroxymethyl)phenol
in DIA mode. While SIRIUS correctly assigned the chemical formula
C<sub>15</sub>H<sub>24</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, the cosine similarity between
SIRIUS predicted fingerprint and correct structure fingerprint was
only 0.33, indicating that MS<sup>2</sup> data were insufficient for
correct fingerprint prediction. This is also reflected in the fact
that the top1 candidate structure was 2,2-bis(4-oxocyclohexyl)propane.
Despite sharing the same chemical formula, these two compounds have
vastly different structures. The spiked chemical possesses an aromatic
ring with hydroxy groups, whereas the predicted structure features
nonaromatic rings and ketone groups. This example highlights how incorrect
toxicity predictions can arise even when the chemical formula is correctly
assigned. The primary issue is the accuracy of the predicted molecular
fingerprints, possibly due to incomplete fragmentation data.</p>

</div></div></section></section><section id="sec3.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">CombinedConfidence Score: Evaluation of MS2Tox Prediction Reliability</h3>
<p>For the discussion above, the true formula and structure of the
spiked chemicals were always known; however, in real-world analysis
of environmental samples it would be useful to accompany the MS2Tox
LC<sub>50</sub> predictions with a relative measure of confidence.
SIRIUS+CSI:FingerID reports several scores or values that can be useful
in this regard: for example, the mass error between theoretical mass
of predicted formula and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> of
detected peak, as well as a SiriusScore, which is used to rank the
predicted formulas. In this work, mass accuracy of precursor <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>, ConfidenceScore, and topCSIscore were
evaluated and combined into a combined confidence score (CombinedConfidence)
to accompany predicted LC<sub>50</sub> values from MS2Tox. CombinedConfidence
scores were observed to be generally higher for correctly assigned
formulas than for incorrectly assigned formulas (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>E–H), and CombinedConfidence
yielded a clear separation of correct and incorrect formula predictions.</p>
<p>CombinedConfidence scores (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>E–H) can also be used to evaluate reliability
of the LC<sub>50</sub> predictions for unknown features. Molecular
features with high CombinedConfidence, and higher toxicity (upper
left corner in each panel of <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>E–H) can be prioritized for structural annotation
with higher reliability. In contrast, the upper right corners of these
plots contain features considered to have low toxicity, with high
confidence, and thus are less likely to contribute to toxicity of
the water samples. Features with low CombinedConfidence scores should
be treated with caution and cannot necessarily be ruled out as high
or low toxicity features.</p></section><section id="sec3.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Prioritization of Toxic Compounds in Environmental Water Samples</h3>
<p>As a proof of concept, MS2Tox was applied to prioritize nontargeted
features in the spiked environmental samples based on the predicted
fish LC<sub>50</sub>. LC<sub>50</sub> values were predicted with MS2Tox
for all features assigned by MS-DIAL as [M + H]<sup>+</sup>, [M +
Na]<sup>+</sup>, or [M – H]<sup>−</sup> that had SIRIUS
predicted fingerprint from MS<sup>2</sup> data. Altogether 936, 1034,
and 4486 LC<sub>50</sub> values were predicted from DDA data, whereas
3605, 3650, and 13094 LC<sub>50</sub> values were predicted from DIA
data for groundwater, surface water, and wastewater, respectively.
Out of these, 791, 795, and 4185 features from DIA overlapped with
724, 740, and 3807 features from DDA, with a retention time difference
of &lt;0.5 min and precursor <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> difference of &lt;5 ppm and matched assigned adduct. The nonmatching
numbers across the two acquisition methods here are explained by multiple
matches per feature with the automatic workflow and set criteria.
All overlapping features are compared based on their predicted LC<sub>50</sub> values. Features with CombinedConfidence scores exceeding
3 generally had better agreement in toxicity values between the two
acquisition modes (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>A), suggesting higher agreement of molecular fingerprint predicted
with SIRIUS for higher confidence features.</p>

<p>To assess the performance of the MS2Tox fish LC<sub>50</sub> predictions
among detected features, we applied CompTox to predict the fish LC<sub>50</sub> values from the structure as comparison for the probable
structures (<em>N</em> = 13, level 2a) and detected reference
chemicals (<em>N</em> = 7, <a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">Table <a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>). Out of these, vicenin 2 (level 2a), annotated
in wastewater by DDA, was assigned the most toxic fish LC<sub>50</sub> (0.17 mg/L) by MS2Tox. Vicenin 2 is a natural product in plants
and has also shown to possess a variety of pharmacological activities.<sup><a href="#ref65" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref65">65</a>−<a href="#ref68" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref68">68</a></sup> No LC<sub>50</sub> values are reported or could be predicted in
CompTox, but the corresponding MS2Tox predicted toxicity based on

between MS2Tox and CompTox predicted fish LC<sub>50</sub> values (Wilcoxon

for one outlying annotation, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-proline (<a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">Table <a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>). Excluding <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-proline

has been reported to ionize more efficiently in positive ionization

that the structural annotation was incorrect leading to a biased CompTox
prediction, rather than a poor toxicity prediction. For other chemicals
with divergent predicted toxicity values, no underlying structural
or functional similarities were identified that could account for
the observed differences.</p>

between DDA and DIA acquisition methods can be observed.</p></section><section id="sec3.7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Implications of Our Findings</h3>
<p>To conclude, DDA MS<sup>2</sup> spectra generally had higher correct annotation rates (i.e.,
ratio between correct rank1 annotations and number of reference chemicals
with acquired MS<sup>2</sup> spectra) in structural annotations using
SIRIUS:CSI:FingerID and spectral library matching, suggesting better
MS<sup>2</sup> spectral quality compared to DIA. Additionally, lower
RMSE for LC<sub>50</sub> values predicted from DDA spectra suggest
higher fingerprint accuracy from DDA spectra, compared to DIA spectra.
Although MS<sup>2</sup> spectral quality was shown to be an important
factor for the accuracy of toxicity predictions, DIA advantages cannot
be ignored, as DIA yields higher MS<sup>2</sup> detection rates for
targeted and nontargeted features. In combination with satisfactory
MS<sup>2</sup> spectral quality, this led to a higher true positive
rate in DIA (i.e., percentage of correct rank1 annotations among 191
reference chemicals) of formula predictions and spectral library matches
than in DDA. MS2Tox can successfully be applied to prioritize potentially
toxic molecules in environmental samples; however, it should be applied
with understanding of limitations as with other <em>in silico</em> prediction tools. Nevertheless, MS2Tox relies on correct first-ranked
formula predictions from SIRIUS, affecting the accuracy of LC<sub>50</sub> predictions. To mitigate this limitation, the new CombinedConfidence
score gives the user an indication of the fingerprint accuracy. In
terms of MS2Tox, the formula derived from the first-rank structure
could be utilized instead of the first-rank formula. Future research
could focus on predicting toxicity directly from MS<sup>2</sup> data,
eliminating the need for SIRIUS software.</p>
<p>Separate data preprocessing
of individual samples poses a limitation to fully benefiting from
the nontargeted data processing workflows. For example, processing
more samples together would likely enhance MS<sup>2</sup> spectral
quality because the sample having the highest peak intensity (and
thus higher spectral quality) would be used for the MS<sup>2</sup> deconvolution. Similarly, data alignment and gap filling among multiple
samples could increase the number of features with an acquired MS<sup>2</sup>, and thereby broaden the range of considered chemicals. Nevertheless,
here separate data processing of samples was necessary to compare
the MS<sup>2</sup> acquisition performances in water samples of variable
complexities. For future applications of nontarget workflows with
MS2Tox feature prioritization, iterative DDA workflows can potentially
be applied for comprehensive high-quality MS<sup>2</sup> data acquisition
for a small number of samples or pooled samples. However, due to long
analysis time with repeated measurements per sample, we argue that
it is beneficial to apply DIA workflows followed by spectral deconvolution
for a nonbiased comprehensive MS<sup>2</sup> acquisition for a large
number of samples. It is therefore essential to optimize DIA and DDA
workflows to ensure higher MS<sup>2</sup> quality, both <em>in
operando</em> (i.e., instrumental parameters) and <em>in silico</em> (i.e., spectral deconvolution), to ensure higher accuracies in both
structural annotations and toxicity predictions.</p></section></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
