</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p><sup>15</sup>N longitudinal relaxation rates are extensively used for the characterization of protein dynamics; however, their accurate measurement is hindered by systematic errors. <sup>15</sup>N CSA/<sup>1</sup>H–<sup>15</sup>N dipolar cross-correlated relaxation (CC) and amide proton exchange saturation transfer from water protons are the two main sources of systematic errors in the determination of <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates through <sup>1</sup>H–<sup>15</sup>N HSQC-based experiments. CC is usually suppressed through a train of 180° proton pulses applied during the variable <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period (T), which can perturb water magnetization. Thus CC cancellation is required in such a way as to minimize water saturation effects. Here we examined the level of water saturation during the T period caused by various types of inversion proton pulses to suppress CC: (I) amide-selective IBURP-2; (II) cosine-modulated IBURP-2; (III) Watergate-like blocks; and (IV) non-selective hard. We additionally demonstrate the effect of uncontrolled saturation of aliphatic protons on <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates. In this paper we present an optimized pulse sequence that takes into account the crucial effect of controlling also the saturation of the aliphatic protons during <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> measurements in non-deuterated proteins. We show that using cosine-modulated IBURP-2 pulses spaced 40 ms to cancel CC in this optimized pulse program is the method of choice to minimize systematic errors coming from water and aliphatic protons saturation effects.</p>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Electronic supplementary material</h3>
<p>The online version of this article (doi:10.1007/s10858-015-9937-4) contains supplementary material, which is available to authorized users.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> <sup>15</sup>N relaxation, Longitudinal relaxation, R<sub>1</sub>, Water saturation, <sup>15</sup>N CSA/<sup>1</sup>H–<sup>15</sup>N dipolar cross-correlated relaxation (CC), Radiation damping (RD)</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>The intrinsic dynamic properties of proteins play a key role in their function. Information about dynamics on several timescales can be studied through solution NMR spectroscopy using <sup>15</sup>N spin relaxation experiments on <sup>15</sup>N-labeled protein samples. Longitudinal relaxation rate R<sub>1</sub>, transverse relaxation rate R<sub>2</sub>, and <sup>15</sup>N–<sup>1</sup>H steady-state NOE parameters of backbone <sup>15</sup>N nuclei measured at various magnetic fields are commonly used to address global and local protein dynamics at ps–ns and μs–ms timescales (Torchia <a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">2011</a>).</p>
<p>Quantitative analysis of backbone dynamics requires accurate and consistent relaxation measurements (Morin <a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">2011</a>). It has long been recognized that systematic errors in the measurements of <sup>15</sup>N relaxation rates R<sub>1</sub>, R<sub>2</sub> and <sup>15</sup>N–<sup>1</sup>H NOE can be significant and far larger than random errors. Two main sources of these errors have been identified as amide proton exchange saturation transfer from water protons (Grzesiek and Bax <a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">1993</a>; Farrow et al. <a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">1994</a>; Ferrage et al. <a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">2010</a>; Chen and Tjandra <a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2011</a>; Lakomek et al. <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">2012</a>; Jurt and Zerbe <a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">2012</a>) on the one hand, and <sup>15</sup>N CSA/<sup>1</sup>H–<sup>15</sup>N dipolar cross-correlated (CC) relaxation (Boyd et al. <a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1990</a>; Kay et al. <a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">1992a</a>; Palmer et al. <a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">1992</a>; Kumar et al. <a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">2000</a>; Gong and Ishima <a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">2007</a>; Ferrage et al. <a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">2009</a>; Ishima <a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">2014</a>) on the other.</p>
<p>Water saturation has a strong effect on <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> measurements (Chen and Tjandra <a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2011</a>). It can arise as a consequence of improper handling of water magnetization effects of RF pulses and/or pulsed field gradients (PFG) during the whole pulse program (Stonehouse et al. <a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">1994</a>). Saturation can be transferred through proton–proton exchange or via NOE to exchangeable amide protons—directly detected in HSQC-based R<sub>1</sub> measurements—during the inter-scan delay and it distorts proton amide signal intensities. In addition, varying degrees of water saturation during variable <sup>15</sup>N magnetization decay periods can alter the intensity decay, thus causing systematic errors in R<sub>1</sub> relaxation rates (Lakomek et al. <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">2012</a>). Cryogenic probeheads (Kovacs et al. <a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">2005</a>) exacerbate water suppression-related problems by strong radiation damping (RD) effects (Krishnan and Murali <a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">2013</a>) especially at high magnetic fields (Shishmarev and Otting <a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">2011</a>).</p>
<p>Another important source of systematic errors on R<sub>1</sub> measurements is longitudinal CC between <sup>1</sup>H–<sup>15</sup>N DD and <sup>15</sup>N CSA. The CSA component increases also with magnetic fields. Insufficient cancellation of cross-correlation effects at very high fields is reported to generate significant deviations in <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates for deuterated proteins (Ishima <a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">2014</a>). CC is typically suppressed by applying a series of proton 180° pulses during the variable <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period (Kay et al. <a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">1992a</a>). However, these pulses can perturb water magnetization if care is not taken to prevent it. In addition, they can cause unwanted saturation effects on protein protons. In particular, saturation of aliphatic protons in non-deuterated proteins may cause significant errors on <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> measurements, a phenomenon that has not been properly addressed so far.</p>
<p>In this paper we present an optimized method to determine <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates on non-deuterated proteins, which can be used at high fields using cryogenic probes, in order to address the problems described above. The optimized sequence relies on the control of both water and aliphatic protons saturation while CC-suppressing pulses are applied during the variable <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Experimental section</h2>
<p>Our study was carried out with a fully protonated <sup>15</sup>N-labeled sample of the small and highly stable third Igg-binding domain of protein G (GB3).</p>
<p>The NMR sample consisted of 1.5 mM <sup>15</sup>N-labeled GB3 in 25 mM phosphate buffer, 25 mM NaCl, 0.02 % NaN<sub>3</sub> and 10 % D<sub>2</sub>O, pH 6.5. <sup>15</sup>N longitudinal relaxation experiments were performed on a 600 MHz Bruker Avance III and an 800 MHz Bruker DRX NMR spectrometer operating at 60.79 and 81.06 MHz <sup>15</sup>N resonance frequencies, respectively. Both instruments were equipped with TCI cryogenic probes and a Z-gradient coil. Data were recorded at 298 K.</p>
<p>We used 1D versions of the pulse scheme shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a> to measure the water signal intensity—reporting the level of non-saturated water (M<sub><em>nsw</em></sub>)—using a short angle readout pulse. Readout pulses were introduced just before the <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period T (point <em>a</em>) and at the start of the regular <sup>1</sup>H acquisition time (point <em>b</em>). Experiments were carried out at 600 and 800 MHz. Simultaneous phase cycling (0°, 180°) of the receiver and the readout pulse allowed the cancelation of the protein signal. Readout pulses shorter than 1° were used in order to avoid artifacts caused by RD. The resulting water signal was integrated (I<sup>x</sup>) in the region 6.5–2.9 ppm (spectral width of 3.65 ppm which corresponds to a range equal to 50 times the observed full peak width at half maximum). <section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Exploring the effect of water saturation caused by CC-suppressing pulses on <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> measurements</h3>
<p>Two sources of non-equilibrium water magnetization can be considered in experiments for measuring <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> relaxation in proteins: partially saturated water coming from water magnetization handling before the variable <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period (Chen and Tjandra <a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2011</a>) on one hand, and additional water saturation created as a side effect of the 180° proton pulses used to cancel CC effects during this period (Lakomek et al. <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">2012</a>) on the other.</p>
<p>Preventing water saturation while efficiently suppressing CC is crucial for accurately measuring R<sub>1</sub> rates. The pulse scheme depicted in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a> is designed for this purpose: PFG around 180° pulses during spin-echoes when water is transverse minimize RD; a water-flip-back pulse during the initial INEPT keeps water at +z axis; water magnetization is brought to +z at the start of <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period T; bipolar PFG are used during the second part of t<sub>1</sub> evolution time where water remains at −z axis to reduce RD; and PFG are immediately applied to minimize RD every time water is placed at −z axis.</p>
<p>The pulse sequence in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a> can be divided into two blocks, A and B. Ideally, water magnetization should reach the +z axis just after each of these modules (points <em>a</em>, <em>b</em>) and retain equilibrium magnetization (no saturation). However, as a consequence of imperfections of RF pulses, water dephasing and rephasing caused by PFG, and strong RD effects present at high fields with cryoprobes, the path for water magnetization deviates from this ideal situation and is partially saturated. By comparing water saturation at points <em>a</em> and <em>b</em> in the pulse sequence, we can evaluate the additional water saturation introduced in block B, which includes mostly the effect of the CC-suppression scheme. Since CC-suppression is applied for a variable duration, water saturation can modify the intensity of amide proton decay differently for each value of T and introduce systematic errors in the measurement of <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> relaxation rates.</p>
<p>The most widely used approaches to suppress CC are: (i) amide-selective IBURP-2 pulses (Geen and Freeman <a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">1991</a>), inverting only magnetization of the amide protons (Lakomek et al. <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">2012</a>; Chill et al. <a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">2006</a>); (ii) cosine-modulated selective pulses (Smallcombe <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">1993</a>), exciting both the amide and the aliphatic region of the protein while performing zero excitation at the water resonance (Farrow et al. <a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">1994</a>; Zhu et al. <a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">2000</a>); and (iii) combination of selective and non-selective pulses in a Watergate-like (Piotto et al. <a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">1992</a>) way to selectively invert non-water protons (Chen and Tjandra <a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2011</a>).</p>
<p>Here we evaluated the water saturation level caused by the three CC-suppressing schemes described above using a 1D version of the pulse program depicted in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>. Non-selective hard 180° proton pulses were also included in our study as a reference. Experiments were carried out at 800 and 600 MHz, using TCI cryoprobes with the following proton pulses: amide-selective 180° off-resonance IBURP-2 pulses (I); cosine-modulated 180° IBURP-2 pulses (II); Watergate scheme based on the block 90° water-selective pulse—180° hard pulse—90° water-selective pulse (III); and non-selective 180° hard pulses (IV). Figure <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a> shows the corresponding experimental excitation profiles at 800 MHz. Experimental profiles at 600 MHz are presented in Fig. S1 SM. Selective excitation schemes should minimally affect water magnetization in contrast to the use of hard pulses. However, residual water saturation present after the various schemes is significantly different, in practice.</p>
<p>Water saturation levels were measured after the application of the above mentioned CC-suppressing schemes for short (80 ms) and long (800 ms) <sup>15</sup>N relaxation periods T. In addition, the effect of pulsing rate (R<sub>p</sub>) during cancellation of CC was also studied using two rates: fast pulsing regime (200 s<sup>−1</sup>, proton pulses spaced 5 ms) and slow pulsing regime (25 s<sup>−1</sup>, proton pulses spaced 40 ms). Results are presented in Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>.</p>

<p>Faster pulsing rates increase both the level of water saturation and the difference in saturation levels between short and long <sup>15</sup>N relaxation delays, <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IEq3"><math id="M26" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Δ</mi><msubsup><mtext>M</mtext><mrow><mrow><mtext>sw(800</mtext><mo>-</mo><mn>80</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mtext>b/a</mtext></msubsup></mrow></math></span>. At pulsing rates of R<sub>p</sub> = 200 s<sup>−1</sup>, water saturation was higher for the Watergate scheme than for IBURP-2-based sequences. In addition, a significant increase in the level of saturation was observed between the short and the large relaxation delay when Watergate-like blocks were used to suppress CC (<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IEq4"><math id="M28" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Δ</mi><msubsup><mtext>M</mtext><mrow><mrow><mtext>sw(800</mtext><mo>-</mo><mn>80</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mtext>b/a</mtext></msubsup></mrow></math></span> = 74 % at 600 MHz and 44 % at 800 MHz, see Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>). In contrast, when using IBURP-2-based sequences, water saturation differences between short and large T periods were less important, around 9–16 %.</p>
<p>These results indicate that the use of an inter-pulse delay of 40 ms (R<sub>p</sub> = 25 s<sup>−1</sup>) allows the build-up of a steady-state polarization at the start of each scan, in the three approaches tested. Therefore the effect of water saturation is independent of the <sup>15</sup>N relaxation delay and it should not affect the measured <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates. On the contrary, water saturation increased along this relaxation period when faster pulsing rates were used (R<sub>p</sub> = 200 s<sup>−1</sup>). Because this effect was stronger for Watergate-like than for IBURP-2-based sequences (IBURP-2 or cosine-modulated IBURP-2 pulses), the latter are expected to show better performance at cancelling CC with minimum water saturation on <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> measurements.</p>
<p>To study the impact of the previously CC-suppressing schemes on <sup>15</sup>N longitudinal rates of non-deuterated proteins, eight protocols were compared for the measurement of <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> at 800 and 600 MHz. They included four proton inversion sequences (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a> and Fig. S1 SM) at two pulsing rates R<sub>p</sub> (25 and 200 s<sup>−1</sup>). The individual apparent R<sub>1</sub> rates obtained at R<sub>p</sub> = 25 s<sup>−1</sup> are shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>. The average R<sub>1</sub>, the average pairwise root mean square deviation (<em>rmsd</em>) and the correlation coefficients, R, are presented in Table 1 SM.</p>
<p>Measurements carried out at 600 MHz in minimal water saturation conditions (approaches I, II and III) at slow pulsing rates R<sub>p</sub> = 25 s<sup>−1</sup> resulted in identical R<sub>1</sub> rates for methods II (cosine-modulated IBURP-2) and III (Watergate-like pulses), while systematically shorter R<sub>1</sub> values were obtained for all residues in GB3 when using approach I (IBURP-2), see Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>a. The same effect was observed at 800 MHz (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>b). The differences in R<sub>1</sub> values obtained by methods I and II were small (rmsd = 0.075 s<sup>−1</sup>, 3.15 % at 600 MHz and 0.077 s<sup>−1</sup>, 3.76 % at 800 MHz) but systematic and significant, and larger than the reproducibility of the individual experiments (rmsd = 0.030 s<sup>−1</sup>, 1.2 % for duplicate measurements). R<sub>1</sub> rates measured at 600 MHz using the cosine-modulated IBURP-2 pulses at R<sub>p</sub> = 25 s<sup>−1</sup> (approach II) are in perfect agreement (rmsd = 0.022 s<sup>−1</sup>, 0.9 %, Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>d) with those reported for deuterated GB3<a href="#Fn1" class="usa-link">1</a> at the same field (Lakomek et al. <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">2012</a>). The same happened for the Watergate-like scheme (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>e).</p>
<p>The use of hard 180° proton pulses (approach IV), causing strong water saturation, resulted in clear overestimation of R<sub>1</sub>, at both 25 and 200 s<sup>−1</sup> pulsing rates (Table 1 SM). In addition, errors coming from water saturation effects varied along the protein sequence, being largest in the most solvent-exposed regions of the GB3 protein: residues 15–23 (A region) and 44–55 (B region), Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>a, b. The effect of water saturation can be estimated by the clear correlation between the fractional increase in R<sub>1</sub> rates, [R<sub>1</sub>(IV) − R<sub>1</sub>(II)]/R<sub>1</sub>(II), and the degree of saturation I<sub>sat</sub>/I<sub>0</sub> measured in <sup>1</sup>H–<sup>15</sup>N HSQC spectra (Mori et al. <a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">1995</a>) acquired with and without water presaturation (Fig. S2 SM).</p>
<p>A common alternative to suppress the effect of non-uniform water saturation in R<sub>1</sub> measurements performed with the classical CC-suppressing scheme employing hard 180° pulses (scheme IV, R<sub>p</sub> = 200 s<sup>−1</sup>) consists of explicitly purging all proton polarization at the start of the inter-scan delay, using a 90 <sup>1</sup>H pulse followed by a pulse field gradient. Taking the R<sub>1</sub> values obtained at 600 MHz using the cosine-modulated IBURP-2 (R<sub>p</sub> = 25 s<sup>−1</sup>) scheme as reference (approach II) we compared the R<sub>1</sub> measured by approach IV with and without the purging element. Indeed, the use of the initial purge in scheme IV provides a better agreement with the reference experiment (rmsd = 0.065 s<sup>−1</sup>, 2.6 %, R = 0.931) than approach IV without purging (rmsd = 0.392 s<sup>−1</sup>, 9.3 %, R = 0.195). However such an approach suffers from a significant loss of sensitivity (up to 35 % in the present experiment) and therefore scheme II should be clearly preferred.</p>
<p>Evaluating the effect of R<sub>p</sub> on R<sub>1</sub> values measured with approaches II and III, showed that suppression of longitudinal CC at faster proton pulsing rate (R<sub>p</sub> = 200 s<sup>−1</sup>) resulted in slightly higher R<sub>1</sub> values for approach II (cosine-modulated IBURP-2), and significantly larger R<sub>1</sub> rates for approach III (Watergate-like), with respect to those measured at slower pulsing rates (R<sub>p</sub> = 25 s<sup>−1</sup>). Figures S3 SM and S4 SM show results at 600 and 800 MHz, respectively. R<sub>1</sub> differences along GB3 amino acid sequence between the two proton pulsing rates were not uniform. Higher deviations appeared for residues 15–23 (A region) and 44–55 (B region), coincident with regions more affected by water saturation effects (approach IV, hard pulses, Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>). This observation is consistent with results shown in Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>.</p>
<p>Sequence specific R<sub>1</sub> deviations depend also on the characteristics of the excitation profile of these selective pulses. With cosine-modulated IBURP-2 pulses, showing a wide zero-excitation region around water resonance (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>), R<sub>1</sub> differences lower than 3 % were observed in regions A and B. However, the use of Watergate-like pulses yielded R<sub>1</sub> rates around 6–10 % higher at faster pulsing rates (see Table 2 SM). Degradation of the performance of Watergate-like pulses at high proton pulsing rates may result from overuse of water-selective 90° pulses, continuously exciting water magnetization from +z-axis to the transverse plane and returning it back from +x/+y to +z. Whenever water magnetization is moved from equilibrium it remains in a vulnerable state, due to the strong RD effects, so it is more prone to be in a saturated state.</p>
<p>Our results showed that short pulsing rates resulted in minimal water saturation during the <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period. It is clear that R<sub>p</sub> = 25 s<sup>−1</sup> is enough to cancel longitudinal CC relaxation in GB3 at 600 MHz. For larger non-deuterated proteins, longitudinal CC effects will be smaller because proton spin-flips, resulting from effective proton–proton cross-relaxation, induce exchange between the longitudinal doublet components at a rate faster than their R<sub>1</sub> difference. The larger the protein, the higher the amide proton spin-flip rate would be. Therefore a pulsing rate of 25 s<sup>−1</sup> should be high enough to cancel these undesired relaxation mechanisms when measuring <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates at 600 MHz or higher fields on non-deuterated proteins similar to GB3 in size or larger.</p>
<p>Insufficient cancellation of CC with the Watergate approach as a result of both off-resonance effects and pulse imperfection of the central 180° hard proton pulse in the Watergate block has been reported at very high fields (Ishima <a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">2014</a>). With a cosine-modulated IBURP-2 based scheme no 180° hard pulses are involved in CC cancellation. Moreover, larger dispersion in proton spectral widths at higher fields allows the application of these selective pulses at increased offsets from the water resonance, thereby minimizing residual effects of water saturation during the T period. The higher the field, the lower the residual effects would be. Consequently, the use of cosine-modulated IBURP-2 is preferable to Watergate-based schemes in order to obtain more accurate <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Exploring the effect of saturation of aliphatic protons caused by CC-suppressing IBURP-2-based schemes on <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> measurements</h3>
<p>While R<sub>1</sub> rates obtained at 600 MHz with the cosine-modulated IBURP-2 approach were very close (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>d) to those reported for deuterated GB3 at the same field (Lakomek et al. <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">2012</a>), the amide-selective IBURP-2 scheme gave shorter R<sub>1</sub> values (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>c). Systematically shorter rates along the whole GB3 amino acid sequence could not derive from water-saturation effects, because saturated water is very similar for both schemes at R<sub>p</sub> = 25 s<sup>−1</sup> (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>). The origin of this discrepancy could be inefficient CC suppression or additional saturation effects caused, directly or indirectly, by CC-suppressing pulses, which may affect amide proton magnetization at the beginning of each scan. Differential saturation effects should be sensitive to the recycle delay while cross-correlation should not be affected by this delay.</p>
<p><sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates were measured at 600 MHz using IBURP-2 and cosine modulated IBURP-2 with 1.7 and 3.5 s of recycle delay values. While R<sub>1</sub> rates measured with the second approach were unaffected by recycle delay, R<sub>1</sub> at long recycle delays using the IBURP-2 approached were larger than values at short delays (Fig. S5 SM). The same happened at 800 MHz. In addition, R<sub>1</sub> rates at 600 MHz with IBURP-2 pulses and 3.5 s of recycle delay approached to rates reported for deuterated GB3 protein (Lakomek et al. <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">2012</a>). These results suggested that the observed deviation originates from non-equilibrium magnetization of aliphatic protons caused by the effect of IBURP-2 or cosine modulated IBURP-2 pulses. While aliphatic protons (H<sup>R</sup>) are affected by cosine modulated IBURP-2 pulses, IBURP-2 only excites the amide region (H<sup>N</sup>).</p>
<p>The effect of the CC-suppressing train of pulses will depend on the aliphatic magnetization present at the beginning of the variable <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period (T). In the pulse sequence shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a> the aliphatic protons magnetization remains on the transverse plane before the T period (XY-pulse program) and is saturated by the PFG G1 applied during the INEPT block. Other pulse sequences used to measure <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates (Lakomek et al. <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">2012</a>) keep aliphatic protons magnetization aligned along the z-axis (Z-pulse programs) at the start of this period. In both XY- and Z-pulse programs, aliphatic protons magnetization will be affected by CC-suppressing pulses, but in a different way. Thus, depending on the pulse program and on the approach used to cancel CC a T-dependent aliphatic protons saturation level will develop during T.</p>
<p>Of course, the state of non-amide protons magnetization has a significant effect on effective longitudinal relaxation time of amide protons (Pervushin et al. <a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">2002</a>; Schanda <a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">2009</a>), the protons excited at the start of HSQC-based pulse programs to measure <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates. Fast H<sup>N</sup> relaxation relies on keeping the non-amide magnetization fully aligned along +z, in a non-perturbed state, so saturation of non-amide spins reduce the effective longitudinal H<sup>N</sup> rates (Schanda <a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">2009</a>). In consequence, slower amide relaxation will take place at high levels of aliphatic spins saturation respect to low saturation states. Slow H<sup>N</sup> recovery rates will result in a smaller amount of H<sup>N</sup> magnetization at the start of the pulse program for a fixed recycle delay versus high H<sup>N</sup> recovery rates. Thus, an increase in H<sup>R</sup> magnetization saturation at long versus short T periods is expected to produce artificially higher <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates, respect to a T-independent aliphatic protons saturation level. On the contrary, shorter <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates would result from a decreased saturation of aliphatic protons at long respect to short T periods.</p>
<p>We used 1D versions of the pulse programs (XY- and Z) shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a> to measure H<sup>N</sup> and H<sup>R</sup> magnetization after a short (80 ms) and a long (800 ms) <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period, in which IBURP-2 or cosine modulated IBURP-2 sequences were applied to cancel CC. Measurements were performed at 800 MHz. Figure <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a> and Table 3 SM show a comparison of the available longitudinal magnetization (non-saturated) after the <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period (point ii) as a function of the CC-suppression scheme. Also, magnetization was evaluated at point (iii), after a Δ period equivalent to the recycle delay used on the R<sub>1</sub> measurement experiments, representing the available proton magnetization at the start of the scan. The amount of H<sup>R</sup> and H<sup>N</sup> magnetization present after Δ = 1.7 s (recycle delay used on <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> measurements shown in Table 1 SM) was calculated respect to the magnetization measured with Δ = 3.5 s, and the percentage of H<sup>N</sup> magnetization is represented in the box at the left over the corresponding 1D spectra (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>b). In addition, <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates were also measured at 800 MHz with the Z-pulse program using the two CC-suppressing schemes (IBURP-2 and cosine modulated IBURP-2). Average R<sub>1</sub> rates and the individual apparent R<sub>1</sub> rates for both XY and Z-pulse programs are presented in Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>a.</p>
<p>With the XY pulse program no signal from aliphatic protons was observed just before the <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period (point <em>i</em> in Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>, Table 3 SM). The longitudinal aliphatic magnetization grows during the <sup>15</sup>N relaxation time when the aliphatic protons are not excited, this is when CC is suppressed by means of amide-selective IBURP-2 pulses. Because of this, a higher amount of longitudinal H<sup>R</sup> magnetization has developed at long T periods (800 ms), resulting in a faster recovery of the H<sup>N</sup> magnetization respect to short T periods (80 ms), see Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>b. A high degree of H<sup>N</sup> at long periods, at the start of each scan, explains the shorter <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates measured with IBURP-2 (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>). On the contrary, the train of cosine-modulated IBURP-2 pulses maintains a full saturation of aliphatic protons constant during the T period so the recovery rate of the H<sup>N</sup> magnetization is unaffected. Thus, <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> relaxation rates measured with this scheme are not subject to saturation effects.</p>
<p>On the other hand, when the aliphatic protons magnetization is placed on the z-axis at the beginning of the <sup>15</sup>N relaxation T period (Z pulse program), cosine-modulated IBURP-2 pulses cause T period-dependent variable degrees of saturation, the larger the relaxation period the larger the saturation level (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>b). As a consequence, the amount of H<sup>N</sup> polarization at the beginning of each scan is reduced for long T values, resulting in artificial and significant increased <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates (Fig S6 SM). The use of amide-selective IBURP-2 pulses to cancel CC, in principle, should not affect the aliphatic protons as their magnetization is not directly perturbed. However, amide protons inversion during the train of IBURP-2 pulses perturbs aliphatic protons magnetization by cross-relaxation and the non-equilibrium aliphatic magnetization eventually feeds-back to the amide protons in a way that depends on the <sup>15</sup>N relaxation delay. Actually, a slightly higher degree of aliphatic protons saturation is observed at long T periods resulting in a reduced amount of H<sup>N</sup> magnetization at the start of the scan, versus that observed at short T periods. This is consistent with an erroneous increase of measured <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates with this scheme.</p></section></section><section id="Sec6"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Concluding remarks</h2>
<p>Systematic errors in <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> measurements arise from unwanted saturation of water and aliphatic protons by the train of 180° proton pulses used to eliminate cross-correlations effects. Errors coming from water saturation show a high degree of variability, being more important for residues in solvent-exposed regions of the protein, whose amide protons show fast exchange with water.</p>
<p>We have shown that saturation of aliphatic protons is another important source of systematic errors on <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates measurements in non-deuterated proteins. Errors show less variability along the amino acid sequence, although they are probably sensitive to local variations in the relaxation rates of relevant protons.</p>
<p>Maintaining the transverse magnetization of aliphatic protons in a dephased state (saturated state) during the variable <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period T of the XY-pulse scheme by using the cosine modulated IBURP-2 scheme ensures that the amount of H<sup>N</sup> polarization at the start of the pulse sequence does not depend on the length of the variable relaxation delay T.</p>
<p>In conclusion, the optimized pulse scheme shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a> (XY-sequence) using cosine-modulated IBURP-2 pulses spaced 40 ms minimize systematic errors on <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> rates measurements in non-deuterated proteins. This sequence efficiently controls both water and aliphatic protons saturation while cancelling CC effects during the variable <sup>15</sup>N relaxation period. The optimized pulse program applied to protonated GB3 gives identical <sup>15</sup>N R<sub>1</sub> values to those obtained with a deuterated sample.</p></section><section id="sec8"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Electronic supplementary material</h2>
