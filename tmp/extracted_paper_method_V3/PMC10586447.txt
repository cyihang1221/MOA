</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=10586447_ao3c04647_0006.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/08c0/10586447/54138460aa8e/ao3c04647_0006.jpg" loading="lazy" id="ab-tgr1" height="301" width="805" alt="graphic file with name ao3c04647_0006.jpg"></a></p>
<p>Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)-based
molecular
networking analysis was applied to <em>Streptomyces</em> sp.
MC16. The automatic classification of the MolNetEnhancer module revealed
that its major constituent was an angucycline derivative. By targeted
isolation of unique clusters in the molecular network, which showed
different patterns from typical angucycline compounds, two new <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine-attached angucycline derivatives (<strong>1</strong> and <strong>2</strong>) were isolated. The structures were elucidated
based on intensive NMR analysis and high-resolution electrospray ionization
mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS). All isolated compounds (<strong>1</strong>–<strong>4</strong>) were tested for their inhibitory effects
on the proliferation of A431, A549, and HeLa cell lines. Antibiotics
100-1 (<strong>3</strong>) and vineomycinone B<sub>2</sub> (<strong>4</strong>) showed moderate inhibitory effects on these three cell lines with
IC<sub>50</sub> values ranging from 18.5 to 59.0 μM, while compounds <strong>1</strong> and <strong>2</strong> with an additional <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine residue showed weak inhibitory effects only on the
HeLa cell line with IC<sub>50</sub> values of 54.7 and 65.2 μM,
respectively.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Angucycline is the largest group of polycyclic
aromatic polyketides,
produced mainly by various species of <em>Streptomyces</em>, and has various chemical structures of angularly assembled tetracyclic
scaffolds and rearranged linear forms.<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a>,<a href="#ref2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref2">2</a></sup> Additionally,
many kinds of deoxy sugars, such as <span class="font-variant-small-caps">d</span>-olivose, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-cinerulose, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-rhodinose, and certain amino sugars like <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-rednose, could attach to the aglycone of angucycline via a <em>C</em>- or <em>O</em>-glycosidic bond.<sup><a href="#ref3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref3">3</a>,<a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup> With
this diversity of the chemical structure, angucycline derivatives
showed a broad spectrum of biological activities, such as antibacterial,
antiviral, and especially anticancer activities for many cancer cell
lines.<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a>,<a href="#ref2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref2">2</a>,<a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> As part of
the ongoing research to find antiproliferative compounds from natural
sources, <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. MC16 was selected for further
investigation after the detection of various angucycline derivative
peaks using the liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)
dereplication method.</p>
<p>Molecular networking as a dereplication
strategy effectively and
efficiently isolates bioactive constituents from natural sources.
This method is easily accessible through the web-based “Global
Natural Products Social (GNPS)” platform, which has a rapidly
growing social library.<sup><a href="#ref6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref6">6</a></sup> In cases where
the GNPS social library lacks the necessary spectra, open-source spectrum
libraries, such as the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), SUPER NATURAL
2 (SUPNAT), FooDB, and the Natural Product Atlas (NPAtlas), can be
utilized for <em>in silico</em> analysis.<sup><a href="#ref7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref7">7</a>,<a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> The
NPAtlas is especially useful as it contains information on about 30,000
compounds from bacterial and fungal sources<sup><a href="#ref9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref9">9</a></sup> and can be coupled with <em>in silico</em> analysis, such
as network annotation propagation (NAP), to aid in dereplication.
The MolNetEnhancer module of GNPS can automatically classify molecular
network clusters based on outputs from <em>in silico</em> tools
(NAP or DEREPLICATOR) and provide visualization of the chemical classes
of major natural source constituents.<sup><a href="#ref10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref10">10</a></sup></p>
<p>This study applied molecular networking analysis with <em>in
silico</em> analysis (NAP and MolNetEnhancer) to the <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. MC16 extract. Among the molecular networks, one cluster showed
a pattern different from that of a typical angucycline and was selected
for further isolation. As a result, two new <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine-containing
angucycline derivatives (<strong>1</strong>–<strong>2</strong>) were
isolated. Herein, we describe the molecular networking-guided isolation
and structure determination of compounds <strong>1</strong> and <strong>2</strong> along with two known angucycline derivatives (<strong>3</strong> and <strong>4</strong>) (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>) and their antiproliferative activity against A431, A549,
and HeLa cell lines.</p>

<p>The LC-HRMS/MS data for <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. MC16
extract was analyzed using classical molecular networking via the
GNPS web platform (<a href="https://gnps.ucsd.edu" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gnps.ucsd.edu</a>) and an <em>in silico</em> fragment analysis tool, NAP,
combined with the structure libraries of GNPS and NPAtlas. The results
of classical molecular networking and the output from NAP were integrated
by using the MolNetEnhancer module, which afforded automatic classification
via the ClassyFire algorithm. The largest cluster (MN1) was classified
as angucycline. The results of the GNPS library matching and <em>in silico</em> analysis using NAP revealed the structure of each
node as various types of angucycline compounds (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>). In contrast to MN1, the next cluster (MN2)
did not match any spectrum in the GNPS library. Only a few nodes matched
the <em>in silico</em> analysis using NPAtlas.</p>

<figcaption><p>Molecular networking
analysis of <em>Streptomyces</em> sp.
MC16 extract. (A) The molecular network is automatically classified
by MolNetEnhancer (the singleton node is excluded in this figure).
(B) The GNPS library matched the structure of the representative node
in MN1, which is classified as angucycline. (C) The candidate structure
by <em>in silico</em> fragment analysis using NPAtlas in MN2.</p></figcaption></figure><p>The proposed structure of the node with <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 722.1900, determined by <em>in
silico</em> fragmentation
analysis, has a tetracyclic ring core. However, its entire structure
shows a different pattern from typical angucyclines. The element composition
analysis by high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry
(HR-ESI-MS) data revealed its molecular formula as C<sub>36</sub>H<sub>36</sub>NO<sub>13</sub>S. Therefore, the node in cluster MN2 was
assumed to be an unusual angucycline derivative containing a sulfur
atom. Using the retention time at each node, peaks in the total ion
chromatogram were identified and selected for targeted isolation.</p>
<p>Compound <strong>1</strong> was isolated as a reddish amorphous powder,
and its molecular formula was assigned as C<sub>36</sub>H<sub>39</sub>NO<sub>15</sub>S from the HR-ESI-MS data (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 758.2111 [M + H]<sup>+</sup>; calcd 758.2113). The <sup>1</sup>H NMR data for <strong>1</strong> closed to saquayamycin B<sub>1</sub><sup><a href="#ref3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref3">3</a></sup> and displayed three olefinic
protons (δ<sub>H</sub> 7.92, 7.73, and 7.03), seven oxymethine
protons (δ<sub>H</sub> 5.47, 5.12, 5.10, 4.45, 3.89, 3.87, and
3.68), four methylene protons (δ<sub>H</sub> 3.45, 3.24, 2.96,
2.85, 2.68, 2.60, and 1.81), two secondary methyl protons (δ<sub>H</sub> 1.50 and 1.46), and a tertiary methyl proton (δ<sub>H</sub> 1.52) (<a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>). The <sup>13</sup>C NMR and HSQC spectra exhibited 31 carbon signals,
similar to those of saquayamycin B<sub>1</sub>, including 4 carbonyls,
3 olefinic carbons, 10 quaternary carbons, 4 methylenes, 7 methines,
and 3 methyl groups (<a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">Table <a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>). The molecular formula of the remaining moiety corresponded
to C<sub>5</sub>H<sub>7</sub>NO<sub>3</sub>S, which was identified
as <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine from the <sup>1</sup>H and <sup>13</sup>C NMR data. The proton signals at δ<sub>H</sub> 5.71
(m), δ<sub>H</sub> 4.14 (dd, <em>J</em> = 4.2, 12.9
Hz), and δ<sub>H</sub> 3.77 (dd, <em>J</em> = 8.8, 12.8
Hz) were revealed as H-α and H<sub>2</sub>-β of a cysteine.
The protons at δ<sub>H</sub> 9.52 (d, <em>J</em> = 8.0
Hz) and δ<sub>H</sub> 2.20 (s) were revealed as a secondary
amine proton and methyl proton of an <em>N</em>-acetyl moiety,
respectively. Through the heteronuclear multiple bond correlation
(HMBC) of H<sub>2</sub>-β (δ<sub>H</sub> 4.14 and 3.77),
H-4 (δ<sub>H</sub> 2.68), and H-6 (δ<sub>H</sub> 7.03)
with C-5 (δ<sub>C</sub> 163.6), the position of <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine and the attachment through the sulfur bridge at C-5
were determined (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>). The nuclear overhauser effect spectroscopy (NOESY) correlation
between H-6 (δ<sub>H</sub> 7.03) and H<sub>2</sub>-β (δ<sub>H</sub> 4.14 and 3.77) supports the attachment of <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine at the C-5 position (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">4</a></a>).</p>

The relative configuration of the sugar moiety was determined by the
NOESY experiment along with the coupling constants of anomeric protons
(<em>J</em><sub>H-1′</sub> = 10.3 Hz and <em>J</em><sub>H-1″</sub> = 2.8 Hz) (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">4</a></a>). <p>Compound <strong>2</strong> was obtained as a reddish
amorphous powder,
and its molecular formula was determined as C<sub>36</sub>H<sub>37</sub>NO<sub>13</sub>S from the HR-ESI-MS data (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 724.2054 [M + H]<sup>+</sup>; calcd 724.2059), which was
34 Da less than that of <strong>1</strong>. The mass difference between <strong>1</strong> and <strong>2</strong>, along with the disappearance of two
oxymethine carbons around 80 ppm, revealed that the aglycone of <strong>2</strong> is a moromycin-type angucycline.<sup><a href="#ref3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref3">3</a>,<a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a></sup> The
NMR signals of <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine were also observed
in <sup>1</sup>H and <sup>13</sup>C NMR spectra [H-α (δ<sub>H</sub> 5.59), H<sub>2</sub>-β (δ<sub>H</sub> 4.22 and
3.97), NH (δ<sub>H</sub> 9.37), <em>N</em>-acetyl CH<sub>3</sub> (δ<sub>H</sub> 2.21); C=O (δ<sub>C</sub> 173.8), C-α (δ<sub>C</sub> 53.1), C-β (δ<sub>C</sub> 35.4), <em>N</em>-acetyl C=O (δ<sub>C</sub> 170.5), and <em>N</em>-acetyl CH<sub>3</sub> (δ<sub>C</sub> 23.0)]. To determine the position of <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine,
an HMBC experiment was performed. However, the HMBC correlations of
these two protons were not observed because of the broad peaks of
H-α and H<sub>2</sub>-β of cysteine. The NOE correlations
between H<sub>2</sub>-β (δ<sub>H</sub> 4.22 and 3.97)
and H-6 (δ<sub>H</sub> 8.50) were observed in the ROESY spectrum.
As a result, the attachment of <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine at
C-5 via a sulfur bridge was determined (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">4</a></a>). In addition, the <sup>1</sup>H and <sup>13</sup>C NMR signals of sugar were closed to the α-L-cinerulosyl-(1
→ 4, 2 → 3)-β-D-olivosyl chain of <strong>1</strong>, and its position and relative configuration were determined by
the HMBC and ROESY experiments, respectively (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figures <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a> and <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>). Thus, the
structure of <strong>2</strong> was elucidated, as shown in <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>, and the compound was named
5-<em>N</em>-acetylcysteinylmoromycin B.</p>
<p>To determine
the absolute configuration of the <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine
moiety, compounds <strong>1</strong> and <strong>2</strong> were defined by
advanced Marfey’s analysis of the hydrolysate
of <strong>1</strong> and <strong>2</strong> after desulfurization with Raney
nickel as a catalyst.<sup><a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a></sup> The advanced Marfey’s
method led to identification of the absolute configuration of the
alanine as the L-form (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.3c04647/suppl_file/ao3c04647_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S16</a>).</p>
<p>Compound <strong>3</strong> was obtained as an amorphous yellow powder,
and its molecular formula was assigned as C<sub>31</sub>H<sub>34</sub>O<sub>10</sub> from the HR-ESI-MS data (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 567.2225 [M + H]<sup>+</sup>; calcd 567.2225). In the <sup>1</sup>H and <sup>13</sup>C NMR data, the signals corresponding to
the aglycone moiety were close to those of compound <strong>2</strong>, except for the disappearance of the <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine
moiety and slight differences in the sugar signals (<a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Tables <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">1</a></a> and <a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">2</a>). The NMR data of the sugar moieties corresponded to that of <span class="font-variant-small-caps">d</span>-olivose and <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-rhodinose, compared to the previous
reports.<sup><a href="#ref3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref3">3</a>,<a href="#ref13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref13">13</a></sup> The arrangement of the α-<span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-rhodinosyl-(1 → 3)-β-<span class="font-variant-small-caps">d</span>-olivose and its attachment
to C-9 of aglycone were determined by HMBC correlations (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.3c04647/suppl_file/ao3c04647_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figures S17–S22</a>). These assignments and
configurations were consistent with antibiotic 100-1, an urdamycin
derivative isolated from a mutant strain of <em>Streptomyces fradiae</em>.<sup><a href="#ref14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref14">14</a></sup></p>
<p>Compound <strong>4</strong> was
obtained as a yellow amorphous powder,
and its molecular formula was determined as C<sub>25</sub>H<sub>26</sub>O<sub>10</sub> from the HR-ESI-MS data (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 487.1596 [M + H]<sup>+</sup>, calcd 487.1599), with 13
hydrogen deficiency indices. The <sup>1</sup>H and <sup>13</sup>C
NMR data of <strong>4</strong> were similar to those of compounds <strong>1</strong>–<strong>3</strong>. However, the signal of the carboxylic
acid (δ<sub>C</sub> 176.1) was observed instead of the ketone
carbon near 200 ppm, implying the rearrangement of the tetracyclic
core into a linear tricyclic form (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.3c04647/suppl_file/ao3c04647_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S1</a>, Supporting Information). The full NMR assignment using 2D NMR corresponded
to the reported data for vineomycinone B<sub>2</sub> (also known as
fridamycin A),<sup><a href="#ref15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref15">15</a>,<a href="#ref16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref16">16</a></sup> and the relative configuration
determined by the ROESY experiment was consistent (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.3c04647/suppl_file/ao3c04647_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figures S24–S29</a>).</p>
<p>After the isolation and structure
determination of compounds <strong>1</strong>–<strong>4</strong>, they
were all annotated to the molecular
network (<a href="#fig5" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig5" class="usa-link">5</a></a>).
First, the node with <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> = 487.1600
in MN1 was matched to aquayamycin by comparing its spectrum to that
of the GNPS library. However, the structure of this node, as elucidated
by NMR, was vineomycinone B<sub>2</sub> (<strong>4</strong>), a rearranged
isomer of aquayamycin. Next, the isolated antibiotic 100-1 (<strong>3</strong>) was annotated with a node of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> = 567.2230 and was close to the candidate structure (sakyomycin
A) by the GNPS library matching. However, the predicted structure
had only one sugar and different positions. In MN2, the node with <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> = 722.1900, which matched that of bravomicin
A by <em>in silico</em> analysis with the NPAtlas library,
could not be isolated because of its small amount in the extract.
However, compounds <strong>1</strong> and <strong>2</strong> were annotated
to the connected nodes with <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> =
724.2060 and 758.2120, respectively. Their structure showed an unusual
attachment of the <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine moiety to angucycline,
as predicted by the <em>in silico</em> analysis of MN2. Assuming
a cosine score and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> difference
between the nodes in MN2, the other nodes seem to be derivatives of
the <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine-attached angucycline. Sulfur-containing
angucyclines such as WS009A and B (from <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. no. 89009)<sup><a href="#ref17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref17">17</a></sup> and nocardiopsistin
E and F (from <em>Nocardiopsis</em> sp. HB-J378)<sup><a href="#ref18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref18">18</a></sup> are rare compounds observed in Actinomycetes.
Therefore, the biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) responsible for angucycline
synthesis was investigated; however, the specific gene involved in
the addition of <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine was not identified
(<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.3c04647/suppl_file/ao3c04647_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Tables S2 and S3</a>, Supporting Information).
Rohr observed the nonenzymatic formation of methanethiol on angucycline
urdamycin E through a reaction with methionine.<sup><a href="#ref19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref19">19</a></sup> More recently, Terenzi et al. demonstrated that Michael
adducts of landomycin derivatives with biothiols, such as reduced
glutathione and cysteine, can form spontaneously both inside and outside
cells.<sup><a href="#ref20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref20">20</a></sup></p>

</div></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p>Two new <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine-attached
angucyclines
(<strong>1</strong>–<strong>2</strong>) were isolated from <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. MC16 using molecular networking and element
composition analysis based on UPLC-HR-MS/MS. Annotation of the isolated
compounds in the molecular network revealed that MN2 was a cluster
of <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine-attached angucycline derivatives
formed by the Michael-type addition of biothiol groups. The biological
activities of <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine-attached angucycline
derivatives have been reported to go beyond anticancer, such as WS009A
and B, which have been shown to exhibit antagonistic binding activity
on endothelin receptors.<sup><a href="#ref17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref17">17</a></sup> The results
of this study suggest that <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. MC16
may be a promising source for large-scale fermentation and targeted
isolation of <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine-attached angucyclines,
which are presented as clustered nodes in the molecular network.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Experimental Section</h2>
<section id="sec4.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">General Experimental Procedures</h3>
<p>Optical rotations and
UV and IR spectra were obtained on a JASCO DIP-1000 polarimeter, a
JASCO UV-550 spectrophotometer, and a JASCO FT-IR 4100 spectrometer,
respectively. NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker AVANCE 400 and 900
MHz spectrometers using CD<sub>3</sub>OD and pyridine-<em>d</em><sub>5</sub> as solvents. HR-ESI-MS and UPLC-MS/MS analyses were
performed by using an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer coupled
with a Vanquish UHPLC system (ThermoFisher Scientific). Column chromatography
was performed by using a silica gel column (Merck, 70–230 mesh).
Medium-pressure liquid chromatography (MPLC) system was performed
using a CombiFlash RF (Teledyne ISCO) system with a Redisep RF C18
reverse-phase column 43 g (Teledyne ISCO). Preparative HPLC was performed
using a Waters HPLC system equipped with two Waters 515 pumps, a 2996
photodiode-array detector, and a YMC J’sphere ODS H-80 column
(4 μm, 150 × 20 mm, i.d., flow rate 6.0 mL/min). Thin-layer
chromatography was performed using precoated silica gel 60 F<sub>254</sub> (0.25 mm, Merck) plates, and spots were detected using a 10% vanillin-H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> aqueous spray reagent. <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. MC16 was isolated from a soil sample collected at Ochang, South
Korea. The nucleotide sequence, obtained through 16S rRNA sequencing,
shared a 98% homology with <em>Streptomyces leeuwenhoekii</em>.</p></section><section id="sec4.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LC-MS/MS-Based Molecular Networking Analysis</h3>
<p>The LC-MS/MS
data were acquired by a Vanquish UHPLC-Orbitrap Exploris 120 system
by the following conditions. Chromatographic separation was conducted
with a YMC Triart C18 (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm, 0.3 mL/min)
column at a temperature of 30 °C, using a mobile phase of water
+0.1% formic acid (A) and CH<sub>3</sub>CN + 0.1% formic acid (B),
and the gradient consisted of a linear gradient of 10–100%
B (0–10 min). The mass detection range was set as <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 200–2000, and the resolution for the full
MS scan was fixed at 60,000 for the full MS scan and 15,000 for the
data-dependent MS<sup>n</sup> scan. The following parameters were
used for the tuning parameter for MS measurements: a spray voltage
of 3.5/2.5 kV for the positive/negative modes, an ion transfer tube
temperature of 320 °C, a HESI probe vaporizer temperature of
275 °C, and RF lens 70 (%). Ultrapure nitrogen (&gt;99.999%)
was
used as both sheath and auxiliary gases of HESI probe and set to 50
arb and 15 arb, respectively. A normalized higher-energy collision
dissociation (HCD) energy of 30% was used for the collision of ions
in the Orbitrap detector. The four most intense ions in the full MS
spectrum were selected for MS/MS fragmentation by the data-dependent
MS<sup>n</sup> mode, and a dynamic exclusion filter was used to exclude
the repeated fragmentation of ions within 2.5 s after acquiring the
MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum. The acquired MS/MS data were uploaded to
the GNPS (<a href="http://gnps.ucsd.edu" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://gnps.ucsd.edu</a>) for classical molecular networking. In addition, the network annotation
propagation (NAP) tool in the GNPS web platform was used for <em>in silico</em> analysis with the following parameters: 10 first
candidates, mass tolerance of 5 ppm, database of GNPS and NPAtlas
(Natural Products atlas). The result of the classical molecular networking
and NAP was integrated by the MolNetEnhancer module, which was conducted
via the “Advanced Views-Experimental Views” tab of the
result of classical networking with the job ID of NAP above. After
analysis, the resulting file was exported to Cytoscape 3.9.1, and
the structures were visualized using the ChemViz2 plug-in. The detailed
method and result of molecular networking analysis could be browsed
on the following GNPS website links: <a href="https://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=8d0bc780cdf44c62a556966d63b8ba14" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=8d0bc780cdf44c62a556966d63b8ba14</a> (classical molecular networking) <a href="https://proteomics2.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=522525e2910143c2bae7864553c85f35" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://proteomics2.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=522525e2910143c2bae7864553c85f35</a> (NAP).</p></section><section id="sec4.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Fermentation, Extraction, and Isolation</h3>
<p>The <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. MC16 was cultured in M2 media (2.0 g/L
yeast extract, 5.0 g/L glucose, 25.0 mL/L glycerol, 4.0 g/L soytone,
and 0.03 g/L CaCO<sub>3</sub>) for 7 days at 28 °C on a rotary
shaker operating at 165 rpm in a baffled Erlenmeyer flask. The cultured
broth (8 L) was extracted with an equal volume of ethyl acetate (EtOAc)
three times. The EtOAc-soluble extract (3.1 g) was fractionated by
a CombiFlash RF (Teledyne ISCO) MPLC system on a Redisep RF C18 reverse-phase
column 43 g (Teledyne ISCO) using a stepwise gradient of MeOH-H<sub>2</sub>O (from 20:80, 30:70, 40:60, 60:40, 80:20 to 100:0; 200 mL
for each step). The 60% MeOH MPLC fraction (213.5 mg) was further
purified by preparative HPLC (MeCN-H<sub>2</sub>O with 0.1% formic
acid, 40:60, isocratic) to yield vineomycinone B<sub>2</sub> (<strong>4</strong>) (<em>t</em><sub>R</sub> = 12.6 min, 4.0 mg), antibiotic
100-1 (<strong>3</strong>) (<em>t</em><sub>R</sub> = 19.1 min,
1.8 mg), and compound <strong>2</strong> (<em>t</em><sub>R</sub> = 24.7 min, 3.6 mg). The 40% MeOH MPLC fraction (156 mg) was isolated
by preparative HPLC (MeCN-H<sub>2</sub>O with 0.1% formic acid, 40:60,
isocratic) to obtain compounds <strong>1</strong> (<em>t</em><sub>R</sub> = 18.1 min, 5.8 mg).</p>

Moiety of Compounds <strong>1</strong>–<strong>4</strong>
</h3>
<p>Advanced Marfey’s analyses for acid hydrolysis of desulfurized <strong>1</strong> and <strong>2</strong> with Raney nickel were used to determine
the absolute configurations of the <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine
moiety of both. Raney nickel (10.0 mg) was added to a solution of <strong>1</strong> (1.0 mg) and <strong>2</strong> (0.8 mg) in MeOH (1.0 mL), and
the mixture was stirred at room temperature. The suspensions were
purged with a H<sub>2</sub> atmosphere under 1 atm. After being stirred
for 18 h at room temperature, the suspensions were filtered through
a filter pad and washed with MeOH (1.0 mL × 3) and H<sub>2</sub>O (1.0 mL × 3), respectively. MeOH and H<sub>2</sub>O filtrates
were concentrated in vacuo. To hydrolyze the acetyl moiety of <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine, the H<sub>2</sub>O filtrates, processed
as above, were dissolved in 500 μL of 6 M hydrochloric acid,
followed by heat treatment at 100 °C for 12 h. The product of
each sample was concentrated to dryness in vacuo, and the residue
was dissolved in 500 μL of H<sub>2</sub>O. Twenty microliters
of 1 M NaHCO<sub>3</sub> and 50 μL of <em>N</em><sup>α</sup>-(5-fluoro-2,4-dinitrophenyl)-<span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-leucinamide
(<span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-FDLA) were added to the 50 μL of hydrolysates, followed
by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. The mixtures were neutralized
by the addition of 20 μL of 1 M HCl and then concentrated to
dryness in vacuo. The resulting dried residue was dissolved in 1 mL
of acetonitrile, followed by passage through a filter. Similarly,
the standard <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>- and <span class="font-variant-small-caps">d</span>-alanine were derivatized according
to the method described above. The <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-FDLA derivatives of
the hydrolysate and the standard amino acids were subjected to a Micromass
ZQ (Waters) mass spectrometer coupled with a 1260 Infinity HPLC (Agilent
technology) with a Phenomenex Luna C18 (100 × 2.0 mm, 3.0 μm,
0.4 mL/min) column at 40 °C using the following gradient program:
solvent A, H<sub>2</sub>O with 0.1% formic acid; solvent B, CH<sub>3</sub>CN with 0.1% formic acid; linear gradient 10–100% of
B (0–15 min).</p></section><section id="sec4.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Measurement of Cytotoxicity of Compounds <strong>1</strong>–<strong>4</strong>
</h3>
<p>A431 (epidermoid carcinoma of the human epidermis),
A549 (carcinoma of the human lung), and HeLa (adenocarcinoma of the
human cervix) cells were purchased from the American Type Culture
Collection (ATCC). The cells were routinely cultured in Dulbecco’s
modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, 11965092) containing
10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Australia, 10099141), 100 units
of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin (Gibco, 15140-122)
at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO<sub>2</sub>. Cell viability activities were determined using the Cyto X colorimetric
assay (LPSsolution, Korea, CYT3000). For cytotoxicity, cells were
cultured in 96-well plates (0.7 × 10<sup>4</sup> cells/well)
for 12 h. The cells were treated with various concentrations of compounds
for 24, 48, and 72 h. The cells were replaced with a Cyto X solution
for 1 h. The cells were measured by using a microplate reader (Molecular
Devices, Spectra Max 190) at 450 nm. Cell viability was normalized
to the DMSO control group.</p></section><section id="sec4.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Genome Analysis of <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. MC16</h3>
<p>The sequenced genome of the <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. MC16
strain was analyzed for the prediction of secondary metabolites and
biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) using the genome mining tool of
the antibiotics &amp; secondary metabolite shell (antiSMASH).<sup><a href="#ref21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref21">21</a></sup> A total of 17 putative BGCs were annotated (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.3c04647/suppl_file/ao3c04647_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S2</a>, Supporting Information), including
two type II polyketide synthases BGCs. Region 5 in the assembly displayed
a high similarity (&gt;54%) to that of BGCs linked to several angucycline
compounds, such as saprolmycin E (91%), grincamycin (88%), saquayamycin
A (85%), saquayamycin Z (76%), and landomycin A (71%). Since the <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. MC16 produces primarily tricyclic glycosylated
structures, vineomycinone B<sub>2</sub>, antibiotic 100-1, and new <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine derivatives (<strong>1</strong>–<strong>2</strong>), we were interested in possible enzymatic processes that
could lead to the biosynthesis of these <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine
derivatives. The <em>N</em>-acetylcysteine moiety has been
rarely found in <em>Streptomyces</em>-produced polyketides
but is also found in the angucyclinones, seongomycin, homoseongomycin,
and cysfluoretin. On the other hand, the seongomycins are readily
formed from diazofluorenes, suggesting that they are shunt or detoxification
metabolites in the biosynthesis of the kinamycins and lomaiviticins.<sup><a href="#ref22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref22">22</a></sup> Recently, it has been reported that a flavin-dependent
monooxygenase AlpJ in kinamycin BGC can generate the addition of <em>N</em>-acetyl-<span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-cysteine during diazofuran ring formation
by cleaving the C-ring of the angucyclinone skeleton.<sup><a href="#ref23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref23">23</a></sup> However, we were unable to identify AlpJ homogeneous gene
in our BGC. However, there are several monooxygenase genes found in
the BGC, including the UrdM homologous gene, which is involved with
D-ring opening for the production of fridamycins (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.3c04647/suppl_file/ao3c04647_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S3</a>, Supporting Information). Perhaps, the process of
the addition of <em>N</em>-acetyl-<span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-cysteine to the
benzene structure of compounds <strong>1</strong> and <strong>2</strong> is
assumed to be bound by a mechanism different from the previously reported
process in a diazofuran ring.</p></section></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
