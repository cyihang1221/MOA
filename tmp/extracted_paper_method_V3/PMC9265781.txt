</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Sweetpotato is prone to disease caused by <em>C. fimbriata</em> without obvious lesions on the surface in the early period of infection. Therefore, it is necessary to explore the possibility of developing an efficient early disease detection method for sweetpotatoes that can be used before symptoms are observed. In this study, sweetpotatoes were inoculated with <em>C. fimbriata</em> and stored for different lengths of time. The total colony count was detected every 8 h; HS-SPME/GC–MS and E-nose were used simultaneously to detect volatile compounds. The results indicated that the growth of <em>C. fimbriata</em> entered the exponential phase at 48 h, resulting in significant differences in concentrations of volatile compounds in infected sweetpotatoes at different times, especially toxic ipomeamarone in ketones. The contents of volatile compounds were related to the responses of the sensors. E-nose was combined with multiple chemometrics methods to discriminate and predict infected sweetpotatoes at 0 h, 48 h, 64 h, and 72 h. Among the methods used, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) had the best discriminant effect, with sensitivity, specificity, precision, and accuracy scores of 100%. E-nose combined with K-nearest neighbours (KNN) achieved the best predictions for ipomeamarone contents and total colony counts. This study illustrates that E-nose is a feasible and promising technology for the early detection of <em>C. fimbriata</em> infection in sweetpotatoes during the asymptomatic period.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> electronic nose, sweetpotato, infection, volatile compounds, asymptomatic period, rapid detection</p></section></section><section id="sec1-foods-11-01919"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>Sweetpotato (<em>Ipomoea batatas</em> L. Lam) is the sixth largest crop in the world and is rich in carbohydrates, dietary fibre, vitamins, minerals, and bioactive compounds (such as phenolic acids, flavonoids, and carotenoids) [<a href="#B1-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-foods-11-01919">1</a>,<a href="#B2-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2-foods-11-01919">2</a>]. Sweetpotatoes are grown in more than 100 countries worldwide, mainly in Asia and Africa. China has the largest sweetpotato plantation areas in the world [<a href="#B3-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3-foods-11-01919">3</a>,<a href="#B4-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4-foods-11-01919">4</a>]. According to FAO statistics, in 2020, sweetpotato planting areas in China covered 2.25 million square kilometres, and the total output was about 49 million tons, representing 30.40% and 54.97% of the world sweetpotato planting area and output, respectively. Compared with other crops, sweetpotatoes can support more people per unit of land, and they are the staple food for large populations over the world [<a href="#B5-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5-foods-11-01919">5</a>]. However, sweetpotatoes are prone to diseases during postharvest storage and transportation, such as black rot [<a href="#B6-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6-foods-11-01919">6</a>], soft rot [<a href="#B7-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-foods-11-01919">7</a>], and root rot [<a href="#B8-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8-foods-11-01919">8</a>]. Among them, black rot is the most typical. It is estimated that about 10% to 20% of sweetpotatoes are lost due to black rot disease during postharvest storage and transportation each year [<a href="#B9-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-foods-11-01919">9</a>]. Black rot is caused by <em>Ceratocystis fimbriata</em> Ellis &amp; Halsted (<em>C. fimbriata</em>) infection [<a href="#B10-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10-foods-11-01919">10</a>], which usually occurs on the wounds and lenticels of sweetpotatoes [<a href="#B11-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11-foods-11-01919">11</a>]. The initial symptom is the presence of black, round, slightly depressed spots. With the advancement of infection, the black spots gradually expand, the depressions increase, and black bristles appear [<a href="#B12-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12-foods-11-01919">12</a>]. The disease eventually leads to wilting, ulcers, and rot [<a href="#B13-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13-foods-11-01919">13</a>]. After sweetpotatoes are infected with <em>C. fimbriata</em>, large numbers of toxins are produced during the disease process [<a href="#B14-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14-foods-11-01919">14</a>], mainly ipomeamarone [<a href="#B15-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15-foods-11-01919">15</a>], which is seriously harmful to human health. The LD<sub>50</sub> in mice is 230 mg/kg body weight [<a href="#B16-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16-foods-11-01919">16</a>]. Ipomeamarone can cause liver and lung poisoning in humans and animals [<a href="#B17-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17-foods-11-01919">17</a>,<a href="#B18-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18-foods-11-01919">18</a>], resulting in respiratory distress, pulmonary oedema, congestion, and even livestock death [<a href="#B19-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19-foods-11-01919">19</a>].</p>
<p>At present, the discrimination of postharvest disease of sweetpotatoes relies mainly on manual work. However, there are no obvious symptoms on the surface of <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes in the early period, resulting in inaccurate identification of infected products as acceptable. Only by removing the infected sweetpotatoes during the incubation period of the disease can large-scale rot be reduced to the greatest extent possible during storage and the economic loss mitigated. It is of great significance to explore the development of a fast, accurate, and non-destructive method of sweetpotato disease detection for use before symptoms are observed on the surface.</p>
<p>When agricultural products are infected by pathogens, normal physiological metabolism is disturbed, causing volatile compound contents to change significantly [<a href="#B20-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20-foods-11-01919">20</a>,<a href="#B21-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21-foods-11-01919">21</a>,<a href="#B22-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22-foods-11-01919">22</a>]. Therefore, the disease condition can be determined by detecting changes in volatile compounds in <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes. At present, the main detection technologies for volatile compounds in agriculture include gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) [<a href="#B23-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23-foods-11-01919">23</a>], gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC–IMS) [<a href="#B24-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B24-foods-11-01919">24</a>], and gas chromatography–olfactometry (GC–O) [<a href="#B25-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B25-foods-11-01919">25</a>]. These technologies have high accuracy; however, there are some disadvantages to these methods, including cumbersome operation, high costs, and long detection times [<a href="#B26-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B26-foods-11-01919">26</a>]. Therefore, these technologies are usually combined with other rapid detection methods, such as electronic nose (E-nose) and hyperspectral imaging, to achieve rapid detection and accurate identification [<a href="#B27-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27-foods-11-01919">27</a>]. Simultaneously, quantitative prediction of physiological indicators can also be achieved [<a href="#B28-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28-foods-11-01919">28</a>].</p>
<p>As a non-destructive, rapid, sensitive, and real-time volatile compounds detection technology, E-nose has been widely used in postharvest quality control, freshness measurement, disease detection, and pest infestation of agricultural products [<a href="#B29-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29-foods-11-01919">29</a>,<a href="#B30-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30-foods-11-01919">30</a>,<a href="#B31-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31-foods-11-01919">31</a>]. For example, E-nose has achieved excellent results in detecting citrus fruit <em>Bactrocera dorsalis</em> infection [<a href="#B32-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32-foods-11-01919">32</a>], garlic <em>Alternariaembellisia</em> infection [<a href="#B33-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B33-foods-11-01919">33</a>], and pine mushroom <em>Aspergillus niger</em> infection [<a href="#B34-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34-foods-11-01919">34</a>]. In the quantitation of toxins, E-nose has been used to quantify aflatoxin B<sub>1</sub> and fumonisin in corn [<a href="#B35-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B35-foods-11-01919">35</a>] and has been combined with Raman microspectroscopy to quantify pesticide residues in tea [<a href="#B36-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B36-foods-11-01919">36</a>].</p>
<p>At present, most of the applications of E-nose are used to discriminate and monitor agricultural product disease when obvious lesion symptoms have appeared on the surfaces of crops. There has been no previously reported study on the use of E-nose for the detection of <em>C. fimbriata</em> infection in sweetpotatoes during the surface asymptomatic period (0 to 72 h). Therefore, in this study, E-nose was used to obtain volatile compounds and was combined with chemometrics to establish a rapid discriminant model of <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes. The volatile compounds were also used to predict the content of sweetpotato toxins and the total colony count to evaluate the feasibility of E-nose technology for early warning of sweetpotato disease. This study provided a new method for real-time detection of <em>C. fimbriata</em> infection and toxin production in sweetpotatoes during the asymptomatic period.</p></section><section id="sec2-foods-11-01919"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec2dot1-foods-11-01919"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Sample Preparation</h3>
<p>The pathogenic fungi <em>C. fimbriata</em> was provided by the Xuzhou Academy of Agricultural Sciences in Xuzhou, China. In a sterile environment, <em>C. fimbriata</em> was inoculated on potato dextrose agar (PDA) and cultured at 28 °C and 85% relative humidity for 7 to 14 days. The spores on the surface of the medium were rinsed with sterile saline solution (0.85% NaCl; all solution concentrations are volume/volume). Then, the spore suspension was collected in a conical flask and counted with a haemocytometer under a microscope. The spore suspension was adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 10<sup>6</sup> spores mL<sup>−1</sup> with sterile saline solution.</p>
<p>Sweetpotatoes of the variety “Xinxiang” were provided by the sweetpotato planting base of the crop research institute at Zhejiang A&amp;F University in Hangzhou, China. Fresh sweetpotatoes of the same colour and size without diseases or insect pests were selected. The sweetpotatoes were first washed with tap water and soaked in 1% sodium hypochlorite solution for 2 min to disinfect them, then rinsed with purified water, and, finally, 75% ethanol solution was used to disinfect the surface of the sweetpotatoes and they were dried. Six holes were punched evenly in the equatorial part of the sweetpotatoes; each hole was 2 mm in diameter and 5 mm in depth. All holes were inoculated with 10 µL of spore suspension. After the spore suspension was absorbed, sweetpotato samples were placed in a sterilized incubator at 28 °C and 80% relative humidity and taken for detection at 0 h, 8 h, 16 h, 24 h, 32 h, 40 h, 48 h, 56 h, 64 h, and 72 h. Sweetpotatoes inoculated with sterile saline solution at 0 h were used as control.</p></section><section id="sec2dot2-foods-11-01919"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Colony Count</h3>
<p>The detection method of the total colony count followed the method of Zhang et al. [<a href="#B37-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B37-foods-11-01919">37</a>], with appropriate modifications. The colonies were counted as colony-forming units (CFUs) and presented as CFU g<sup>−1</sup>.</p></section><section id="sec2dot3-foods-11-01919"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Headspace Solid-Phase Microextraction/Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (HS-SPME/GC–MS) Detection</h3>
<p>Volatile compounds in sweetpotatoes were analysed by HS-SPME/GC–MS technology. The method was referred to Lan et al. [<a href="#B38-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38-foods-11-01919">38</a>], with appropriate modifications. A DVB/CAR/PDMS (50/30 µm, Supelco, St. Louis, MO, USA) fibre was used to extract and concentrate the volatile compounds. Then, the volatile compounds were separated and identified by QP-2010 GC–MS (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Accurately weighed, 3.0 g of chopped sweetpotato tissue was used as the sample, and 5 µL of 2-octanol (0.1 µL mL<sup>−1</sup>, as an internal standard) was added to a 20 mL headspace vial, which was then sealed with a silicone rubber lid. After equilibration in a water bath at 70 °C for 15 min, the fibre was inserted into the headspace vial. The fibre tip was pushed out and exposed 1 cm above the samples at 70 °C for 45 min. Finally, the fibre was injected into the GC for desorption at 250 °C for 5 min and the data collection was started at the same time.</p>
<p>An HP-5 capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 µm) was used in the GC. The injection port temperature was 250 °C. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL min<sup>−1</sup>. The splitless injection was carried out with the following heating procedure of the column. The initial temperature was set at 45 °C and held for 3 min. Then, the temperature was increased to 125 °C at a constant ramp-up rate of 5 °C min<sup>−1</sup>. Subsequently, the temperature was increased to 175 °C at a constant ramp-up rate of 3°C min<sup>−1</sup> and held for 3 min. Finally, the temperature was increased to 230 °C at a constant ramp-up rate of 10 °C min<sup>−1</sup> and held for 2 min. The MS connector temperature was 280 °C. The MS was operated in the electron impact ionization mode (70 eV), and the data were scanned from 50 to 550 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>. The temperature of the ion source was 230 °C, and the solvent was extended for 2 min.</p>
<p>The volatile compounds were identified by comparison with National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) standards, and the substances with matching degrees greater than 80% were screened out [<a href="#B39-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B39-foods-11-01919">39</a>]. The internal standard of 2-octanol was used to quantify the volatile compounds in sweetpotatoes. The data for each group were from three replicate experiments and the results were presented as the means ± standard deviation (SD) ng g<sup>−1</sup> fresh weight (FW) equivalent of 2-octanol.</p></section><section id="sec2dot4-foods-11-01919"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. E-Nose Detection</h3>
<p>E-nose was self-assembled by the agricultural products storage laboratory of Zhejiang A&amp;F University in Hangzhou, China. As shown in <a href="#foods-11-01919-f001" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>, it consisted of an electromechanical control unit, a sensor array, and a data acquisition unit. The volume of the air chamber was 350 mL. The sensor array consisted of 12 metal oxide sensors and one temperature and humidity sensor to monitor environmental temperature and humidity. The metal oxide sensors were purchased from Figaro Engineering Corporation, Japan. The models of the sensor were TGS2620, TGS826, TGS822, TGS832, TGS813, TGS816, TGS2600, TGS2610, TGS2611, TGS2615, TGS2602, and TGS2603, numbered S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, S8, S9, S10, S11, and S12, respectively. The temperature and humidity sensor HTU20 was purchased from the Humirel company, France, and numbered S13. The sensitive gases of each sensor are shown in <a href="#app1-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>.</p>

The prepared sweetpotato samples were put into 500 mL clean and odourless beakers, which were then sealed and placed in a constant-temperature environment of 30 °C for 15 min to obtain headspace gas. E-nose was preheated for 2 h in advance. When the sample was measured, the valves on both sides of the air chamber were closed, and air pump 1 was turned on. The headspace gas in the beaker was pumped out to the air chamber at a constant flow rate of 300 mL min<sup>−1</sup> for 90 s, then equilibrated for 30 s. After the sample was detected, sweetpotato and beaker were removed. The valves on both sides of the air chamber were opened, air pumps 1 and 2 were turned on at the same time, and the airflow rate was adjusted to the maximum. Fresh air filtered with activated carbon was pumped into the air chamber for 120 s for cleaning. When the response curve returned to the baseline, the experiment proceeded to the next sample detection. Thirty sweetpotato samples were detected at each time point.</p></section><section id="sec2dot5-foods-11-01919"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Data Analysis</h3>
<p>IBM SPSS Statistics 22 and Origin 2021b were used to analyse the HS-SPME/GC–MS data, and Origin 2021b and MATLAB R2018b were used to analyse E-nose data.</p>
<p>To reduce the influence of ambient temperature and humidity, the signal was denoised and smoothed, and E-nose data were divided into calibration sets and prediction sets after removing outliers. The maximum response value [<a href="#B41-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B41-foods-11-01919">41</a>] was selected as the extraction feature representing the processed signal for subsequent analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), and classification and regression trees (CART) were used to establish early discrimination models of <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes during the asymptomatic period. The discrimination performance of each model was evaluated by sensitivity (Sens), specificity (Spec), precision (Prec), and accuracy (Ac).</p>
<p>K-nearest neighbours (KNN), partial least squares (PLS), and principal component regression (PCR) were used to predict the content of ipomeamarone and total colony count for <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes during the asymptomatic period. <section id="sec3dot1-foods-11-01919"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Growth of C. fimbriata during Infection in Sweetpotatoes</h3>
<p>The number of pathogens is an important factor affecting disease in sweetpotatoes. The growth curve of <em>C. fimbriata</em> in sweetpotatoes is shown in <a href="#foods-11-01919-f002" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>. From 0 to 72 h, the total colony count showed a stable trend at first and then increased. Fungi growth can be divided into three phases: the lag phase, the exponential phase, and the stationary phase [<a href="#B42-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42-foods-11-01919">42</a>]. <em>C. fimbriata</em> exhibited a long lag phase (0 to 40 h) and entered the exponential phase from 48 h. The longer the infection time, the faster the growth rate. The total colony count was in a low stable state from 8 to 40 h, which indicated that the <em>C. fimbriata</em> needed to re-adapt to the new environment in the early period of inoculation [<a href="#B43-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B43-foods-11-01919">43</a>] and begin synthesising enzymes, coenzymes, or intermediate metabolites necessary for growth. During that time, the total colony count did not increase significantly (<em>p</em> &gt; 0.05). From 48 to 72 h, the spores of <em>C. fimbriata</em> began to proliferate, resulting in a rapid and significant increase in total colony count.</p>

Therefore, it is of great significance to explore a technology for early identification of sweetpotato disease during storage.</p>

(<strong>B</strong>) Internal disease symptoms.</p></figcaption></figure></section><section id="sec3dot2-foods-11-01919"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Analysis of Volatile Compounds by HS-SPME/GC–MS</h3>
<p>When agricultural products are infected by pathogens, they usually release volatile compounds that are obviously different from those that occur in a healthy state [<a href="#B44-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B44-foods-11-01919">44</a>,<a href="#B45-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B45-foods-11-01919">45</a>]. Using HS-SPME/GC–MS technology and an online search of NIST, a total of 65 kinds of volatile compounds were identified and quantified, as shown in <a href="#app1-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link">Table S3</a>, including 11 types of aldehydes, 11 types of alcohols, 7 types of alkanes, 19 types of alkenes, 7 types of ketones, 7 types of esters, and 3 types of other compounds. The changes in the contents of different categories of volatile compounds at 10 time points are shown in <a href="#foods-11-01919-t001" class="usa-link">Table 1</a> and <a href="#foods-11-01919-f004" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>. Results showed that there was no statistical difference in volatile compounds at 0 h between the <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected group and the control group (<em>p</em> &gt; 0.05). At the time of inoculation (0 h), the volatile compounds were mainly aldehydes, alcohols, and alkenes. The contents of these three categories of volatile compounds were significantly higher than those of alkanes, ketones, esters, and others (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05). From 48 to 72 h, the content of all categories of volatile compounds increased significantly, especially ketones. At 0 h, the content of ketones was extremely low—23.39 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW, accounting for only 2%. At 48 h, the content of ketones was 2932.65 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW, and at 72 h of infection, the content of ketones was as high as 17,346.42 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW, accounting for 74% of the total volatile compounds. This was followed by alkenes, with a content of 133.39 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW at 0 h, accounting for 13%. At 72 h, the content of alkenes was 2304.64 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW, accounting for 10%. The content showed a significant increase overall (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) but fluctuated. At the time of inoculation (0 h), the content of alcohols was 296.54 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW, accounting for 29%. The content was relatively stable from 0 to 40 h, with no significant change (<em>p</em> &gt; 0.05). At 48 h, the content began to increase significantly (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05), and at 72 h it was 1473.76 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW but the proportion had decreased to 6%. The content of aldehydes was 499.00 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW at 0 h and the ratio reached 49%. After infection with <em>C. fimbriata</em>, the aldehydes content showed a fluctuating upward trend. At the late period of infection (72 h), the content increased significantly (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05), reaching 963.91 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW, but the proportion had decreased to 4%. The alkanes and esters showed a significant upward trend from 0 to 72 h in general, but the proportion was always low. The content at 0 h was 12.59 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW and 50.95 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW, respectively, accounting for 1% and 5%. The content at 72 h after infection was 376.55 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW and 270.25 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW, accounting for 2% and 1%, respectively. In general, <em>C. fimbriata</em> infection led to the decrease or disappearance and increase or appearance of different types of volatile compounds, which was beneficial for the early discrimination of <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes during the asymptomatic period.</p>

It can be seen from <a href="#foods-11-01919-f005" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>A that ipomeamarone was not detected at 0 h of inoculation. During the early period of infection from 0 to 32 h, the growth of <em>C. fimbriata</em> in sweetpotatoes was in the lag phase, resulting in low levels of ipomeamarone, from 0 to 12.93 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW. The release of trace amounts may have been due to the stress response generated by mechanical damage at the inoculation holes [<a href="#B46-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B46-foods-11-01919">46</a>]. From 48 h on, the content of ipomeamarone increased rapidly and reached 14,203.80 ng g<sup>−1</sup> FW at 72 h, accounting for 81.88%, which was the main component of ketones. Dehydroipomeamarone is a direct precursor for the synthesis of ipomeamarone [<a href="#B47-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B47-foods-11-01919">47</a>], which is also toxic [<a href="#B48-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B48-foods-11-01919">48</a>]. Trans-farnesol has been confirmed to be the initial precursor for the synthesis of ipomeamarone, and 6-oxodendrolasin is an intermediate in the synthesis from trans-farnesol to ipomeamarone. Therefore, it can be speculated that dendrolasin may also be one of the intermediates for the synthesis of ipomeamarone according to the structural formula. The trends of trans-farnesol, dendrolasin, and dehydroipomeamarone content from 0 to 72 h are shown in <a href="#foods-11-01919-f005" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>B–D. The content of these four substances increased with the prolongation of infection time. <a href="#foods-11-01919-t002" class="usa-link">Table 2</a> shows the correlation coefficients between the total colony count of <em>C. fimbriata</em> and trans-farnesol, dendrolasin, dehydroipomeamarone, and ipomeamarone. The correlation coefficients were all higher than 0.9, indicating a high connection among the four substances mentioned above in the synthetic pathway. To sum up, even when there are no obvious lesion symptoms on the surface of sweetpotatoes, large amounts of ipomeamarone may have accumulated in the internal infected parts, greatly increasing the risk of toxicity and compromising food safety. Therefore, it is of great significance to explore early discrimination and prediction technology for <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes.</p>

<figcaption><p>Response of 12 sensors to volatile compounds in sweetpotatoes.</p></figcaption></figure></section><section id="sec3dot4-foods-11-01919"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.4. Correlation Analysis between E-Nose and HS-SPME/GC–MS</h3>
<p>HS-SPME/GC–MS mainly focuses on the identification and quantification of a single substance and is highly sensitive and accurate. However, its high operating cost and required personnel input limit its widespread practical application in agriculture [<a href="#B49-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B49-foods-11-01919">49</a>]. E-nose is economical, fast, non-destructive, and capable of batch analysis. According to the results of HS-SPME/GC–MS, the content of volatile compounds in <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes changed greatly from 40 to 72 h due to the proliferation of <em>C. fimbriata</em>. Therefore, a correlation analysis between E-nose and HS-SPME/GC–MS from 40 to 72 h was performed, and the results are shown in <a href="#foods-11-01919-f007" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>. <a href="#app1-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link">Table S1</a> showed the reported sensitive substance of the sensor array in E-nose. Sensors S1 (r = 0.62, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) and S3 (r = 0.63, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05), which were reported to be sensitive to organic solvents, showed high correlations with aldehydes. S5 (r = 0.72, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05), S8 (r = 0.94, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05), and S9 (r = 0.61, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05), which were reported to be sensitive to alkanes, also showed correlations with alkanes in this study. S11 was reported to be sensitive to volatile organic compounds, and its correlation with aldehydes, alkanes, alkenes, ketones, and esters was 0.85, 0.80, 0.72, 0.68, and 0.71, respectively, in the present study. S6 was reported to be sensitive to alkanes, but we found that it also had a high correlation with aldehydes (r = 0.97, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) and alcohols (r = 0.85, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05). Although the volatile compounds, including ammonia, amine series, and organic sulfur, were not detected by HS-SPME/GC–MS, sensors S2 and S12 showed responses to these substance in sweetpotatoes. Simultaneously, S2 and S12 had a high correlation with other volatile compounds in sweetpotatoes. The correlations between S2 and aldehydes, aldehydes, alkanes, alkenes, ketones and esters were 0.89, 0.78, 0.76, 0.71, and 0.73, respectively. The correlations between S12 and aldehydes, aldehydes, alkanes, alkenes, ketones, and esters were 0.83, 0.82, 0.71, 0.69, and 0.73, respectively. S4, S7, and S10 had low correlations with various volatile compounds. These three sensors were reported to be sensitive to freon and hydrogen, but these two volatile compounds were not detected by HS-SPME/GC–MS. So, then, the sensors (S4, S7, and S10) showed low correlations with freon and hydrogen in the present work.</p>

In addition, compared with a single sensor, E-nose, which is composed of a sensor array, can reflect the changes in sweetpotato volatile components more accurately and comprehensively, which further enhances the reliability of the detection results.</p></section><section id="sec3dot5-foods-11-01919"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.5. Early Discrimination of C. fimbriata-Infected Sweetpotatoes Combined with Chemometrics</h3>
<p>First, PCA was used to analyse <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes from 0 to 72 h. It can be seen from <a href="#foods-11-01919-f008" class="usa-link">Figure 8</a> that the first and second PCs explained 89.5% and 6.1% of the variance in the input variables, respectively, with a total of 95.6%. The first two PCs can be considered as comprehensive information for the representation of entire samples [<a href="#B50-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B50-foods-11-01919">50</a>]. The samples of the <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected group and the control group at 0 h showed a large amount of overlap. This indicated that the volatile compound contents of fresh sweetpotatoes and <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes were mostly the same at 0 h, which was consistent with the results of HS-SPME/GC–MS. Based on the results of the two flavor-detection techniques, it can be considered that the <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected group and the control group had the same freshness at 0 h. The fresh samples at 0 h were well distinguished from the infected samples from 8 to 72 h. However, <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes from 8 to 72 h were indistinguishable. This was probably because the volatile compound contents did not change significantly in the early period of inoculation. Therefore, four distinguishable time points were screened out: 0 h, 48 h, 64 h, and 72 h. Three models were used to discriminate <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes at different infection times: PLS-DA, LDA, and CART.</p>

Among them, the sensitivity, specificity, and precision of LDA at different time points all reached 100%. However, the precision of PLS-DA was lower than that of LDA at 48 h. At the same time, it can be seen from <a href="#foods-11-01919-f009" class="usa-link">Figure 9</a>A,B that the discrimination result of LDA was better than that of PLS-DA, indicating that LDA can more accurately discriminate <em>C. fimbriata</em> infection at different times, followed by PLS-DA. <a href="#foods-11-01919-f009" class="usa-link">Figure 9</a>C showed that CART could also distinguish sweetpotatoes at different infection times, but its accuracy was lower than LDA and PLS-DA. Among the above three models, LDA had the best discrimination effect, followed by PLS-DA and finally CART. These results showed that E-nose could not only discriminate fresh sweetpotatoes (0 h) from <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes but also accurately discriminate infected sweetpotatoes at 48 h, 64 h, and 72 h. At the same time, it also indicated that E-nose can discriminate <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes as early as 48 h in the asymptomatic period. At 48 h, the growth of <em>C. fimbriata</em> entered into the exponential phase. Slight black lesions could be seen in the internal tissue. Volatile compounds changed significantly (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05), which was consistent with the earliest time point when E-nose could distinguish infected sweetpotatoes from fresh sweetpotatoes.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/foods-11-01919-t003/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="sec3dot6-foods-11-01919"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.6. Prediction of Ipomeamarone Content and Total Colony Count Using E-Nose Combined with Chemometrics</h3>
<p>E-nose data for <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes at 0 h, 48 h, 64 h, and 72 h were used for prediction. Three models were used to predict: KNN, PLS, and PCR. A model that had higher <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm19" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msup><mi mathvariant="normal">R</mi><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span> and RPD but lower RMSE (both for calibration and prediction sets) had better performance [<a href="#B52-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B52-foods-11-01919">52</a>]. When RPD &gt; 3.0, the model had excellent calibrating robustness and high predictive accuracy, which could be applied for actual value prediction [<a href="#B53-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B53-foods-11-01919">53</a>].</p>
<p>The prediction model parameters of ipomeamarone were set as follows. When the number of K neighbours of KNN was 2, the latent variable (LV) of PLS was 7, and the number of PCs of PCR was 10, the cross-validation sets of the three models had the highest <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm20" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">R</mi><mrow><mi>cv</mi></mrow></msub><msup><mrow></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span> and the lowest RMSE of cross-validation (RMSECV). Therefore, these parameters were used to train the calibration sets and then the prediction sets were used to test models. The prediction results are shown in <a href="#foods-11-01919-f010" class="usa-link">Figure 10</a>(A1–C1) and <a href="#foods-11-01919-t004" class="usa-link">Table 4</a>, in which the contents of ipomeamarone are presented as µg g<sup>−1</sup> FW. The <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm21" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">R</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">p</mi></msub><msup><mrow></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span> of KNN (<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm22" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">R</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">p</mi></msub><msup><mrow></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span> = 0.932) was higher than that of PCR and PLS. The RMSEP of KNN (RMSEP = 1.410 µg g<sup>−1</sup> FW) was lower than that of PCR and PLS. The RPD of KNN (RPD = 3.852) was also higher than that of PCR and PLS, indicating that KNN had the best highest predictive accuracy, followed by PCR and PLS. The <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm23" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">R</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">p</mi></msub><msup><mrow></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span> of PCR was higher than that of PLS, the RMSEP was lower than that of PLS, and the RPD was higher than that of PLS, indicating that the prediction performance of PCR was better than PLS. In summary, the best model for the prediction of ipomeamarone was KNN, followed by PCR and finally PLS.</p>

The <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm30" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">R</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">p</mi></msub><msup><mrow></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span> of PLS was higher than that of PCR, the RMSEP was lower than that of PCR, and the RPD was higher than that of PCR, indicating that the prediction performance of PLS was better than that of PCR. To sum up, the best model for the prediction of total colony count was KNN, followed by PLS and finally PCR.</p>

In regression by KNN, the input consists of the closest training examples in the data set, and the output is the property value for the object—in other words, the average of the values of K-nearest neighbours [<a href="#B54-foods-11-01919" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B54-foods-11-01919">54</a>]. Therefore, distance and K neighbours are the important factor affecting the prediction results of KNN. In this study, Euclidean distance and optimized K neighbours used in the model achieved a good effect in prediction. This also illustrates the feasibility of KNN for predicting sweetpotato toxins and pathogen growth.</p></section></section><section id="sec4-foods-11-01919"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Conclusions</h2>
<p>In this study, a self-assembled E-nose was used to evaluate the feasibility of early discrimination and prediction for <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes during the asymptomatic period. The results showed that when the sweetpotatoes were infected by <em>C. fimbriata</em>, the growth of <em>C. fimbriata</em> was in the lag phase before 40 h and entered the exponential phase at 48 h. The total colony count increased rapidly and lesion growth accelerated from 48 h, resulting in significant differences in volatile compound contents at different times, especially in the increase of ipomeamarone. The response signal of the sensors had a good correlation with volatile compounds detected by HS-SPME/GC–MS. The sensor signals combined with PCA, PLS-DA, LDA, and CART were used to discriminate <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes. Among them, LDA achieved the best discrimination effect. It could accurately discriminate <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes during the asymptomatic period, as early as 48 h after infection, and the accuracy of the prediction set reached 100%. E-nose combined with KNN, PLS, and PCR was used to predict contents of ipomeamarone and total colony counts. In tests of prediction models, the optimal prediction result for ipomeamarone contents was achieved by KNN (<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm34" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">R</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">p</mi></msub><msup><mrow></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span>= 0.932, RMSEP = 1.410 µg g<sup>−1</sup> FW, and RPD = 3.852), and the optimal prediction result for total colony counts was also achieved by KNN (<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm35" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi mathvariant="normal">R</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">p</mi></msub><msup><mrow></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span>= 0.909, RMSEP = 0.110 log (CFU g<sup>−1</sup>), and RPD = 3.306).</p>
<p>To the best of our knowledge, there has been no prior study on the volatile compounds associated with sweetpotato disease being detected by HS-SPME/GC–MS and E-nose combined with pathogen growth. This study demonstrated that E-nose could be used as a practical and efficient detection method for <em>C. fimbriata</em>-infected sweetpotatoes during the asymptomatic period. In practical applications, ambient gas from different positions in storage warehouses can be collected by gas samplers or pipeline systems and these can be connected to a computer to detect disease in sweetpotatoes online and in real time using E-nose. To sum up, E-nose has great potential application prospects in early disease control and monitoring of agricultural products during storage and transportation.</p></section><section id="app1-foods-11-01919" class="app"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Materials</h2>
