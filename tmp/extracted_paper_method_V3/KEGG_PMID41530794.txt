>Materials and methods   Composition analysis of  DV     The components of the 70% ethanol extract of  DV  were profiled by UPLC–MS/MS. The data collection instrument system used was an ExionLC™ AD ( https://sciex.com.cn/ ). The liquid phase conditions were as follows: chromatography column: Agilent SB-C18 (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm); mobile phase A: ultrapure water with 0.1% formic acid; and mobile phase B: acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient programme was as follows: 0.00 min, 5% B; from 0.00 to 9.00 min, linear increase to 95% B; maintained at 95% B until 10.00 min; from 10.00 to 11.10 min, linear decrease to 5% B; and maintained at 5% B until 14.00 min. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, the column temperature was 40 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The mass spectrometry conditions were as follows: electrospray ionization (ESI) source temperature: 500 °C; ion spray voltage (IS): 5500 V (positive ion mode)/− 4500 V (negative ion mode); ion source gas I (GSI): 50 psi; ion source gas II (GSII): 60 psi; curtain gas (CUR): 25 psi; collision-induced dissociation (CAD) gas pressure set to high; and QQQ scanning used MRM mode with the collision gas (nitrogen) set to medium pressure. Through optimization of the declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE), the optimal DP and CE values for each MRM transition were determined. Based on the retention times of the metabolites, specific MRM transitions were monitored in scheduled time windows. Animal experiments and sample processing   SPF-grade female Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (56 days old) were purchased from the Hubei Provincial Experimental Animal Research Center (Licence No.: SCXK (Hubei) 2020-0018). The experimental protocol was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Review Committee of Hubei Minzu University (Approval No.: [2024]088). The rats were housed in the animal centre of Hubei Minzu University under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 22–24 °C; relative humidity: 50–60%). The 70% ethanol extract of  DV  was dissolved in water to a concentration of 33.75 mg/mL. After 5 days of acclimatization, the rats were randomly assigned to a control group ( n  = 6) or an experimental group ( n  = 6). Based on the adult human equivalent dose of  DV  and its extraction yield in 70% ethanol, the experimental group rats received a daily gavage dose of 0.5 mL/100 g body weight for 5 consecutive days, whereas the control group received an equivalent volume of water. Daily measurements of body weight, faecal occult blood, and stool consistency were recorded for each group, and the Disease Activity Index (DAI) was calculated using the formula DAI = (weight loss score + stool consistency score + bloody stool score)/3 [ 8 ]. One hour after the final gavage administration, the rats were euthanized. Colon tissues were excised, rinsed with saline solution, and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for subsequent pathological examination. Colon tissues from three randomly selected rats per group were collected for transcriptomic analysis. Additionally, colon tissues from all the rats were subjected to metabolomic analysis. Network bioinformatics analysis   Target prediction:  For substances detected in the  DV  component analysis, oral bioavailability (OB) and drug likeness (DL) were assessed using TCMSP ( https://old.tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.ph ). Active substances meeting the screening criteria (OB > 30 and DL > 0.18) were selected, and their corresponding target information was obtained [ 9 ]. SWISSADME ( http://www.swissadme.ch/ ) was used for further substance screening. The pharmacokinetics analysis focused on GI absorption, and the druglikeness analysis evaluated the Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge criteria. Substances showing high GI absorption and meeting at least two of the five druglikeness criteria were retained. Subsequently, SwissTargetPrediction ( http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/ ) was used to predict the targets of these screened substances. Additionally, the SymMap database ( https://www.symmap.org/ ) provided target information for the detected substances. Target information identified by the three databases was used to obtain the target profile for each DV component. Using the keyword “colon epithelial toxicity”, we identified disease-related targets from GeneCards ( https://www.genecards.org/ ), with a score of 10 as a screening criterion [ 10 ].   Construction of intersection genes and core targets:  Venn diagrams identified intersecting targets between  DV  and colon epithelial toxicity. These targets were analysed using the STRING database ( https://string-db.org ) to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network, with the top 10 highest degree nodes defined as core targets [ 11 ].   GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses:  Using intersecting genes from STRING, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses in R 4.3.3 (with DOSE, clusterProfiler, and pathview packages) [ 12 ]. Significantly enriched terms (adjusted  P  < 0.05) were visualized via bubble plots.   Molecular docking:  Protein crystal structures were retrieved from the PDB, and the 3D coordinates of podophyllotoxin were downloaded from PubChem (CAS number). Docking was performed with AutoDock Vina (v1.2.3) and visualized in PyMOL (v2.5.5). The lower the binding energy is, the stronger the affinity between the small molecule and the protein. A binding energy value less than − 5 kcal/mol indicates strong affinity [ 13 ]. Colon tissue transcriptome analysis   Transcriptome analysis was performed on three rat colon tissue samples per group (control and experimental). The RNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer, and integrity was assessed using a Qsep400 bioanalyzer. PolyA-tailed mRNA was enriched with oligo(dT) magnetic beads. RNA fragmentation buffer was added to generate short fragments. First-strand cDNA synthesis uses random hexamers, followed by second-strand synthesis with buffer, dNTPs, and DNA polymerase. Double-stranded cDNA was purified with magnetic beads and then processed for end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. Size-selected libraries were PCR-amplified and quantified by Qubit. The library insert size was verified by a fragment analyser. Qualified libraries were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina platform using the  Rattus norvegicus  reference genome (Ensembl: mRatBN7.2.109;  https://asia.ensembl.org/Rattus_norvegicus ). Untargeted metabolomics analysis of colon tissue   Colon metabolites were profiled by LC–MS/MS (Vanquish UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X). Separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY HSS T3 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm) at 40 °C with 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B) at 0.4 mL min⁻ 1  (4 µL injection). MS parameters: ESI + 3.5 kV; capillary, 320 °C; probe, 300 °C; stepped CE, 30, 40, 50 eV; intensity threshold, 1 × 10 5 . Statistical analysis   Group comparisons were performed using SPSS 20.0 (Wilcoxon rank sum;  P  < 0.05). Differential genes were identified with DESeq2 (v1.38.3) (|log2FC|≥ 1, FDR < 0.05, Benjamini–Hochberg) and visualized by PCA and clustering [ 14 ,  15 ]. Enrichment was performed using clusterProfiler 4.6.0. Metabolites were analysed by PCA and OPLS-DA (MetaboAnalystR: VIP > 1,  P  < 0.05) [ 16 ]; annotation was performed using KEGG, HMDB, and MSEA. The correlations were |r|> 0.8 and  P  < 0.05. O2PLS modelling (OmicsPLS 2.0.2) was used to integrate all differentially abundant metabolites and genes, with R 2  and loading plots used to identify key variables. By integrating three databases (TCMSP, SwissDrugDesign, and SymMap), we screened 247 bioactive substances, primarily including podophyllotoxin and its derivatives (podophyllotoxin, 4′-demethylpodophyllotoxin, picropodophyllin, podophyllotoxone, and 4′-demethylepipodophyllotoxin) and flavones (6-hydroxyluteolin, kaempferol, and morin), and identified 1901 associated targets. GeneCards identified 1872 “colon epithelial toxicity” targets. Venn analysis yielded 658 overlapping targets (Fig.  2 a). These genes were input into STRING to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network (Fig.  2 b). The top 30 target edges were counted based on the PPI network (Fig.  2 c). The top 10 targets—GAPDH, AKT1, TP53, ACTB, TNF, IL6, ALB, EGFR, INS, and STAT3—represent core action targets, with their interactions visualized in Fig.  2 d. GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment primarily in molecular functions such as protein serine/threonine kinase activity, DNA-binding transcription factor binding, and cytokine receptor binding (Fig.  2 e). KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in the PI3K–Akt signalling pathway, lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis, and MAPK signalling pathway (Fig.  2 f).   Fig. 2. Molecular-docking affinity heatmaps revealed that 4′-demethylpodophyllotoxin exhibited the strongest binding to GAPDH and AKT1 (Fig.  3 a). The ligand structure is depicted in Fig.  3 b, and its binding modes with GAPDH and AKT1 are illustrated in Fig.  3 c and 3d, respectively, and summarized in Table  2 . As shown in Fig.  3 c, 4′-demethylpodophyllotoxin forms hydrogen bonds with CYS-152, ASN-316, and THR-153 of the GAPDH protein, enhancing the tightness of their binding. Additionally, this molecule exhibits cation–π interactions with ARG-234 and π–π interactions with HIS-179, collectively forming the critical basis for its binding to GAPDH. As shown in Fig.  3 d, 4′-demethylepipodophyllotoxin forms hydrogen bonds with multiple residues of the AKT1 protein (LYS-179, GLU-198, PHE-293, GLY-294, LEU-295, ASP-274, and LYS-276), which are further stabilized by van der Waals forces via hydrophobic interactions with PHE-161, thereby consolidating the binding of the small molecule to AKT1. The DAI of the rats in the normal group (rank mean = 3.5; rank sum = 21) was significantly lower than that of the experimental group (rank mean = 9.5; rank sum = 57) ( z  =  − 2.966;  p  < 0.05). The experimental process for the rats in the two groups is shown in Fig.  4 A. Pathology revealed that the colonic tissue of the rats in the normal group had an intact basic structure, normal mucosa, prominent mucosal folds, no sloughed cells in the intestinal lumen, no inflammatory cell infiltration in the submucosa, and a normal morphology of goblet cells (Fig.  4 b). The structure of the colon tissue of the rats in the experimental group was damaged, with no obvious folds in the mucosal layer, the mucosal barrier destroyed, and the mucosal layer thickened (Fig.  4 c). The mucosal epithelium was composed of a single layer of columnar epithelium and goblet cells, with many necrotic and shed mucosal epithelial cells visible in the intestinal lumen. The lamina propria contained many intestinal glands, which were densely arranged, with few goblet cells and multiple haemorrhagic spots visible. Mild dilation of intestinal glands, occasional shedding of epithelial cells in the lumen, and focal aggregation of lymphocytes were observed. The muscularis mucosa separates the lamina propria from the submucosa. The submucosa is composed of loose connective tissue. The muscular layer structure is incomplete and uneven in thickness.
