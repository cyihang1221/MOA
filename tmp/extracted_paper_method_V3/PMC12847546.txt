</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="Par1">The progression of spermatogenesis is under dynamic transcriptional regulation. As a subunit of the transcription-export complex 2 (TREX-2), PCI domain-containing protein 2 (PCID2), participates in RNA processing. However, the physiological functions of PCID2 in spermatogenesis remain poorly understood. Here, we generate germline conditional knockout (<em>Pcid2</em>-SKO) mice using <em>Stra8</em>-<em>Cre</em>, and it is found that <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice are infertile, exhibit extensive germ cell apoptosis, impaired spermatogonial differentiation, and failure of meiosis initiation. Single-cell transcriptome analysis reveals developmental arrest at the transition from type A to type B spermatogonia in <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrates a significant decrease in the enrichment of mRNA splicing pathway in <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO germ cells. IP-MS results indicate candidate proteins interacting with PCID2 are significantly enriched in RNA splicing pathway. Co-IP results indicate that PCID2 interacts with SNRPG, hnRNPH1 and SF3B1 to modulate alternative splicing in germ cells. Combining RNA sequencing and PCR identifies four key genes (<em>Prpf3</em>, <em>Nek3</em>, <em>Dvl2</em>, and <em>Slc30a9</em>) as splicing targets of PCID2. Collectively, PCID2 is essential for normal spermatogenesis and male fertility by regulating the alternative splicing (AS) of genes critical for cell cycle progression, spliceosome assembly, and mitochondrial homeostasis. This study provides novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying spermatogenesis and highlights the importance of AS in germ cell development.</p>

<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05840-4.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> PCID2, Male infertility, Spermatogenesis, Spermatogonial differentiation, Alternative splicing</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par3">Spermatogenesis is a complex and dynamic process that occurs in the seminiferous tubules of the testes [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>], where germ cells located in the spermatogenic epithelium of the testes reproduce and differentiate to promote sperm production [<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>]. Abnormalities in this process can lead to male infertility. During male germ cell development, primordial germ cells (PGCs) migrate to and colonize the germ ridge and develop into primitive spermatogonia in seminiferous tubules. After the onset of puberty, primitive spermatogonia initiate the process of spermatogenesis, which encompasses three main stages: (i) mitosis of spermatogonia, (ii) meiosis of spermatocytes, and (iii) spermiogenesis, ultimately undergoing a series of morphological changes to mature sperms [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>]. Spermatogonia initiate spermatogenesis, starting with undifferentiated spermatogonia, such as single type A spermatogonia (A<sub>single</sub> spermatogonia, A<sub>s</sub>), which self-renew to maintain stem cell stability and proliferate to form paired type A spermatogonia (A<sub>paired</sub> spermatogonia, A<sub>pr</sub>) and aligned type A spermatogonia (A<sub>aligned</sub> spermatogonia, A<sub>al</sub>) [<a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>, <a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>]. A<sub>al</sub> transform into type A<sub>1</sub> spermatogonia through multiple mitotic divisions and sequentially differentiate into A<sub>2</sub>, A<sub>3</sub>, A<sub>4</sub>, intermediate spermatogonia, and type B spermatogonia [<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>, <a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>]. Type B spermatogonia transition into pre-leptotene spermatocytes (pre-L), initiating meiosis [<a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>]. Spermatogonia are key to maintaining male fertility through their proliferation and differentiation. The regulatory mechanisms of different spermatogonial subtypes are crucial for male germ cell development. And the distinct cellular fate decisions are regulated by gene expression [<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>, <a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>].</p>
<p id="Par4">AS is a critical post-transcriptional process that boosts transcriptome and proteome diversity across organisms, especially in intricate organs such as the brain and testis [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>]. It is vital for tissue development, differentiation, and regulation of cellular pathways in higher eukaryotes, and is facilitated by the spliceosome [<a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>–<a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>]. As the focus on the modulation of RNA splicing regulators and mRNA isoforms during spermatogenesis intensifies [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>, <a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>], an expanding array of novel splicing factors implicated in this process has been identified through research on knockout mouse models, including BUD31, hnRNPU, BCAS2, CWF19L2 and METTL16 [<a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>–<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>], suggesting that the loss of core splicing factors can alter the splicing of different gene subsets and ultimately affect fertility. TREX-2 is conserved in eukaryotes and composes five proteins based on a Sac3 scaffold with Thp1, Sem1, Cdc31, and two copies of Sus1 binding to it in <em>Saccharomyces. cerevisiae</em> (yeast), and PCID2 is a homologue of Thp1 in mammals [<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>]. Previous studies have primarily focused on the function of PCID2 in regulating the nuclear export of mRNA and proteins [<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>–<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>]. Moreover, PCID2 palys a major role in maintaining the pluripotency of embryonic stem cells (ESCs) [<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>], establishing the immune system [<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>], and the onset and recurrence of cancer [<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>]. A recent study reported that PCID2 regulates the AS of HIV-1, and the absence of PCID2 leads to the over-splicing of intron-containing viral RNA (vRNA) [<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>]. Although substantial progress has been made in understanding its structural and functional mechanisms, the role of PCID2 in germ cell development has not been elucidated. In our previous study, <em>PCID2</em> was substantially down-regulated in spermatogonia and spermatocytes of idiopathic non-obstructive azoospermia (iNOA) patients [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>].</p>
<p id="Par5">Here, we showed that PCID2 was enriched in the testes, with high expression levels at all stages of spermatogenesis. The germline-specific <em>Stra8-Cre</em> mediated inactivation of PCID2 led to abnormal spermatogonial differentiation and meiosis initiation, promoted germ cell apoptosis, and ultimately resulted in male infertility. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis indicated that the absence of PCID2 led to developmental arrest from type A to type B spermatogonia in mouse testes and abnormalities in meiotic entry. In addition, our results suggest that PCID2 maintains normal spermatogonial differentiation by regulating AS of genes related to the cell cycle, spliceosome assembly, and mitochondrial homeostasis during spermatogenesis. Taken together, our study reveals a crucial role for PCID2 during spermatogonial differentiation in maintaining normal male fertility.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Mice and Genotyping</h3>
<p id="Par6">All the mice used in this study had a C57BL/6J genetic background. <em>Pcid2</em> has six transcripts. FLAG-tagged <em>Pcid2</em> KI mice were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. A Linker-3×FLAG was knocked into the translation termination codon of the <em>Pcid2</em>−201 transcript (ENSMUST00000164416.8). The sequence of the Linker-3×FLAG was designed according to a study published in 2022 [<a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>]. The sequence was GGAGGAGGAGGATCTGACTACAAAGACCATGACGGTGATTATAAAGATCATGACATCGATTACAAGGACGATGACGACAAG. The exogenous gene was expressed under the regulation of the endogenous <em>Pcid2</em>. The <em>Pcid2</em>-201 transcript contains 14 exons, with a length of 2860 bp. The translation initiation site is located in exon1, and the translation termination site TGA is located in exon14. The <em>Pcid2</em> gene was modified using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. The brief procedure was as follows, a vector was constructed in vitro, and the CRISPR/Cas9 system and the donor vector were microinjected into the oocyte of C57BL/6JGpt mice to obtain F0 generation mice. After PCR and sequencing verification, the positive F0 generation mice were mated with C57BL/6JGpt mice to obtain a stable positive F1 generation mouse model. The detailed sequences of the PCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S1</a>.</p>
<p id="Par7">The <em>Stra8-GFP Cre</em> knock-in mouse line was kindly gifted by Dr Ming-Han Tong from the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences [<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a>]. We generated floxed <em>Pcid2</em> (<em>Pcid2</em><sup><em>Flox/Flox</em></sup>) alleles using the CRISPR/Cas9 system to study the effects of <em>Pcid2</em> deletion in germ cells. Based on the Ensemble database, the <em>Pcid2</em> gene has six transcripts, and the exon2-exon5 of <em>Pcid2-</em>201 transcript is recommended as the knockout region. The region contains a 272 bp coding sequence, and knockout of this region results in disruption of protein function. Briefly, a pair of sgRNAs was transcribed in <em>vitro</em> and a donor vector was constructed. Cas9, sgRNA, and the donor were microinjected into the oocytes of C57BL/6J mice. Fertilised embryos were transplanted to obtain F0 mice which were confirmed using PCR and sequencing. A stable F1 generation mouse model was obtained by mating positive F0-generation mice with C57BL/6J mice. <em>Stra8-GFP Cre</em> knock-in mice were mated with <em>Pcid2</em><sup><em>Flox/Flox</em></sup> mice to obtain the<em> Pcid2</em>-SKO progeny. All mice were housed under a regulated lighting schedule comprising 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness at a temperature range of 21–22 °C, with free access to water and food. Genotyping was conducted by PCR using the genomic DNA obtained from the tails of 5 days postpartum (dpp) mice. The detailed sequences of the PCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S1</a>.</p>
<p id="Par8">The Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University Health Science Center (LA2021579) approved all experimental procedures and animal care protocols.</p></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Fertility test</h3>
<p id="Par9">Each male mouse (8–12 weeks, <em>n</em> = 3) of control and <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO was caged with two WT C57BL/6J females (6–8 weeks), and the female mice were checked for vaginal plugs every morning. Plugged female mice were separated into single cages. Pregnancy status and number of pups were recorded for each female. The fertility test lasted for six months.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Histological analysis</h3>
<p id="Par10">After isoflurane inhalation in a closed room, mice were euthanised using the cervical dislocation method. The abdomens of the mice were sprayed with 75% ethanol and a V-shaped incision was made in the pelvic cavity using sterile scissors and forceps. Removing epididymal fat to locate the testicles, cut them with scissors, and place them in a culture dish containing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Testicles were separated and fixed overnight with Blouin’s solution in a rotator. The tissue was embedded in paraffin, and a Leica vibratome machine was used to cut it into 5 μm thick slices. Slices were dewaxed and rehydrated using gradient concentrations of ethanol and then stained with haematoxylin and eosin. After fixing the slices with neutral resins, images were captured using NanoZoomer Digital Pathology (WISLEAP, WS-10, China).</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunofluorescence</h3>
<p id="Par11">Testicles extracted from male mice were isolated immediately after cervical dislocation and immobilised overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Servicebio, G1101) at 4 °C for immunostaining. After sequential dehydration in an ethanol series, the tissues were embedded in paraffin wax and sliced using a Leica slicing machine (Leica, RM2235, Germany). After dewaxing and hydration, the slices were boiled in Tris-EDTA (pH 8.0) for 15 min, washed in 1× PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST), blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h, and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing three times with PBST, the slices were incubated with secondary antibodies and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 1:1000, Abcam, ab285390) to stain the nuclei at room temperature for 1 h. The cells were then washed three times with PBST. Images were captured using a Zeiss LSM880 inverted confocal microscope. 10 to 15 regions were randomly selected to count the positively stained cells. The primary and secondary antibodies used for immunofluorescence are shown in Supplementary Table <a href="#MOESM2" class="usa-link">S2</a>.</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining</h3>
<p id="Par12">Paraffin-embedded slides were fixed with 4% PFA and washed three times with PBS. The slices were boiled in sodium citrate buffer for 15 min for antigen retrieval. After blocking with 3% BSA, each slide was incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C and then washed three times with PBS. Slides were treated with 3% H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> to eliminate internal peroxidase activity. After washing thrice with PBS, the slides were stained with a secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP). Finally, the slides were stained with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and the nuclei were stained with haematoxylin. Images were captured using the NanoZoomer Digital Pathology System (WISLEAP, WS-10, China).</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Western blotting</h3>
<p id="Par13">Male mouse tissues were separated, pulverised in liquid nitrogen, and collected in ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (RIPA) containing protease inhibitors. The lysates were diluted with 2× Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad, 1610737) and boiled in water for 10 min. Protein samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE, 10% acrylamide gel) and electrically transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After transfer, 5% skimmed milk in Tris-buffered saline (10 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) was used to block the PVDF membrane for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated for 1 h with rabbit or mouse secondary antibodies. Finally, the protein bands were observed using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Tanon-5200). Western blotting images were analysed using ImageJ software (Wayne Rasband, USA). Full and uncropped Western blotting are shown in the Supplementary Material. The antibodies used for Western blotting are listed in Supplementary Table <a href="#MOESM2" class="usa-link">S2</a>.</p></section><section id="Sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunoprocipitation-Mass Spectrometry (IP-MS)</h3>
<p id="Par14">The testes of male mice were separated and collected in ice-cold RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. After tissue grinding and ultrasonication, the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 10 min and the supernatant was incubated with a volume of antibody at 4 °C for 3 h. Next, 40 µL Dynabeads-protein-A (Invitrogen, 10002D) was added and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The beads were washed four times with PBS, and the precipitated material was diluted with SDS-PAGE sample buffer and boiled for 10 min to separate the beads. The supernatant from each sample was loaded onto a 10% acrylamide gel and electrophoretically separated at 80 V. As a control for peptide visualisation, the gels were stained with liver stain for 30 min.</p>
<p id="Par15">Proteins in the gel were hydrolysed using trypsin. In the LC-MS/MS analysis, the digested products were separated by 120 min gradient elution at a flow rate of 0.300 µL/min with a Thermo Ultimate 3000 nano-UPLC system directly interfaced with a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer. The analytical column used was an Acclaim PepMap RSLC column (75 μm ID, 250 mm length, C18). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid and mobile phase B consisted of 100% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent acquisition mode using the Xcalibur 4.1.50 software, and there was a single full-scan mass spectrum in the Orbitrap (375–1500 m/z, 60,000 resolution), followed by data-dependent MS/MS scans. Raw data were searched against the selected database downloaded from UniProt using the Proteome Discovery software (version 2.4).</p>
<p id="Par16">The normalised abundance of each protein was transformed into log space and a differential test was performed using the R package limma (v3.50.3) [<a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>]. Only proteins with an adjusted <em>p value</em> &lt; 0.05, and fold change (IP/control) &gt; 2 were identified as candidate interacting proteins of PCID2.</p></section><section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)</h3>
<p id="Par17">Full-length sequences of mouse <em>Pcid2</em>, <em>Snrpg</em>, <em>Sf3b1</em>, and <em>hnRNPH1</em> were obtained from testis cDNA and cloned into p3×FLAG-CMV-10 and pEGFP-C1 vectors using Phanta<sup>®</sup> Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme, P505). The constructed plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were lysed in ice-cold ELB buffer (50 mM HEPES, 250 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF (Sigma-Aldrich, P7626) and protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04693132001). Following centrifugation at 12,000 ×g for 10 min, supernatants were incubated with 1 µg target antibody at 4 °C for 3 h. Subsequently, 15 µL Dynabeads-protein-A (Invitrogen, 10002D) was added, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. After four washes with ELB buffer, immunoprecipitated complexes were denatured in SDS-PAGE sample buffer and analysed by Western blotting.</p></section><section id="Sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">RNA FISH</h3>
<p id="Par18">The probe <em>Pcid2</em>-C1 was designed for a target sequence between 1057 and 1999 bp. RNA FISH was performed using the RNAscope<sup>®</sup> Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 (ACD, 323100), according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, the paraffin slices underwent the following steps: baked at 60 °C for 30 min, fresh xylene and anhydrous ethanol incubation, followed by air drying, incubated in hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, washed with distilled water and incubated in 1× codetection target repair reagent at 100 °C for 15 min, washed with distilled water and PBST. The slides were then incubated with the first antibody (MVH, see Supplementary Table <a href="#MOESM2" class="usa-link">S2</a>) overnight at 4 °C, washed twice with PBST, incubated with 10% neutral formalin solution for 30 min, washed with PBST, treated with protease Plus at 40 °C for 30 min, washed with distilled water, incubated with probe <em>Pcid2</em>-C1 (ACD, 1133331-C1) at 40 °C for 2 h, AMP1 and AMP2 at 40 °C for 30 min, AMP3 at 40 °C for 15 min, HRP-C1 at 40 °C for 15 min, Opal™ 570 (Akoya Biosciences, FP1488001KT) for <em>Pcid2</em> at 40 °C for 30 min, HRP-blocker at 40 °C for 15 min, washed twice with RNAscope<sup>®</sup> 1× washing buffer, and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat-anti-rabbit antibody at room temperature for 30 min. The slides were washed twice with PBST and incubated with DAPI solution at room temperature for 60 s. The slides were sealed with antifade mountant (Invitrogen, <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/P36930" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">P36930</a>). Fluorescence images were obtained using a Zeiss LSM880 inverted confocal microscope.</p></section><section id="Sec12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS)</h3>
<p id="Par19">Cells were prepared from mouse testes. In the final wash of testicular cells, cells were resuspended in 4 mL of 1× MojoSort™ Buffer (BioLegend, 480017). The cells were then filtered with a 70 μm cell strainer, centrifuged at 300 ×g for 5 min, and resuspended in a volume of MojoSort™ Buffer. The cell concentration was adjusted to 1 × 10<sup>8</sup> cells/ml. A cell suspension (100 µL, 10<sup>7</sup> cells) was aliquoted into a new tube, and a volume of PE-conjugated antibody Anti-CD117 (c-KIT, eBioscience,12-1171-83) was added to the cell suspension, mixed well, and incubated on ice for 15 min. Cells were washed with 1× MojoSort™ buffer and centrifuged at 300 ×g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded and resuspended in 100 µL of 1× MojoSort™ buffer. 10 μL of PE Nanobeads (BioLegend, 480080) were added, mixed well, and incubated on ice for 15 min. The cells were then washed with 1× MojoSort™ buffer (up to 4 mL). Next, the cells were centrifuged at 300 ×g for 5 min and the supernatant was discarded. Adding 2.5 mL of 1× MojoSort™ buffer was added, and the tube was placed in a MojoSort™ Magnet (BioLegend, 480019) for 5 min. The unlabelled fraction was discarded, and the labelled cells were resuspended in 2.5 mL MojoSort™ Buffer. Repeat the separation steps for three times to remove the unlabelled fractions and retain the labelled cells.</p></section><section id="Sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">RNA extraction and RT-PCR</h3>
<p id="Par20">Total RNA was extracted from whole testes or sorted cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Scientific, 15596018) and trichloromethane (TGREAG, 67-66-3) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For RT-PCR, isolated total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, RR047A). The cDNA and specific primers were subjected to appropriate PCR cycles. The PCR products were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis and quantified using the ImageJ software. Splicing ratios are represented as percentage spliced in (PSI) values, PSI = (Splice-in)/(Splice-in + Splice-out), indicating the proportion of mRNA transcripts for a gene that contains a particular exon or splice site. The primers used for PCR are listed in Supplementary Table <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S1</a>.</p></section><section id="Sec14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LncRNA library construction and data analysis</h3>
<p id="Par21">After the RNA samples were evaluated and qualified, rRNA was removed from the total RNA samples (some IncRNAs had the same polyA tail structure as the mRNA and rRNA maximally retained IncRNAs containing polyA tails were removed). Library construction was performed according to the NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, E7420L) operating instructions and different index tags were selected for library construction.</p>
<p id="Par22">Raw fastq was processed using the fastp software to remove sequencing adapters and low-quality reads. The clean reads were aligned to the mm10 genome (gene annotation was gencode-vM23) using STAR software. rMATS was used to analyse AS events between control and <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO testes. To detect effective AS events, events with a false discovery rate (FDR) &lt; 0.01 and |△PSI| &gt;5% were classified as differential splicing events based on the original read counts.</p></section><section id="Sec15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Single-cell transcriptome library construction and data analysis</h3>
<p id="Par23">Using the Chromium Single Cell 3’ GEM Kit v3.1, single-cell suspensions are processed on the Chromium Single Cell Chip for encapsulation with barcoded gel beads, targeting 6000 cells per sample. Cells are lysed, and RNA is reverse-transcribed and barcoded within gel beads, followed by cDNA amplification using a T100 PCR thermal cycler. cDNA quality and concentration are assessed with a Qubit fluorometer and Bioanalyzer 2100. Libraries are sequenced on the Illumina platform by Annoroad Gene Technology, with 40,000 reads per cell.</p>
<p id="Par24">STARsolo (v2.7.9a) was used to align reads to mm10 (mm10-2020-A) mouse reference genome and generated the raw count matrix. Then raw count matrix was imported into Seurat (v4.3) for preprocessing. Only cells fulfill with the following criteria were kept for further analysis: (1) at least 500 detected genes; (2) percentage of mitochondria UMIs &lt; 10%. The filtered cells were normalized to log (CPM/100 + 1).</p>
<p id="Par25">Top 2000 highly variable genes were adopted for data integration. The data were integrated using mutual nearest neighborhood correction algorithm implemented by RunFastMNN function with default settings [<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>]. Then Louvain clustering was performed on the first 50 MNN embeddings with k = 100. Cells were annotated through projecting them onto the mouse testicular cell atlas (mTCA) using ProjectSVR [<a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>]. Only cells with high-quality projection (adjust <em>p</em> value &lt; 0.01) were kept. Then the annotations were checked manually according to the expression level of canonical markers. Clusters expressing markers from two different cell types were identified as doublets and were removed. To visualize the scRNA-seq data, the first 50 MNN embeddings of somatic cells were used to generate UMAP. The germ cells were visualized using the projected UMAP generated by ProjectSVR.</p>
<p id="Par26">To calculate the pseudotime of germ cells along the spermatogenesis, the principle curve was fitted on projected UMAP embeddings using the princurve (v2.1.6) package in R. Pseudotime was calculated as the distance between the closest position on the curve and the start position.</p>
<p id="Par27">The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of each stage of germ cells were identified through MAST algorithm [<a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a>]. DEGs were visualized using jjVolcano function implemented in R package scRNAtoolVis (v0.0.7).</p>
<p id="Par28">The Single Cell Pathway Analysis (SCPA) was performed using the R package SCPA (v1.5.4) [<a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>] according to the package tutorial. The affected pathway was visualized using the R package ComplexHeatmap (v2.10.0) [<a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a>]. The gene set enrichment analysis of type B spermatogonia was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.2.2) [<a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>].</p></section><section id="Sec16"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Transferase dUTP nick-end labelling (TUNEL) assay</h3>
<p id="Par29">The terminal deoxynucleotidyl TUNEL assay was performed using the In situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche, 11684817910) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the tissue slides were permeabilised with 0.3% Triton X-100. After the slides were equilibrated, TdT reaction mixture was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The slides were then incubated with DAPI for 10 min for nuclear staining. After the final washing, the slides were dried and mounted with an antifade reagent (Invitrogen, <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/P36930" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">P36930</a>) and stored in a black box at 4 °C until image scanning with Pannoramic MIDI.</p></section><section id="Sec17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="Par30">Each experiment was performed with three replicates. All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 8 (GraphPad software) and are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (mean ± SEM). Statistical significance was determined using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s <em>t</em>-test unless otherwise stated in the figure legend. The significance threshold was *<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05, **<em>P</em> &lt; 0.01, ***<em>P</em> &lt; 0.001, ****<em>P</em> &lt; 0.0001.</p></section></section><section id="Sec18"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="Sec19"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">PCID2 is expressed at all stages of germ cells during spermatogenesis</h3>
<p id="Par31">According to sequence alignment and motif analyses, PCID2 is a highly conserved protein expressed in multiple vertebrate species (Figure <a href="#MOESM6" class="usa-link">S1</a>A-C). We evaluated the expression levels of PCID2 in multiple organs. The results showed that the PCID2 protein was universally expressed but was enriched in the testes (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>A). In our previous study, which aimed to reveal the molecular features of human iNOA, we discovered a substantial decrease in <em>PCID2</em> expression in both spermatogonia and spermatocytes compared to that in normal controls (Figure <a href="#MOESM6" class="usa-link">S2</a>A) [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>]. According to data from a previous single-cell transcriptome study [<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a>], <em>Pcid2</em> is highly expressed at various stages of mouse spermatogenesis (Figure <a href="#MOESM6" class="usa-link">S2</a>B). The results from the extraction and reanalysis of published proteomic data [<a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a>] were consistent with our Western blotting results, which showed PCID2 protein levels from 3 dpp to 56 dpp, indicating that PCID2 was highly expressed during spermatogenesis (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>B and C). Because the commercial anti-PCID2 antibody cannot be used for immunofluorescence staining, to study the location and function of PCID2 during spermatogenesis, we engineered the <em>Pcid2</em> locus to produce FLAG-tagged knock-in (KI) mice using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. The design of the targeting vector for the KI allele and genotyping results are shown (Figure <a href="#MOESM6" class="usa-link">S2</a>C and D). The localization and expression of PCID2 during spermatogenesis was further characterised by immunofluorescence and IHC staining of paraffin-embedded testicular sections. The results showed that FLAG-tagged PCID2 was highly expressed in spermatogonia, meiotic prophase I spermatocytes, and round spermatids (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>D and E). We speculate that PCID2 plays an essential physiological role during spermatogenesis.</p>

These results suggested that PCID2 ablation caused defects in spermatogonial differentiation and meiotic entry.</p></section><section id="Sec22"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">PCID2 deficiency caused developmental arrest from type A to type B spermatogonia</h3>
<p id="Par43">Spermatogenesis is a complex and regulated process of cell differentiation, which coordinated by numerous molecules [<a href="#CR45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR45">45</a>, <a href="#CR46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR46">46</a>]. The above results suggest that the loss of PCID2 did not affect undifferentiated spermatogonia, but resulted in a decrease in the number of differentiated spermatogonia. To investigate the molecular changes during spermatogonial differentiation in <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice, we performed scRNA-seq on whole testicular cells from WT and <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mouse testes at 10 dpp, when the cell numbers in the testes of both WT and <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice were comparable. Subsequent analyses were based on the WT (22,823 cells) and <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO (31,306 cells) datasets. By performing clustering analysis on cells from the testes of WT and <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice at 10 dpp, we found that the somatic cells in the testes of both WT and <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice exhibited similar cell clustering patterns, which were evenly distributed in all cell clusters without significant differences (Figure <a href="#MOESM6" class="usa-link">S6</a>A and B). Cluster analysis showed that the germ cells of male mice at 10 dpp were divided into five cell clusters: undifferentiated spermatogonia (A undiff), type A spermatogonia (Type A), type B spermatogonia (Type B), pre-leptotene spermatocytes (pre-L), and leptotene spermatocytes (L) (Figure <a href="#MOESM6" class="usa-link">S6</a>C and D). Compared to WT, only a few cells in <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mouse testes were in these three cell clusters: Type B, pre-L, and L (Figure <a href="#MOESM6" class="usa-link">S6</a>C).</p>
<p id="Par44">To detect the proportional alternation of different cell types in mouse testes after PCID2 deficiency, we performed cell abundance analysis of whole cells in the testes of WT and <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice and found that, compared with WT, the proportion of germ cells in the testes of <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice was reduced (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>A). Moreover, the proportions of Type B, pre-L and L were significantly lower than those of the WT (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>A), which was consistent with the results of the clustering analysis. To further study the developmental trajectory of germ cells in <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice, we conducted pseudotime analysis of germ cells in WT and <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice. The proportion of A undiff and Type A in <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice showed no difference compared to that in WT mice, indicating that the germ cells of <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice could normally develop into Type A, whereas only a few germ cells could develop into Type B, pre-L, and L (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>B). These results indicated that germ cell development in <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice was arrested during the transition from type A to type B spermatogonia, and the number of germ cells that could enter meiosis and develop into pre-L and L was extremely small, which was consistent with the phenotype shown by immunofluorescence staining.</p>

<p id="Par47">To elucidate the physiological functions of PCID2 in germ cell development, we performed IP-MS to identify candidate proteins that interacted with PCID2 in mouse testes. We used the testes of FLAG-tagged PCID2 mice for this experiment and conducted silver staining and Western blotting to detect the proteins pulled down by immunoprecipitation, confirming the reliability of the results (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>A and Figure <a href="#MOESM6" class="usa-link">S6</a>F). The analysis results of IP-MS identified 547 candidate proteins (adjusted <em>p</em> value &lt; 0.05, fold change (IP/control) &gt; 2) interacting with PCID2 (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>B and Supplementary Table <a href="#MOESM4" class="usa-link">S4</a>).</p>

This result matched scRNA-seq results. Therefore, we speculated that the function of PCID2 in germ cells may be highly related to the regulation of RNA splicing.</p>
<p id="Par49">To further understand the mechanism of germ cell developmental arrest caused by PCID2 deficiency, which results in male infertility, we collected testes from WT and <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice for RNA-seq. Since previous results suggested that the function of PCID2 in germ cells may be highly related to the regulation of RNA splicing, we used the replicate multivariate analysis of transcript splicing (rMATS) software to analyze AS events based on the RNA-seq data. The results showed that, compared with WT, 130 differentially AS events were found in the testes of <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice, most of which were skipped exon (SE) events, accounting for 81.54% of the total AS events (106/130) (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>D and E and Supplementary Table <a href="#MOESM5" class="usa-link">S5</a>). Other differentially AS events occurred included alternative 5’ splice sites (5/130), alternative 3’ splice sites (6/130), exon mutual exclusivity (6/130), and intron retention (7/130) (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>D). Among the 130 differentially AS events, 63 were up-regulated and 67 were down-regulated in <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mouse testes (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>F).</p>
<p id="Par50">Therefore, combining with the phenotype of <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice and previous results, we selected four genes related to spliceosome assembly (<em>Prpf3</em>), cell cycle (<em>Nek3</em>, <em>Dvl2</em>), and mitochondrial zinc homeostasis (<em>Slc30a9</em>) to visualize. The loss of PCID2 led to differential expression of exon 3 in <em>Slc30a9</em> (increased exon skipping), exon 14 in <em>Prpf3</em> (increased exon skipping), exon 11 in <em>Nek3</em> (increased exon skipping), and intron 2 in <em>Dvl2</em> (increased intron retaining) (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>G). PCR provided additional evidence supporting the differentially AS events in c-Kit<sup>+</sup> germ cells extracted from mouse testes at 10 dpp (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>H).</p>
<p id="Par51">We next investigated whether PCID2 directly regulates the splicing machinery or acts indirectly through other pathways. To this end, we performed Co-IP to verify the candidate interacting proteins. We found that in Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>B, SNRPG as a core component of the spliceosome [<a href="#CR47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR47">47</a>], was one of the candidate proteins that most likely to interact with PCID2. And we also noticed that hnRNPH1 and SF3B1 were candidate proteins interacting with PCID2 (Supplementary Table <a href="#MOESM4" class="usa-link">S4</a>), which were found related to mRNA splicing and play essential roles in spermatogenesis and the maintaining of male fertility [<a href="#CR48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR48">48</a>, <a href="#CR49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR49">49</a>]. Therefore, we performed Co-IP and Western blotting to confirm the binding of SNRPG, hnRNPH1 and SF3B1 with PCID2. And the results indicated that PCID2 regulates alternative splicing events in germ cells via interacting with SNRPG, hnRNPH1 and SF3B1 (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>I-K).</p>
<p id="Par52">In conclusion, these data indicate that PCID2 regulates the development of spermatogonia by regulating the AS of genes related to the cell cycle, spliceosome assembly, and mitochondrial homeostasis.</p></section></section><section id="Sec24"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p id="Par54">Spermatogonia are a heterogeneous population of diploid germ cells that undergo extensive mitosis to increase cell numbers, providing the reserve for spermatogenesis [<a href="#CR50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR50">50</a>]. After the establishment of the spermatogonial stem cell (SSC) pool, the differentiation of spermatogonia in mice primarily involves two key events: fate determination of SSCs, which includes self-renewal and initiation of differentiation, and the transition from A<sub>al</sub> to A1 [<a href="#CR51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR51">51</a>]. This process is strictly regulated, PCID2 as a subunit of TREX-2, plays a role in the regulation of gene expression. In our previous work committed to dissecting the molecular characteristics of human iNOA, we found that <em>PCID2</em> was significantly down-regulated in both spermatogonia and spermatocytes in iNOA patients compared to normal controls [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>]. Sequence comparison and motif analysis revealed that PCID2 is expressed in various vertebrates and highly conserved between humans and mice. However, the function of PCID2 in spermatogenesis has not been elucidated.</p>
<p id="Par55">To investigate whether PCID2 plays an essential part during spermatogenesis, we constructed a conditional knockout mouse model, <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mouse, to explore the function of PCID2 in male fertility. Our findings indicate that the deletion of <em>Pcid2</em> in germ cells leads to the failure of spermatogenesis and even complete male infertility. In <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mouse testes, although mitosis of undifferentiated spermatogonia proceeded normally, significant apoptosis of differentiated spermatogonia was observed as early as 10 dpp, which accelerated to a peak at 12 dpp, with few spermatogonia entering meiosis. These findings suggest that the absence of PCID2 can lead to developmental arrest of differentiated spermatogonia and that PCID2 may also be involved in the initiation of meiosis.</p>
<p id="Par56">Based on the results of IP-MS, we found that as a subunit of TREX-2 [<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>], PCID2 can also affect the AS of RNA, participating in the regulation of gene expression and playing an important role in spermatogenesis. This finding is consistent with the function of PCID2 reported by Crespo et al., who recently discovered that PCID2 deficiency causes over-splicing of intron-containing viral RNA in cell lines [<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>], which increases the credibility of our work. Meanwhile, the results of Co-IP also indicated that PCID2 interacted with three key proteins that were highly related to mRNA splicing. SNRPG as a core component of the spliceosome [<a href="#CR47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR47">47</a>], was one of the candidate proteins that most likely to interact with PCID2. We also found that hnRNPH1 and SF3B1 were in the list of candidate proteins interacting with PCID2. Besides, a study reported that conditional knockout <em>Hnrnph1</em> in spermatogenic cells caused male sterility, and the data supported a molecular model wherein hnRNPH1 governs a network of alternative splicing events in germ cells via recruitment of PTBP2 and SRSF3 [<a href="#CR48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR48">48</a>]. Another study found that SF3B1 which also called SAP155, regulates pre-mRNA splicing in spermatogonia and proliferation of spermatogonia and Sertoli cells by phosphorylating [<a href="#CR49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR49">49</a>]. These studies highlighted the importance of hnRNPH1 and SF3B1 in spermatogenesis via regulating splicing, so we also performed the Co-IP of SNRPG, hnRNPH1 and SF3B1. The results indicated that PCID2 regulates alternative splicing events in germ cells via interacting with SNRPG, hnRNPH1 and SF3B1 in spermatogonial differentiation. This study expands our understanding of the functions of PCID2 and TREX-2 in gametogenesis. Both the male and female <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice were infertile. However, the role of PCID2 in oocyte maturation requires further investigations.</p>
<p id="Par57">In this study, regarding the use of RNA-seq data from whole testes for the analysis of AS, we cannot rule out technical limitations, which means that, the identified differentially AS events may be caused by alterations in testicular cell subtypes. To eliminate interference from somatic cells, we re-verified the differentially AS events using c-KIT<sup>+</sup> germ cells isolated from WT and <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice at 10 dpp. Interestingly, during the experimental process, we found that the differentially AS events in isolated germ cells seemed more pronounced than those in the whole testis, further confirming that the differentially AS events in <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice were caused by the loss of PCID2 rather than by the heterogeneity of cell subtypes. Based on the results of rMATS analysis and PCR validation, we found that compared to WT, four genes (<em>Prpf3</em>, <em>Nek3</em>, <em>Dvl2 and Slc30a9</em>) were with differentially AS events in c-Kit<sup>+</sup> germ cells from <em>Pcid2</em>-SKO mice. <em>Prpf3</em> encodes pre-mRNA processing factor 3 (<em>PRPF3</em>), a component of the U4/U6 di-snRNP, which is indispensable for the assembly of the U4/U6•U5 tri-snRNP and its attachment to active spliceosomes, a process that is crucial for the efficiency of pre-mRNA splicing [<a href="#CR52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR52">52</a>, <a href="#CR53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR53">53</a>]. NEK3 is a member of the never in mitosis A (NIMA)-related kinase (NEK) family, which is a group of serine/threonine kinases related to NIMA kinase [<a href="#CR54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR54">54</a>, <a href="#CR55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR55">55</a>]. Upon activation by prolactin, NEK3 promotes the migration and invasion of breast cancer cells, as well as the rearrangement of the actin cytoskeleton, which is essential for such cellular activities [<a href="#CR56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR56">56</a>]. The segment polarity protein dishevelled homologue DVL-2 (DVL2) participates in both canonical and non-canonical Wnt signaling pathways by interacting with the cytoplasmic C-terminus of frizzled receptors, transmitting Wnt signals to the downstream effectors [<a href="#CR57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR57">57</a>]. SLC30A9 (ZnT9) is a mitochondria-resident zinc transporter [<a href="#CR58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR58">58</a>]. Deficiency of ZnT9 within the germline of mice resulted in more extreme effects, the mutant embryos displayed severe developmental defects and died during mid-gestation [<a href="#CR59" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR59">59</a>], indicating the essential functions of ZnT9 and mitochondrial zinc homeostasis in mammalian development. Therefore, PCID2 may be involved in spermatonial differentiation by regulating the AS of genes related to spliceosome assembly, cell cycle, and modulation of mitochondrial homeostasis. Recent studies have reported that hnRNPU and the RNA helicase DDX5 play roles during SSC pool establishment in mice by regulating the AS of cell-cycle-related genes [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>, <a href="#CR60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR60">60</a>], which is similar to the mechanism by which PCID2 participates in spermatogenesis.</p>
<p id="Par58">In this study, we have indicated that PCID2 is a key splicing regulator in spermatogonial differentiation, and provided evidence that PCID2 primarily modulates exon skipping during spermatogenesis. Specifically, we have confirmed that genes involved in the regulation of the cell cycle, spliceosome assembly and mitochondrial homeostasis are among the targets affected by AS. Our results suggest that PCID2-mediated AS is crucial for spermatogonial differentiation. This study significantly enhances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying spermatogonial development, and offers new insights into the genetic basis of male fertility.</p></section><section id="Sec25"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h2>
