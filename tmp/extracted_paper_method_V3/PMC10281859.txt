</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="s1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Motivation</h3>
<p>Tandem mass spectrometry is an essential technology for characterizing chemical compounds at high sensitivity and throughput, and is commonly adopted in many fields. However, computational methods for automated compound identification from their MS/MS spectra are still limited, especially for novel compounds that have not been previously characterized. In recent years, <em>in silico</em> methods were proposed to predict the MS/MS spectra of compounds, which can then be used to expand the reference spectral libraries for compound identification. However, these methods did not consider the compounds’ 3D conformations, and thus neglected critical structural information.</p></section><section id="s2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>We present the <strong>3D Mol</strong>ecular Network for <strong>M</strong>ass <strong>S</strong>pectra Prediction (3DMolMS), a deep neural network model to predict the MS/MS spectra of compounds from their 3D conformations. We evaluated the model on the experimental spectra collected in several spectral libraries. The results showed that 3DMolMS predicted the spectra with the average cosine similarity of 0.691 and 0.478 with the experimental MS/MS spectra acquired in positive and negative ion modes, respectively. Furthermore, 3DMolMS model can be generalized to the prediction of MS/MS spectra acquired by different labs on different instruments through minor fine-tuning on a small set of spectra. Finally, we demonstrate that the <em>molecular representation</em> learned by 3DMolMS from MS/MS spectra prediction can be adapted to enhance the prediction of chemical properties such as the elution time in the liquid chromatography and the collisional cross section measured by ion mobility spectrometry, both of which are often used to improve compound identification.</p></section><section id="s3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Availability and implementation</h3>
<p>The codes of 3DMolMS are available at <a href="https://github.com/JosieHong/3DMolMS" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://github.com/JosieHong/3DMolMS</a> and the web service is at <a href="https://spectrumprediction.gnps2.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://spectrumprediction.gnps2.org</a>.</p></section></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1 Introduction</h2>
<p>Because of its high sensitivity and throughput, mass spectrometry (MS) coupled with gas chromatography (GC) or liquid chromatography (LC) has long been adopted for the characterization and structural elucidation of chemical compounds (<a href="#btad354-B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B31">Vinaixa <em>et al.</em> 2016</a>). More recently, liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), which detects the fragment ions resulting from the high energy collision of compounds in a collision cell has become an essential technology for identifying and quantifying chemical compounds in complex samples with applications in multiple domain areas (<a href="#btad354-B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B22">Richardson 2008</a>, <a href="#btad354-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B10">Hoffmann <em>et al.</em> 2014</a>, <a href="#btad354-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B2">Alseekh <em>et al.</em> 2021</a>). For instance, metabolomics aims to identify and quantify metabolites present in tissues and body fluids, leading to the discovery of molecular biomarkers associated with diseases and clinical conditions (<a href="#btad354-B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B18">Nagana Gowda and Raftery 2013</a>). Specifically, in untargeted metabolomics, LC-MS/MS is used to acquire thousands of MS/MS spectra in a single sample, from which metabolites are to be characterized (<a href="#btad354-B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B38">Xiao <em>et al.</em> 2012</a>). Many MS-based metabolite identification systems exploited the spectra searching against a reference spectral library consisting of the MS/MS spectra of previously identified chemical compounds (<a href="#btad354-B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B27">Stein 2012</a>). In practice, however, the current spectral libraries [e.g. NIST20 (<a href="#btad354-B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B39">Yang <em>et al.</em> 2017</a>), HMDB (<a href="#btad354-B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B37">Wishart <em>et al.</em> 2022</a>), MassBank (<a href="#btad354-B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B11">Horai <em>et al.</em> 2010</a>), and GNPS (<a href="#btad354-B33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B33">Wang <em>et al.</em> 2016</a>)] only contain the experimental MS/MS spectra of a limited number of compounds (e.g. the NIST20 and GNPS spectral libraries contain the MS/MS spectra of 29 890 and 18 163 compounds, respectively, while the other libraries are even smaller), and thus a majority (i.e. over 80%) of MS/MS spectra in metabolomic experiments remain un-identified by the spectral library searching methods (<a href="#btad354-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B8">Griss <em>et al.</em> 2016</a>). Notably, compound identification remains a big obstacle in the other applications of LC-MS/MS such as in environmental chemistry (<a href="#btad354-B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B22">Richardson 2008</a>) and natural product discovery (<a href="#btad354-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B10">Hoffmann <em>et al.</em> 2014</a>), in which the fraction of unknown compounds in a target sample is even greater.</p>
<p>To address this problem, computational methods have been developed for compound identification from their MS/MS spectra (<a href="#btad354-B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B3">Blaženović <em>et al.</em> 2018</a>). Among them, one approach aims at the <em>in silico</em> prediction of MS/MS spectra from compounds and then uses the predicted spectra of potential small molecules in the entire chemical space (e.g. those in the PubChem database) for the spectral library searching. Many of the spectral prediction algorithms adopted a rule-based approach, in which empirical and/or computer-learned rules are devised and combined to derive the fragment ions and their intensities, e.g. those implemented in LipidBlast (<a href="#btad354-B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B13">Kind <em>et al.</em> 2013</a>), MetFrag (<a href="#btad354-B25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B25">Ruttkies <em>et al.</em> 2016</a>), MIDAS (<a href="#btad354-B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B34">Wang <em>et al.</em> 2014</a>), and CFM-ID (<a href="#btad354-B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B32">Wang <em>et al.</em> 2021</a>). The state-of-the-art rule-based method for predicting MS/MS spectra of compounds is CFM-ID 4.0 (<a href="#btad354-B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B32">Wang <em>et al.</em> 2021</a>). Given the chemical structure of a compound, CFM-ID first computes a feature vector of potential fragments and then uses empirical fragmentation rules to compute the putative chemical bond cleavages and the charges of these fragments. Finally, an MS/MS spectrum is predicted by combining the empirical and computer-learned (by using machine learning algorithms) fragmentation rules.</p>
<p>Recently, with the cumulative MS/MS spectra of small molecules enabled by the efforts of several large-scale spectral libraries, it has become plausible to train deep learning models for predicting MS/MS spectra of chemical compounds. In fact, deep learning models have been successfully adopted to predict the MS/MS spectra of peptides, not only for the prediction of the fragment ions’ intensities (<a href="#btad354-B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B7">Gessulat <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>, <a href="#btad354-B30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B30">Tiwary <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>, <a href="#btad354-B40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B40">Yang <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>, <a href="#btad354-B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B29">Tarn and Zeng 2021</a>) but also for the prediction of full MS/MS spectra from scratch (<a href="#btad354-B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B16">Liu <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>), which demonstrated the power of deep neural networks (DNNs) for learning complex rules and patterns from massive training data. Despite the success of DNNs in peptide MS/MS spectra prediction, their application to compound spectra prediction is limited, mainly because unlike peptides that can be represented as linear sequences, small molecules are represented as molecular graphs, and thus the sequence-to-sequence deep learning models cannot be directly exploited. On the other hand, machine learning methods have been widely used to predict the chemical properties of compounds. For these prediction tasks, an essential issue is how to represent chemical compounds as the input to the DNNs. A rule-based method takes as input the stereochemical information of the compounds by encoding them into <em>fingerprints</em>, like Extended-Connectivity Fingerprints (ECFP) (<a href="#btad354-B24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B24">Rogers and Hahn 2010</a>). However, rule-based encoding limits the capabilities of deep learning models, which are capable of learning new rules directly from training data. The current DNNs for learning compound representations fall into three categories: SMILES-based methods, graph-based methods, and point-based methods. A SMILES-based convolutional neural network (CNN) (<a href="#btad354-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B9">Hirohara <em>et al.</em> 2018</a>) encodes the SMILES (i.e. Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System) strings by one-hot coding, while CHEM-BERT (<a href="#btad354-B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B12">Kim <em>et al.</em> 2021</a>) encodes SMILES strings as embedding vectors, both of which do not contain any structural information of compounds. The graph-based methods encode a compound as a molecular graph, in which the nodes represent atoms, and the edges represent chemical bonds, and then exploit the molecular graph as the input to various graph neural networks (GNNs), e.g. the Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) (<a href="#btad354-B35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B35">Wang <em>et al.</em> 2022</a>) or the Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) (<a href="#btad354-B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B6">Gasteiger <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>, <a href="#btad354-B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B14">Klicpera <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>). However, the bond types are restricted in graph-based methods; for instance, intra-molecular hydrogen bonds are not directly modeled. The point-based methods exploit the atomistic systems (chemical bonds are not taken into account by these methods) and encode the atoms as points in DNNs, e.g. in SchNet (<a href="#btad354-B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B26">Schütt <em>et al.</em> 2018</a>). In SchNet, the continuous-filter convolutional layers are designed to model the atoms, but only the distances among atoms are considered. To the best of our knowledge, NEIMS (<a href="#btad354-B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B36">Wei <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>), and MassFormer (<a href="#btad354-B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B41">Young <em>et al.</em> 2021</a>) are the only existing neural network models for the compound MS/MS spectra prediction, which encode the small molecules as ECFP and molecular graphs, respectively, and then exploit the multilayer perceptron (MLP) neural network and Graph Transformers (<a href="#btad354-B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B42">Yun <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>) for spectra prediction, respectively.</p>
<p>In this article, we present an efficient deep neural network, 3DMolMS, based on an elemental operation of 3D molecular convolution on the 3D conformations of compounds to predict their MS/MS spectra. In 3DMolMS, the 3D conformations are represented as <em>point sets</em> (<a href="#btad354-B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B21">Qi <em>et al.</em> 2017</a>, <a href="#btad354-B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B17">Manessi <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>), which is a commonly used representation in computer vision to simulate the objects in the real world. The molecular point sets encode the accurate 3D coordinates and attributes of the atoms, while the chemical bonds are represented in <em>neighboring vectors</em>. When trained and evaluated on the compounds’ MS/MS spectra collected from the Agilent Personal Compound Database and Library (PCDL) and NIST20, 3DMolMS outperforms CFM-ID 4.0 (<a href="#btad354-B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B32">Wang <em>et al.</em> 2021</a>), NEIMS (<a href="#btad354-B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B36">Wei <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>), and the existing deep learning model MassFormer (<a href="#btad354-B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B41">Young <em>et al.</em> 2021</a>). Furthermore, 3DMolMS achieves satisfactory performance when applied to the prediction of the spectra in the public spectral library MassBank of North America (MoNA) with minor fine-tuning, which indicates the 3DMolMS model can be generalized to the prediction of spectra acquired by different labs on different instruments. Finally, we showed the representation learned in spectra prediction can be adapted to enhance the prediction of chemical properties of compounds such as the elution time (ET) in liquid chromatography, and the collisional cross section (CCS) in the ion mobility spectrometry (IMS), both of which are often used as complementary measures in compound identification.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2 Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1 MS/MS spectral libraries</h3>
<p>To train and evaluate the deep learning model for MS/MS spectra prediction, we gathered about 70 000 spectra, all acquired using the Agilent quadrupole/time-of-flight (Q-TOF) MS instruments, from the Agilent Personal Compound Database and Library (PCDL) and the NIST20 spectral library (<a href="#btad354-B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B39">Yang <em>et al.</em> 2017</a>) (<a href="#btad354-T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). We combined these two libraries for training purposes because (i) although PCDL contains the spectra from a large number (11 239) of compounds, NIST20 provides somewhat complementary data: it collected the experimental MS/MS spectra from 2492 compounds, among which only 532 (i.e. 21%) compounds are also collected by PCDL; (ii) NIST20 collected multiple spectra from the same compound (on average, 11 spectra per compound), which can be exploited by the deep learning models to model the experimental variations among replicated MS/MS acquisitions; and (iii) most (96%) Q-TOF MS/MS spectra in NIST20 were acquired by using Agilent Q-TOF instruments (we only kept these spectra in our training and testing data), and as a result, the MS/MS spectra of the compounds shared by both libraries are highly similar, with the average cosine similarities of 0.870 and 0.982 for the MS/MS spectra acquired in the positive and negative ion modes [Molecules are ionized in mass spectrometers through either protonation (i.e. the <em>positive ion mode</em>) or deprotonation (i.e. the <em>negative ion mode</em>).], respectively. Notably, we used the experimental MS/MS spectra of 90% randomly selected compounds in the combined library for the training, and the spectra of the remaining 10% compounds for testing to ensure the training and testing datasets are not overlapping with each other.</p>

<p>We used the ETKDG (<a href="#btad354-B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B23">Riniker and Landrum 2015</a>) algorithm implemented in the <kbd>AllChem.EmbedMolecule()</kbd> function of the RDkit library. (Open-source cheminformatics. <a href="https://www.rdkit.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.rdkit.org/</a>.) to generate the 3D conformations (also referred to as the <em>conformers</em>.) The function takes the SMILES string of each compound as input, and outputs the 3D conformer in the <kbd>sdf.gz</kbd> format, which contains the <em>x</em>, <em>y</em>, <em>z</em>-coordinates of each atom in the compound. When the number of atoms in a compound is smaller than <em>N</em>, the point set is padded to <em>N</em> points with zeros. <p>We represent an experimental MS/MS spectrum as a 1D spectral vector, in which each dimension represents the total intensity of fragment ions in a bin of the fixed mass-to-charge ratio (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em>). Here, the total number of bins (i.e. the dimension of the spectral vector) is dependent on the mass resolution of the MS/MS spectrum and is set as an adjustable parameter in our model. By default, the resolution is set to be 0.2, resulting in the spectral vector of 7500 dimensions (within the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> range between 0 and 1500 that covers almost all fragment ions observed in the MS/MS spectra of compounds).</p>
<p>In practice, the peak intensities are often transformed to reduce the impact of the most intensive peaks when computing the similarities between spectra, in particular, to separate the similarity distribution of replicated spectra of the same compound from the distribution between the spectra of different compounds. Based on the previous studies [e.g. for peptide spectra prediction (<a href="#btad354-B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B16">Liu <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>)], we adopted the <em>square root</em> transformation, i.e. the intensity of each peak is converted into its square root. We note that the logarithm transformation is also used in previous studies (<a href="#btad354-B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B15">Lam <em>et al.</em> 2006</a>), which can achieve a similar effect.</p>
<p>Finally, we consider the MS experimental conditions, including the precursor types and the collision energy as the metadata, which are concatenated to the embedded point set. <p>Point-based DNNs are widely used in computer vision (<a href="#btad354-B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B21">Qi <em>et al.</em> 2017</a>, <a href="#btad354-B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B17">Manessi <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>). <p>Based on the elemental operation 3DMolConv, we constructed the 3DMolMS model as illustrated in <a href="#btad354-F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>. The input of the network is the encoding vectors of the <em>x</em>, <em>y</em>, <em>z</em>-coordinates and attributes of all atoms in a compound, represented in a matrix. The input matrix is then processed by an encoder consisting of six 3DMolConv-based hidden layers with the dimensions of 64, 64, 128, 256, 512, and 1024, respectively. All molecular representation vectors from the hidden layers in the encoder are concatenated together with the metadata to form the final latent representation vector in 2048 dimensions. Notably, the <em>x</em>, <em>y</em>, <em>z</em>-coordinates are not directly encoded after the first layer in the encoder to ensure the rotation invariance of the model representation, as explained in <a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Section S1</a>. The latent representation vector from the encoder is then fed into a spectrum decoder consisting of five fully connected layers with residual blocks to output the predicted spectrum. All hidden layers in the decoder have the same dimension of 2048 (see <a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S1</a>). After the predicted MS/MS spectrum is output by 3DMolMS, we eliminate all the peaks whose intensities are smaller than 0.001 (i.e. 0.1% of the total intensity of the spectrum), and then convert the intensities of the remaining peaks into their square in the final predicted spectrum.</p>

<p>Through 3DMolMS, each compound is embedded into a 2048D latent vector by the encoder, which contains sufficient information to predict the MS/MS spectra of the compound. We hypothesize that this molecular representation learned from MS/MS spectra prediction captures essential structural information about the compounds, which can be transferred to the relevant prediction tasks, such as predicting chemical properties. As a proof of concept, we demonstrate this transfer learning approach indeed improves the prediction of the ET and the CCS (<a href="#btad354-B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B20">Plante <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>, <a href="#btad354-B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B43">Zhou <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>) and ET (<a href="#btad354-B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B5">Domingo-Almenara <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>) of compounds. Specifically, the weights in the encoder of the pretrained spectra prediction model are directly utilized to generate the compound’s representation vector, which is then fed into another decoder to predict the respective properties. The representation learning is fine-tuned by the respective training dataset of the task, while the decoder is trained independently. In this article, we used the same decoder architecture for the prediction of CCS and ET, which includes five layers of fully connected layers.</p></section></section><section id="sec12"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3 Results</h2>
<section id="sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1 Performance of predicting MS/MS spectra acquired by using Agilent Q-TOF instruments</h3>
<p>We implemented 3DMolMS in the PyTorch framework (<a href="#btad354-B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B19">Paszke <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>). It takes about 0.13 s to predict an MS/MS spectrum from a single input compound, while the additional 0.22 s is needed for the computation of the compound’s 3D structure using RDkit (<a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Section S2</a>). In comparison, it takes around 0.03, 0.05, and 24.1 s for NEIMS, MassFormer, and CFM-ID 4.0 to predict the MS/MS spectrum of a compound.</p>
<p>We compare our model against the existing mass spectra prediction methods, including CFM-ID (<a href="#btad354-B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B32">Wang <em>et al.</em> 2021</a>), NEIMS (<a href="#btad354-B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B36">Wei <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>), and MassFormer (<a href="#btad354-B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B41">Young <em>et al.</em> 2021</a>). NEIMS and MassFormer are retrained by using our training set for a direct comparison. Because the training codes of CFM-ID are not publicly available and the precursor types supported by the software are different from ours, we selected the 3482 and 770 spectra in the testing set with the adduct ions of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE17"><math id="IM17" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msup><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">M</mi><mo>+</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">H</mi><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>+</mo></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE18"><math id="IM18" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msup><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">M</mi><mo>−</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">H</mi><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>−</mo></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span>, respectively, for the evaluation. In addition, we adapted three baselines point-based deep learning methods for MS/MS spectra prediction, including SchNet (<a href="#btad354-B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B26">Schütt <em>et al.</em> 2018</a>) developed for molecular representation learning, and PointNet (<a href="#btad354-B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B21">Qi <em>et al.</em> 2017</a>), and DGCNN (<a href="#btad354-B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B17">Manessi <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>) developed for computer vision tasks. All these models are used to embed the same input point set into a latent representation vector, which is combined with the same metadata and then converted by the same decoder into the final predicted MS/MS spectrum. Cosine distance is used as the loss function for training all the DNNs. The detailed settings of the comparative experiment are described in <a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Section S3</a>.</p>
<p>As depicted in <a href="#btad354-F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>, 3DMolMS predicted the MS/MS spectra that are highly similar to the experimental spectra, with the cosine similarity of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE19"><math id="IM19" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mn>0.691</mn><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mo>±</mo><mn>0.361</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE20"><math id="IM20" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mn>0.478</mn><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mo>±</mo><mn>0.379</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math></span> for the adduct ions of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE21"><math id="IM21" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msup><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">M</mi><mo>+</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">H</mi><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>+</mo></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE22"><math id="IM22" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msup><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">M</mi><mo>−</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">H</mi><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>−</mo></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span>, respectively. The performances of all point-based DNN models (3DMolMS, SchNet, PointNet, and DGCNN) are better than existing spectra prediction algorithms (MassFormer, NEIMS, and CFM-ID), while 3DMolMS outperforms the baseline point-based DNN models. Whitney U-test confirms that 3DMolMS significantly outperforms all other methods on <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE23"><math id="IM23" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msup><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">M</mi><mo>+</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">H</mi><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>+</mo></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span> ion mode and significantly outperforms NEIMS and CFM-ID on <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE24"><math id="IM24" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msup><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">M</mi><mo>−</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">H</mi><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>−</mo></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span> ion mode. All deep learning methods including 3DMolMS perform better on the spectra acquired under the positive ion mode than those under the negative ion mode, which is due to the fact that the training set of the positive ion mode is substantially larger than that of the negative ion mode (<a href="#btad354-T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). As shown in <a href="#btad354-F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>, the accuracy of predictions significantly improves with an increased number of training samples, suggesting room for further enhancements in these models, including 3DMolMS. It is important to note that CFM-ID is designed for all Electrospray ionization (ESI) spectra, but variations may exist among spectra from different instruments. Consequently, the predicted spectra by CFM-ID may not represent the optimal prediction for experimental spectra with the same collision energy. To ensure a fair comparison, we consider the maximum cosine similarity between the experimental spectra and the three predicted spectra at different collision energies (10, 20, and 40 eV) in our evaluation of CFM-ID.</p>

</div></div></section><p>We then investigate the prediction performance of 3DMolMS across different types of compounds. As expected, we observed that the prediction performance of 3DMolMS on a compound (in the testing set) is correlated with its chemical similarity with the compounds in the training set. Here, we used the Bulk Tanimoto similarity (based on the chemical fingerprints) (<a href="#btad354-B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B28">Tanimoto 1958</a>) to measure the structural similarity between two compounds, and for each compound in the testing set, we measured its maximum similarity with any compound in the training set. As shown in <a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S3</a>, in general, the prediction performance increases when more similar compounds are present in the training set. It is not surprising that the model performs relatively better on the compounds with similar chemical structures because their similar compounds have been seen in the training set and thus the model has learned relevant fragmentation rules and patterns related to such compounds. This result also indicates the performance of 3DMolMS will be further enhanced if we expand the training set to cover the compounds with higher structural diversity. The performances of 3DMolMS using different diversity training samples in <a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S4</a> agree with this hypothesis.</p></section><section id="sec14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2 Extension of spectra prediction for other Q-TOF instruments</h3>
<p>Next, we attempted to evaluate the performance of 3DMolMS on the prediction of MS/MS spectra acquired using the non-Agilent Q-TOF instruments (e.g. from the vendors of Bruker and Waters) collected in the MoNA spectral library. We first excluded about 150 compounds in the MoNA library that are also present in the PCDL or NIST20 libraries, and then randomly split the remaining compounds and their spectra into two subsets: one subset with 50% compounds for fine-tuning, and the other subset with 50% compounds for testing. As shown in <a href="#btad354-F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>, when we trained a 3DMolMS model from scratch on the fine-tuning subset, the predicted QTOF MS/MS spectra on the testing subset of the MoNA library achieved the cosine similarities of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE25"><math id="IM25" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mn>0.445</mn><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mo>±</mo><mn>0.379</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE26"><math id="IM26" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mn>0.236</mn><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mo>±</mo><mn>0.300</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math></span> for the positive and negative ion modes, respectively. On the other hand, when we fine-tuned the 3DMolMS model trained on the combined PCDL/NIST20 libraries using the fine-tuning subset, the prediction accuracies were significantly enhanced: the predicted spectra achieved the cosine similarities of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE27"><math id="IM27" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mn>0.506</mn><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mo>±</mo><mn>0.316</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE28"><math id="IM28" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mn>0.430</mn><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mo>±</mo><mn>0.322</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math></span> for the positive and negative ion modes spectra in the same testing data, respectively. For comparison, the spectra predicted by CFM-ID (following the same procedure described in Section 3.1) achieved the cosine similarities of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE29"><math id="IM29" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mn>0.076</mn><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mo>±</mo><mn>0.127</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IE30"><math id="IM30" display="inline" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mn>0.291</mn><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mo>±</mo><mn>0.248</mn><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math></span> for the positive and negative ion modes spectra, respectively. The other spectra prediction methods (NEIMS and MassFormer) performed even more poorly, perhaps because of the small training dataset used here. Note that the spectra in the MoNA library are acquired from various Q-TOF instruments. As a result, the similarities between replicated spectra of the same compounds are not as high. As shown in <a href="#btad354-F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>, the average cosine similarities between the spectra of the same compounds in the MoNA library are 0.655 and 0.804 for the spectra acquired in the positive and negative ion modes, respectively, which are much lower than the similarities (i.e. 0.804 and 0.979, respectively) in the combined PCDL/NIST20 libraries that were acquired by using the Agilent Q-TOF instrument. Hence, our results showed 3DMolMS may be extended to the prediction of MS/MS spectra acquired by different instruments after the fine-tuning on a small set of experimental spectra, even though the prediction accuracy might be relatively lower than the original model trained on the large training set. We also note the performance of 3DMolMS is better on the spectra in positive ion mode than on those in negative ion mode, because of the fewer training samples in negative ion mode in both the combined PCDL/NIST20 libraries used to train the original model and the MoNA library for fine-tuning.</p>

<p>Finally, we investigate the transferability of the latent molecular representation learned from spectra prediction to the tasks of predicting chemical properties of compounds, specifically, their ET in liquid chromatography (LC) and their CCS measured by IMS. For these two regression tasks, we utilized the training datasets collected in previous studies to build customized machine learning models, including the <em>SMRT</em> dataset (<a href="#btad354-B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B5">Domingo-Almenara <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>) for ET prediction and the <em>AllCCS</em> dataset (<a href="#btad354-B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B43">Zhou <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>) for CCS prediction. We randomly split the compounds in the <em>SMRT</em> dataset into the training and test subsets with the ratio of 9:1. For the comparison with the customized CCS prediction method ALLCCS (<a href="#btad354-B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B43">Zhou <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>), which was trained on the whole AllCCS dataset but was not shared in open-source, we used the entire ALLCCS as the training set and used another CCS dataset from Bush Lab (<a href="#btad354-B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B4">Bush <em>et al.</em> 2010</a>) (<em>BushCCS</em>), which does not overlap with AllCCS, as the testing set for model evaluation. The sizes of the training and testing datasets for the ET and CCS prediction are summarized in <a href="#btad354-T4" class="usa-link">Table 4</a>.</p>

</div></div></section><p>To compare the prediction performance, we computed the coefficient of determination (<em>R</em><sup>2</sup>) and the mean absolute error (MAE) between the predicted and the experimentally measured ET and CCS. As shown in <a href="#btad354-T4" class="usa-link">Table 4</a>, the 3DMolMS model trained from scratch on the respective training data achieved better performance than the customized ET and CCS prediction tools SMRT-DLM (<a href="#btad354-B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B5">Domingo-Almenara <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>), ALLCCS (<a href="#btad354-B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B43">Zhou <em>et al.</em> 2020</a>), and DeepCCS (<a href="#btad354-B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="btad354-B20">Plante <em>et al.</em> 2019</a>), respectively on both the <em>R</em><sup>2</sup> and the MAE metrics, while the model trained on the transferred molecular representations (denoted as “3DMolMS-TL”) further improved the performance of 3DMolMS. In particular, the improvement in the CCS prediction is very significant, perhaps because CCS is highly correlated with the 3D structure of the compounds, which are explicitly modeled by 3DMolMS. These results suggest that the latent molecular representations learned from MS/MS spectra can be exploited to enhance the performance of predicting the chemical properties of compounds.</p></section></section><section id="sec16"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4 Conclusion</h2>
<p>In this article, we designed an elemental convolution-based operation to extract feature representations from 3D molecular conformations, and developed 3DMolMS, a point-based deep neural network (DNN) to predict MS/MS spectra compounds. The elemental operation is proved to be invariant to permutation and SE(3), which is essential for point-based representation learning. As our experiments showed, the 3DMolMS model can give accurate predictions of compounds’ MS/MS spectra and outperform existing MS/MS spectra prediction algorithms (e.g. NEIMS, MassFormer, and CFM-ID 4.0) as well as baseline point-based DNN models (e.g. PointNet, DGCNN, and SchNet) equipped with the same decoder as 3DMolMS for MS/MS spectra prediction. Finally, we showed that the molecular representation learned from MS/MS spectra prediction can be used to enhance the prediction of chemical properties of compounds, including the ET and the collision cross section (CCS).</p>
<p>We anticipate that accurately predicted spectra can expand the reference libraries, which, combined with the predicted CCS and ET, can improve compound identification using tandem mass spectrometry. We plan to test the application of our methods to large-scale compound identification (e.g. for untargeted metabolomics) in our future works. It is also worth noting that there is plenty of room for further improvement of MS/MS spectra prediction, in particular by increasing the coverage and diversity of the compounds in the training datasets.</p></section><section id="sec17"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
