</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="Par1">The effect of subculture cycles on somaclonal variation of <em>V. planifolia</em> using intersimple sequence repeat (ISSR) markers was analyzed. Nodal segments of 2 cm in length were established in vitro and multiplied by 10 subculture cycles in Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 8.86 μM BAP (benzylaminopurine). After 45 days in each culture, the length and number of shoots per explant were evaluated. For ISSR markers, ten shoots per each subculture and the mother plant were used. Ten ISSR primers were used and a total of 118 bands were obtained. The polymorphism (%) was calculated and a dendrogram based on Jaccard’s genetic distance between the subcultures and the donor plant was obtained. These results show that the multiplication rate tends to increase until subculture five, whereas shoot length decreases as the number of subcultures increases. The ISSR markers revealed an increase in the polymorphism percentage after the fifth culture cycle. The dendrogram showed the formation of two groups. The first group, with less genetic variability, is the donor plant and subcultures 1–5; the second group has greater genetic distance and is formed by subcultures 6–10. The results revealed that the number of subcultures with 8.86 μM BAP is a factor that affects the somaclonal variation during in vitro regeneration of <em>V. planifolia</em>. In conclusion, the subculture number affects somaclonal variation and in vitro development of <em>V. planifolia</em>.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> <em>Vanilla planifolia</em>, Regeneration, Somaclonal variation, Micropropagation, ISSR</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par2">In vitro plant regeneration is an effective tool for generation of high-quality, disease-free and uniform planting material (Azman et al. <a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2014</a>). However, even though micropropagation provides many advantages, genetic instability has been observed. Larkin and Scowcroft (<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">1981</a>) described somaclonal variation as the epigenetic and genetic changes of in vitro plant regeneration. According to Lázaro-Castellanos et al. (<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">2018</a>), genetic instability is a problem during commercial micropropagation. However, contrary to the negative effects of somaclonal variation, genetic changes can also be an important tool for generation of new varieties that could exhibit disease resistance, abiotic stress tolerance and improvement in quality and yield (Jain et al. <a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">2013</a>; Krishna et al. <a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">2016</a>).</p>
<p id="Par3">During in vitro culture, the type and concentration of growth regulators, propagation methods and the number as well as the duration of subcultures are key factors that determine the frequency of somaclonal variation (Krishna et al. <a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">2016</a>). In particular, the number of subcultures performed is strictly related to the levels of somaclonal variation detected (Peng et al. <a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">2015</a>; Martínez-Estrada et al. <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">2017</a>; Mamedes-Rodrigues et al. <a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">2018</a>). Somaclonal variants can be detected using various physiological, morphological, biochemical and molecular methods (Bairu et al. <a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">2011</a>). Among molecular markers, one of the most used for detecting genetic variability is the intersimple sequence repeat (ISSR) (Akdemir et al. <a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">2016</a>; Martínez-Estrada et al. <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">2017</a>; Babu et al. <a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">2018</a>). This technique uses microsatellites as primers in a single primer PCR reaction to amplify inter-SSR sequences of different sizes (Reddy et al. <a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">2002</a>). In addition, ISSR makers are highly polymorphic, the technique is quick, simple, and reproducible and it does not require prior information about the genome (Peng et al. <a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">2015</a>).</p>
<p id="Par4">Vanillin is one of the most valued substances in fragrance, pharmaceutical and food industries worldwide (Bory et al. <a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">2008</a>; Greule et al. <a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">2010</a>) and is extracted from the fruits of the genus <em>Vanilla</em> (Salazar-Rojas et al. <a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">2012</a>), especially <em>Vanilla planifolia,</em> from which 95% of the world production is obtained (Bory et al. <a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">2008</a>). Despite its importance, in Mexico, <em>V. planifolia</em> is considered a species subject to special protection due to the severe damage that its natural habitat has suffered, the premature fall of fruits (Lee-Espinosa et al. <a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">2008</a>), and the reduction of its genetic diversity caused by the conventional cuttings-based propagation method (Gantait and Kundu <a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">2017</a>). Today, there are different protocols for in vitro propagation of V<em>. planifolia</em> (Sreedhar et al. <a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">2007</a>; Lee-Espinosa et al. <a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">2008</a>; Ramos-Castellá et al. <a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">2014</a>; Gantait and Kundu <a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">2017</a>). However, in most of these studies, the levels of somaclonal variation were not analyzed. In this context, high levels of somaclonal variation were reported during regeneration from callus in <em>V. planifolia</em> (Divakaran et al. <a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">2015</a>; Ramírez-Mosqueda and Iglesias-Andreu <a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">2015</a>). These works suggest that indirect morphogenesis is an alternative to generate genetic diversity in this important crop.</p>
<p id="Par5">In order to avoid the work required for the establishment of new explants, it is necessary to know clonal fidelity during subculture number when the objective is commercial micropropagation. In this sense, the aim of this research was to determine the morphological and molecular variation of in vitro-propagated plants of <em>V. planifolia</em> at different subcultures using ISSR molecular markers.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Plant material and in vitro culture establishment</h3>
<p id="Par6">Stems of 40–50 cm in length were cut from 6-month-old <em>V. planifolia</em> plantlets in a commercial plantation in Veracruz, Mexico. The cuttings were kept under greenhouse conditions for 6 months. After that, the leaves were removed and nodal segments (with a bud) of 2 cm in length were cut off for use as explants. These were washed with tap water and commercial soap for 1 h. The nodal segments were immersed in 0.2% (w/v) 50PH Captan (<em>N</em>-trichloromethylthio-4-cyclohexene-1,2-dicarboximide) for 30 min, followed by 70% ethanol (v/v) for 1 min and three rinses with sterile distilled water. Finally, nodal segments were immersed in sodium hypochlorite at 0.6 and 0.3% (v/v) for 10 and 5 min, respectively, and then rinsed three times with sterile distilled water.</p>
<p id="Par7">The explants were individually transferred into 45 mL flasks containing 10 mL of Murashige and Skoog (<a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">1962</a>) basal medium without growth regulators supplemented with 30 g L<sup>−1</sup> sucrose. The MS medium was adjusted to pH 5.7 with 0.5 N sodium hydroxide, and 0.27% (w/v) Phytagel<sup>®</sup> (Sigma Chemical Company, MO, USA) was added as a gelling agent before being sterilized in an autoclave at 120 °C and 117.7 kPa for 15 min. All cultures were incubated at 25 ± 2 °C with a photoperiod of 16 h under fluorescent light (40 to 50 μmol m<sup>−1</sup> s<sup>−1</sup>).</p></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Effect of subculture cycles on shoot multiplication</h3>
<p id="Par8">The effect of subculture cycles on multiplication rate (expressed as number of shoots per explant) and somaclonal variation were evaluated during <em>V. planifolia</em> micropropagation. Vanilla shoots derived from stock cultures multiplied on semisolid MS medium were subcultured. Each subculture consisted of a transfer to a fresh medium. This culture medium consisted of MS basal medium supplemented with 8.86 μM benzylaminopurine (BAP) (Sigma Chemical Company, MO, USA). Three explants were placed in each flask containing 20 mL of medium. The number of shoots per explants and shoot length as expressed in cm were recorded. Fifteen explants per subculture were used and this was considered as a replicate. Ten subcultures were performed at 45-day intervals over a total period of 450 days.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Experimental design and data analysis</h3>
<p id="Par9">The experiments were conducted using a completely randomized design. All experiments were carried out in triplicate. The statistical analysis was performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Tukey’s comparison of means test (<em>P</em> ≤ 0.05) was performed using SPSS statistical software (version 22 for Windows). When required, data were transformed to natural logarithm (In) to achieve normality and equal variance.</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">DNA isolation</h3>
<p id="Par10">Leaf samples (0.5 g) from the mother plant and leaves from ten randomly-selected shoots per culture cycle were used for DNA extraction. The extraction was performed according to the CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) method (Stewart and Via <a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">1993</a>). The extraction was carried out in duplicate for each sample. The amount and purity of genomic DNA were determined by spectrophotometry (Genesys 10S UVVIS, Thermo Scientific, IL, USA). DNA integrity was verified in 1% (<em>w</em>/<em>v</em>) agarose gel stained with 2.53 µM ethidium bromide (Sigma-Aldrich<sup>®</sup>), visualized in a UV light using a GelDoc-It photodocumentation system (UVP, Upland, Canada).</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">ISSR-PCR analysis</h3>
<p id="Par11">To screen genomic DNA polymorphism in <em>V. planifolia</em>, 30 ISSR primers (Sigma-Aldrich<sup>®</sup>) were evaluated. Based on resolution and reproducibility of banding patterns, 10 primers were selected (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>).</p>

The NJ clustering was performed after bootstrapping 1000 times. The results were obtained using the Past v 3.04 program (Hammer et al. <a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">2001</a>) and were expressed as a dendrogram.</p></section></section><section id="Sec9"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results and Discussion</h2>
<section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Effect of subculture cycles on shoot multiplication</h3>
<p id="Par14">The number and length of shoots showed significant differences among subculture cycles. The lowest number of shoots per explant was observed during subcultures one to five, while the maximum multiplication rate was observed in subcultures six to ten. Regarding shoot length, as the number of subcultures increases, a decrease in shoot length is observed (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>).</p>

Similar to our results, multiplication rate and shoot length have been shown to decrease as the number of subcultures increases. In an early study, Lee (<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">1987</a>) evaluated the in vitro multiplication of <em>Saccharum</em> spp. during ten subcultures and observed a reduction in shoot regeneration between the fifth and tenth subculture, obtaining morphologically normal shoots until subculture seven. Mendes et al. (<a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">1999</a>) in <em>Musa</em> spp. reported a decrease in shoot production in semisolid medium from the fourth subculture. In <em>Aloe vera,</em> Kanwar et al. (<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">2015</a>) reported that the shoot production in the first subculture is lower compared to those obtained in the sixth subculture. From this subculture, they also observed a decrease in shoot number and length. Recently, Martínez-Estrada et al. (<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">2017</a>) reported that the maximum shoot production of <em>Saccharum</em> spp. in temporary immersion is obtained in the eighth subculture. The increase in somaclonal variation in aged cultures or with several subcultures has been reported in several species such as <em>Saccharum</em> spp. (Lee <a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">1987</a>; Martínez-Estrada et al. <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">2017</a>), <em>Musa</em> spp. (Bairu et al. <a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">2006</a>) and <em>Stevia rebaudiana</em> (Soliman et al. <a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">2014</a>).</p>

Similar results were reported by Soliman et al. (<a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">2014</a>) and Kılınç et al. (<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">2015</a>) in <em>Stevia rebaudiana</em> and <em>Pistacia lentiscus</em>, respectively, in which the greatest genetic instability was found in the highest number of subcultures.</p>
<p id="Par19">Somaclonal variation has been detected in different micropropagated plant species through ISSR markers (Bello-Bello et al. <a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">2014</a>; Martínez-Estrada et al. <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">2017</a>; Babu et al. <a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">2018</a>). However, although <em>V. planifolia</em> micropropagation has been widely studied, there are few reports on the analysis of somaclonal variation. Sreedhar et al. (<a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">2007</a>) reported that there was no somaclonal variation in the long-term culture of <em>V. planifolia</em> using RAPD and ISSR molecular markers. Similarly, Gantait et al. (<a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">2009</a>) reported that there was no somaclonal variation after five subcultures using ISSR markers. These results coincide with those obtained in this study, since the percentage of somaclonal variation detected in the first subcultures is practically null, demonstrating that <em>V. planifolia</em> presents genetic stability at least in the first five subcultures.</p>
<p id="Par20">The presence of somaclonal variation by increasing the subculture cycles could be explained under different hypotheses. Jain (<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">2001</a>) mentioned that the in vitro culture process acts as a mutagenic system and may reprogram during plant regeneration. This effect increases as the time and number of subcultures increases, adding new somaclonal variants to each subculture. Another possibility is related to the strong correlation that has been shown between the multiplication rate and genetic variability, which tended to be higher as the shoot production increased. Additionally, the growth regulators are factors that can induce genetic variability. There is probably an endogenous accumulation of growth regulators in the explant that favors an increase in the multiplication rate during the first subcultures, whereas when high concentrations of growth regulators accumulate, there is an inhibition of growth and differentiation, causing the excess regulators to act as a mutagenic agent. According to Sales and Butardo (<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">2014</a>), several growth regulators, such as NAA (naphthalene acetic acid), 2,4-<span class="font-variant-small-caps">d</span> (2,4-dichlorophenoxy acetic acid) and BAP (6-benzylaminopurine), have been most considered to be responsible for obtaining in vitro genetic instability. In this study, probably the endogenous BAP accumulation together with the number of subcultures caused the increase in somaclonal variation after the fifth subculture.</p>
<p id="Par21">Historically, tissue culture was seen as a method of cloning a particular genotype. It was often considered a sophisticated method of asexual propagation enabling a more rapid rate of propagation (Larkin and Scowcroft <a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">1981</a>). However, somaclonal variation among micropropagated plants derived through the culture of organized meristems (Rani and Raina <a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">2000</a>), vegetative buds (Martínez-Estrada et al. <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">2017</a>) and nodal cuttings (Soliman et al. <a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">2014</a>) was observed.</p>
<p id="Par22">It is widely known that the vanilla crop has low genetic variability due to the asexual propagation system to which it has been subjected for centuries, which limits the opportunity to have a genetically diverse germplasm bank that can be used in breeding programs to face the phytosanitary and production problems of this species (Soto Arenas and Cribb <a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">2010</a>). The fact of finding genetic variability during micropropagation could represent an alternative to broaden the genetic base in <em>V. planifolia.</em> Divakaran et al. <a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">2015</a> and Ramírez-Mosqueda and Iglesias-Andreu <a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">2015</a> reported that <em>V. planifolia</em> presents somaclonal variation during in vitro regeneration from callus. Divakaran et al. (<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">2015</a>) in callus regenerants indicated variations in morphology and 76.25% genetic polymorphism using RAPD profiles, while Ramírez-Mosqueda and Iglesias-Andreu (<a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">2015</a>) using ISSR analyses on regenerated plantlets, revealed the existence of 71.66% polymorphism.</p>
<p id="Par23">In conclusion, the subculture number affects somaclonal variation and in vitro development of <em>V. planifolia</em>. In our study, the polymorphism found after the fifth subculture in MS medium supplemented with 8.86 μM BAP suggests that it could be a promising breeding program system. If the objective is to maintain high genetic fidelity during its micropropagation, it is recommended to multiply an explant by no more than five subcultures. On the other hand, if the aim is to maximize the number of shoots per explant, it is recommended to carry out between five and ten subcultures, with the possibility of presenting high percentages of somaclonal variation. It is recommended that future reports of in vitro regeneration of plants on a commercial scale should indicate in which subculture, the experiment was carried out.</p></section></section><section id="fn-group1" class="fn-group"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Footnotes</h2>
