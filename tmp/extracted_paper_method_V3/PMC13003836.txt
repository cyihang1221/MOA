</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="s0001"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Objective</h2>
<p>This exploratory metabolomics pilot study employed non-targeted liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS) to characterize serum metabolic profiles in elite male water polo athletes, thereby assessing physiological adaptation to high-intensity training. We aimed to provide a scientific basis for evaluating physical fitness and optimizing performance capacity in elite athletes.</p></section><section id="s0002"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>
<p>Sixteen male water polo athletes of the Chinese national team were recruited. All athletes underwent a one-week complete break following the end of the previous competitive season to mitigate accumulated fatigue and establish a true resting metabolic baseline. Fasting venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected at 7:00 AM on two time points: the first sample (E1) was collected before commencement of the official training week, and the second sample (E2) was collected immediately after the completion of that week of training. The data were analyzed via XCMS, MetaboAnalyst 6.0, SPSS 21.0, and GraphPad Prism.</p></section><section id="s0003"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<p>(1) Metabolomic analysis identified 363 metabolites in total, 33 of which were differentially expressed between pre- and post-training time points. After one week of routine training, 11 metabolites were significantly up-regulated (<em><em>p</em> &lt;</em> 0.01), and 22 were significantly down-regulated (<em>p &lt;</em> 0.01). (2) KEGG pathway analysis identified the top eight metabolic pathways, with MetPA further highlighting lysine degradation (<em>p &lt;</em> 0.01) and vitamin B6 metabolism (<em>p &lt;</em> 0.05) as key altered pathways. (3) Three metabolites were identified as potential markers associated with the training week changes in water polo athletes on the basis of significant alterations post-training. N6, N6, N6-trimethyl-L-lysine (<em>p &lt;</em> 0.01) and 2-aminoadipic acid (<em>p &lt;</em> 0.01) were significantly decreased, whereas 4-pyridoxic acid (<em>p &lt;</em> 0.01) was significantly increased.</p></section><section id="s0004"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p>Non-targeted LC‒MS/MS provides a valuable tool for monitoring metabolic adaptations at the molecular level in aquatic athletes. In this exploratory study, we observed associated changes in the serum metabolome following intensive training, pointing to adjustments in amino acid and lipid metabolism. These findings offer preliminary insights for guiding fitness and performance optimization.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> High-intensity training, serum metabolomics, non-targeted LC/MS, elite athletes, water polo</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><h2>Graphical abstract</h2>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="uf0001"><p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/2313/13003836/c7b547120b51/RSSN_A_2646628_UF0001_C.jpg" loading="lazy" height="361" width="525" alt="graphic file with name RSSN_A_2646628_UF0001_C.jpg"></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/uf0001/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></figure></section><section id="s0005"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>Metabolomics is an emerging field that focuses on the simultaneous qualitative and quantitative analysis of all low-molecular-weight metabolites in a biological system or cell at a specific physiological state and was initially proposed by Nicholson [<a href="#cit0001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0001">1</a>] in 1999 following the use of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) technology to analyse animal urine. Non-targeted metabolomics is a method that profiles diverse metabolites in biological samples without predefined targets, offering an unbiased view of the metabolic landscape. The advantage of mass spectrometry (MS) lies in its ability to detect and monitor metabolic differences, offering insights into physiological changes and processes occurring at various life stages [<a href="#cit0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0002">2</a>]. The primary objective of metabolomics studies, therefore, is to identify differences between pathological metabolic profiles and normal physiological profiles, facilitating class prediction through metabolite biomarkers [<a href="#cit0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0003">3</a>]. At present, it is widely applied across multiple fields, including medical diagnostics [<a href="#cit0004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0004">4</a>,<a href="#cit0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0005">5</a>], pharmaceutical and drug development [<a href="#cit0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0006">6</a>,<a href="#cit0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0007">7</a>], and agriculture and food science [<a href="#cit0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0008">8</a>,<a href="#cit0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0009">9</a>].</p>
<p>Whole-body exercise responses have been extensively studied to understand the benefits for human health, particularly in relation to metabolism and exercise physiology [<a href="#cit0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0010">10</a>]. Monitoring metabolite biomarkers in response to training provides insights that improve our understanding of training-related adaptations, reflect physiological changes linked to performance, and may support the development of evidence-based approaches for athletes' monitoring and nutritional programming [<a href="#cit0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0011">11–13</a>]. In this context, Moreira et al. [<a href="#cit0014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0014">14</a>] pioneered the use of Raman spectral peaks in urine samples from physically active participants (undergoing 30 min of cycling or running) to identify exercise-associated metabolites (creatine (Cr), ketone bodies, nitrogenous compounds, and phosphate), thereby supporting the monitoring and optimisation of training performance.</p>
<p>Water polo is a physically demanding sport that combines swimming, handball, and volleyball. Research has identified water polo as an intermittent sport characterised by intense bursts of activity lasting less than 15 seconds, interspersed with lower-intensity intervals averaging less than 20 seconds [<a href="#cit0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0015">15</a>], and involving a mix of metabolic energy systems, including the phosphagen system, anaerobic glycolysis, and aerobic oxidation system [<a href="#cit0016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0016">16</a>]. Significant focus has been given to the technical and strategic aspects of water polo; however, research on the physical and physiological demands, particularly the metabolic requirements that underpin elite performance during competition, remains limited.</p>
<p>Over the past few decades, research has relied predominantly on established physiological and biochemical markers to analyse specific metabolites, providing a broader understanding of the body’s responses to training. These markers include blood lactate for assessing energy metabolism, creatine kinase (CK) as an indicator of muscle damage, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) for evaluating protein metabolism [<a href="#cit0017" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0017">17</a>]. However, these markers have limitations in their ability to capture subtle metabolic changes at the molecular level, which constrains the discovery of novel biomarkers and our understanding of complex adaptive processes.</p>
<p>In contrast to traditional techniques, the non-targeted, high-sensitivity advantages of LC‒MS not only enable detailed analysis of metabolites at the molecular level but also reflect the interactions among metabolic biomarkers, including metabolomic pathways and signal transduction information. For instance, Zhao et al. [<a href="#cit0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0018">18</a>] utilised LC-MS metabolomics systematically revealed significant dynamic changes in the urinary metabolic profiles of elite female football players, confirming multidimensional interactions among energy metabolism, neurotransmitter activity, and amino acid metabolic networks. In a complementary multi-omics study of over 600 serum samples, Contrepois et al. [<a href="#cit0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0019">19</a>] demonstrated that exercise systematically modulates pathways governing energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory repair in patients. Kozłowska et al. [<a href="#cit0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0020">20</a>] further investigated the effects of beetroot juice supplementation in fencing athletes using urinary metabolomics and identified significant alterations in tyrosine, tryptophan, epinephrine, and norepinephrine metabolism, providing molecular-level insights relevant to training-related responses and performance adaptation. These studies affirm the cross-disciplinary value of molecular-level exercise research and underscore the power of integrated omics technologies. Research of this kind provides valuable insights for understanding training load and physiological adaptation at the metabolic level.</p>
<p>Accordingly, this study employed a hypothesis-free, non-targeted LC‒MS-based metabolomics approach to investigate the serum metabolic profiles of Chinese national youth male water polo athletes after competition following one week of routine training. By integrating metabolic pathway analysis, enrichment and topology assessments, and energy metabolism changes, this research aims to characterise the acute molecular and metabolic response induced by one week of routine training.</p></section><section id="s0006"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="s0006-s2001"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Subjects</h3>
<p>Sixteen male youth water polo athletes from the Chinese national team were selected as research participants (mean age: 17.6 ± 0.5 years, mean height: 185.1 ± 3.9 cm, and mean BMI: 23.9 ± 2.9). The sample size (<em>n</em> = 16) is reflective of the finite, elite population available. The initial selection pool consisted of 22 athletes from the national youth team. To ensure metabolic and physiological homogeneity within the cohort, we focused specifically on field players (<em>n</em> = 18), excluding goalkeepers. This decision is supported by established research in team sports, which demonstrates that athletes in different positions (e.g. goalkeepers vs. field players) exhibit distinct metabolic and physiological profiles due to marked differences in exercise patterns, intensity, and duration during competition and training [<a href="#cit0021" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0021">21</a>]. Ultimately, 16 field players completed the full longitudinal study protocol.</p>
<p>According to the official athlete classification system, 15 participants were classified as national-level athletes and 1 as a first-class athlete. All participants had comparable training and competitive exposure, with similar training loads and competition intensity (mean time: 5.63 ± 0.48 years of systematic water polo training). During the study period, the athletes trained 5.68 ± 0.24 hours per day, with one rest day per week.</p>
<p>Blood samples from 16 athletes were successfully collected and analysed as valid data. All participants were confirmed to be in good health, with no reported metabolic diseases or significant health issues during the training camp. None of the athletes were on medication, smoked, consumed excessive alcohol, stayed up late, or used stimulants or functional beverages. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants before their participation in the study. All the participants were fully accustomed to the procedures used in this research and were told that they could withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.</p>
<p>To minimise experimental variability, all participants underwent the same training programme at the Shenzhen Sports School swimming pool during the trial period. To standardise dietary intake and minimise metabolic differences, all meals were uniformly prepared and provided by the water polo training centre. The basic information of the athletes is shown in <a href="#t0001" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>.</p>

Athletes also work on small-range ball control and position-specific training, which includes 6-on-5 and 3-on-2 drills. The total duration for these exercises is approximately 90 minutes. <p>To establish a standardised metabolic baseline, all athletes underwent a standardised one-week complete break (washout period) following their previous competitive season, during which they completely abstained from all forms of structured land-based and water-based training to mitigate accumulated fatigue. The samples were drawn into 10 mL vacutainer tubes and labelled E1 (pre-training) and E2 (post-training). After collection, 2 mL of the supernatant obtained via centrifugation of the blood sample (4 °C, 3000 rpm for 15 minutes) was placed in a 5 mL EP tube, initially stored in liquid nitrogen, and then transferred to a −80 °C freezer for later analysis.</p></section><section id="s0006-s2004"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. <div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/t0003/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="s0006-s2005-s3002"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5.2. Serum sample thawing and preparation</h4>
<p>Fasting blood samples were collected in 5 mL vacutainer tubes, after which the samples were centrifuged for 15 min (1500 × g, 4 °C). Each aliquot (50 μL) of the serum sample was stored at –80 °C until UPLC-Q-TOF/MS analysis. Serum samples were thawed on ice. For each sample, 50 µL was aliquoted, and 200 µL of extraction reagent (containing a mixed isotopic internal standard solution) was added. The mixture was placed on a shaker at 1000 rpm and vortexed for 10 minutes. After mixing, the tubes were centrifuged at 15000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 minutes. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a protein precipitation plate. An equal volume of the filtrate was transferred to a new centrifuge tube and subjected to low-temperature freeze-drying.</p>
<p>For detection, the freeze-dried samples were reconstituted with 200 µL of reconstitution solvent. Subsequently, 10 µL of the reconstituted sample was analysed via quantitative mass spectrometry.</p>
<p>Preparation of quality control (QC) samples: QC samples were prepared by mixing equal volumes of sample homogenates. The pretreatment process for the QC samples was identical to that for the experimental samples. QC samples were used to evaluate instrument performance, equilibrate the chromatography‒mass spectrometry system prior to analysis, and monitor the system stability and overall quality throughout the experiment.</p>
<p>Distribution of the QC samples: The QC samples were randomly distributed within the detection sequence to ensure uniform placement across all the analytical points.</p></section></section><section id="s0006-s2006"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Chromatography‒mass spectrometry analysis</h3>
<section id="s0006-s2006-s3001"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6.1. Chromatographic conditions</h4>
<p>Metabolite separation was performed via the HILIC approach with an Acquity™ BEH AMIDE column (Waters Corp., USA). The mobile phase consisted of water and acetonitrile, both of which contained 25 mM ammonium formate and were adjusted to pH 9.0. A linear gradient elution was applied, increasing the aqueous phase from 2% to 98% over 10 minutes, followed by a 2-minute column wash and re-equilibration. The flow rate was maintained at 0.4 mL/min, and the column temperature was set to 40 °C. Data acquisition was carried out in both positive and negative electrospray ionisation (ESI) modes.</p></section><section id="s0006-s2006-s3002"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6.2. Mass spectrometry conditions</h4>
<p>Metabolite detection was performed via a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operated in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. The instrument was equipped with a heated electrospray ionisation (HESI) source. The ionisation voltage was set to 4.0 kV in positive mode and 3.5 kV in negative mode. The sheath gas and auxiliary gas flow rates were set to 45 and 10 arbitrary units, respectively. The capillary temperature was maintained at 320 °C, while the HESI probe heater temperature was set to 355 °C. The RF level of the S-lens was set at 50%.</p>
<p>Full MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 70,000 (at m/z 200), with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1 × 10⁶ and a maximum injection time of 100 ms. The scan range was set from m/z 70-1050. MS/MS spectra were acquired at a resolution of 17,500 (at m/z 200), with an AGC target of 1 × 10⁵ and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. Fragmentation was performed via higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a normalised collision energy (NCE) of 40%, and ultrapure nitrogen was used as the collision gas.</p></section><section id="s0006-s2006-s3003"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6.3. MS data processing and compound identification</h4>
<p>Raw mass spectrometric data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.0 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which was employed for peak picking, peak alignment, compound identification, and quantitative analysis of metabolites. Metabolite identification was conducted in accordance with the confidence levels defined by the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) [<a href="#cit0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0022">22</a>]. Specifically, Glutamic acid, Succinic acid, Phenylalanine, Fumaric acid, Asparagine, and Uric acid were categorised as Level 1 metabolites(confirmed identification). Their identification was validated by matching both retention time (RT) and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) spectra against authentic commercial standards that were analysed in-house. All remaining metabolites were designated as Level 2 (putative annotation), based on the matching of experimental MS/MS spectra to public spectral libraries including HMDB v5.0 and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), with a library match score threshold set at &gt;0.7. Further spectral validation was additionally performed using the mzVault and mzCloud databases.</p>
<p>To improve the accuracy of metabolite identification, a panel of isotopically labelled internal standards (¹³C- and ¹⁵N-labelled; purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) was spiked into each sample prior to analysis. These internal standards included Alanine, Valine, Leucine, Phenylalanine, Glutamic acid, Tyrosine, Acetyl-L-carnitine, Propionyl-L-carnitine, Succinic acid, Fumaric acid, Glucose, Adenosine, and Uridine. The consistency of RT and fragmentation patterns between endogenous metabolites and their corresponding isotopically labelled internal standards was verified to ensure analytical reliability.</p>
<p>All analyses were performed on a Q Exactive HF-X hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer equipped with a heated electrospray ionisation (HESI) source. The mass accuracy tolerances were set at 20 ppm for full-scan MS (MS¹) and 0.02 Da for MS/MS (MS²), with an RT tolerance of 0.2 min. In positive ion mode, the adduct ions [M + H]⁺, [M + Na]⁺, and [M + NH₄]⁺ were considered for metabolite annotation; in negative ion mode, the adduct ions [M-H]⁻ and [M + FA-H]⁻ were taken into account.</p>
<p>The resulting quantitative data matrix was exported and imported into MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (<a href="https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/</a>) for pattern recognition, and the data were preprocessed by autoscaling and subjected to multidimensional statistical analysis, including unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA), supervised partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). Differentially metabolites between the two time points were initially screened using OPLS-DA Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) values. Metabolites with VIP &gt; 1 were further evaluated using univariate paired t-tests. To control for potential false positives arising from multiple comparisons, <em>p-</em>values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Metabolites meeting both VIP &gt; 1 and FDR &lt; 0.05 were considered significantly differential.</p>
<p>Cohen’s d_z was calculated as the effect size for paired comparisons, with effect sizes interpreted as small (0.2 ≤ |d_z| &lt; 0.5), medium (0.5 ≤ |d_z| &lt; 0.8), and large (|d_z| ≥ 0.8). Box plots were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0; GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).</p></section></section></section><section id="s0007"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. <section id="s0007-s2002-s3001"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2.1. Principal component analysis (PCA)</h4>
<p>PCA is a statistical method used for dimensionality reduction. It transforms multiple variables into fewer independent components while retaining as much original data information as possible. PCA helps assess variability within and between sample groups, identifying clustering patterns and potential outliers. As shown in <a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>, a separation trend between pre- and post-training samples was observed. PC1 and PC2 explained 19.6% and 11.1% of the total variance, respectively (30.7% cumulative). For high-dimensional untargeted metabolomic data, this proportion is within a reasonable range. The separation along PC1 suggests that training induced systematic changes in the global metabolome. As PCA is an unsupervised exploratory analysis, these results indicate preliminary evidence of metabolic adaptation rather than definitive group discrimination.</p>

The distribution and variation trends of the 33 metabolites are illustrated in the pie chart (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>) and box plots (<a href="#f0006" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a>).</p>

<p>Water polo, an intermittent high-intensity sport that integrates swimming, passing, and tactical play, relies on aerobic and anaerobic energy systems because of its short, intense bursts (lasting &lt; 15 s) and rapid recovery phases [<a href="#cit0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0015">15</a>,<a href="#cit0023" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0023">23</a>]. In the field of sports science, metabolic analysis techniques such as LC‒MS provide powerful tools for obtaining a wide-ranging overview of exercise-regulated metabolites [<a href="#cit0024" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0024">24</a>]. Therefore, this study employed nontargeted LC‒MS/MS metabolomics to explore blood metabolic profiles in elite youth male water polo athletes during a selected week of their routine training, providing potential insights into metabolic adaptations, physical fitness evaluation, and nutritional implications.</p>
<section id="s0008-s2001"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.1. Metabolomic profiling and key pathways</h3>
<p>A total of 363 metabolites were detected, 33 of which were significantly differentially expressed (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05), underscoring the metabolic responsiveness to routine training. These metabolites reveal a complex interplay between energy metabolism, amino acid turnover, lipid mobilisation, and systemic adaptation processes. Amino acids and fatty acids accounted for the predominant changes following intensive training.</p>
<p>Furthermore, a comprehensive analysis of metabolic pathways and KEGG enrichment revealed three critical pathways potentially responsible for the serum metabolic alterations observed after training. Notably, lysine degradation was the most significantly enriched pathway based on KEGG and MetPA analyses, indicating that metabolites within this pathway were particularly responsive to the training intervention. Other pathways, such as vitamin B6 metabolism and butanoate metabolism, also showed notable enrichment, indicating potential involvement in energy regulation, antioxidant defence, and amino acid turnover.</p></section><section id="s0008-s2002"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.2. Amino acids and their derivatives</h3>
<p>Among amino acid group metabolites, the levels of L-phenylalanyl-L-leucine (Phe-Leu) and 4-amino-1-butanol (4AB) (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01) were significantly elevated after training. In contrast, compared with those before training, the levels of Cr, N8-acetylspermidine, 3-methylhistidine (3-MH), <em>N</em>-a-acetyl-L-arginine, <em>N</em>-methyl-L-glutamic acid, N6, N6, N6-trimethyl-L-lysine (TML), and symmetric dimethylarginine (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01) were significantly lower.</p>
<p>Phe-Leu, a dipeptide derived from the essential amino acid Leu, serves as both a metabolic fuel source and a signalling molecule during exercise. Leu is one of three branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) that undergo increased oxidation during high-intensity exercise, such as associated with a week high intensity routine training, to meet elevated energy demands. Through mitochondrial catabolism, BCAAs are converted into acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA, which enter the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to drive ATP production, sustaining the energy supply during prolonged or intense physical activity [<a href="#cit0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0025">25</a>]. Previous studies have reported a marked elevation in Leu levels following competitive soccer matches [<a href="#cit0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0018">18</a>]. In this study, the significant increase in Phe-Leu (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01) after training suggested increased BCAA utilisation, which was likely hydrolysed to leucine for energy metabolism.</p>
<p>4AB is a naturally occurring amino alcohol that has gained attention as a raw material in gene and protein delivery systems because of its biocompatibility [<a href="#cit0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0026">26</a>]. Owing to its structural ties to <em>γ</em>-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and polyamines and its demonstrated biocompatibility [<a href="#cit0027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0027">27</a>], 4AB represents a candidate for investigating exercise-induced metabolic pathways and TCA cycle anaplerosis. Its role in human exercise physiology remains unclear, although its significant increase (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01) post-training in this study may indicate its involvement in metabolic pathways. While speculative, 4AB could contribute to the TCA cycle anaplerosis or related processes, given its potential ties to polyamine metabolism. However, direct evidence of the endogenous production and specific function of 4AB in exercise is limited, necessitating further investigation to elucidate its metabolic significance.</p>
<p>Both TML (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/C03793" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">C03793</a>) and L-2-aminoadipate (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/C00956" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">C00956</a>), which are annotated in the KEGG database as components of the lysine degradation pathway (KEGG map), were significantly decreased following training (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01). TML is a methylated derivative generated during protein breakdown, including the breakdown of methylated proteins such as histones [<a href="#cit0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0028">28</a>], thereby linking proteolysis to lysine degradation. Importantly, TML serves as the rate-limiting precursor for endogenous carnitine biosynthesis [<a href="#cit0029" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0029">29</a>], a molecule essential for mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (FAO). In metabolically active tissues such as skeletal muscle, heart, and liver, energy production relies on mitochondrial <em>β</em>-oxidation, which is facilitated by the carnitine shuttle transporting long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) into the mitochondrial matrix [<a href="#cit0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0030">30</a>]. The observed reduction in circulating TML may therefore reflect its increased utilisation to meet elevated demands during high-intensity training, which could contribute to sustaining or optimising fatty acid oxidation capacity. From a sports science perspective, this metabolic shift may contribute to improved metabolic efficiency, greater reliance on lipid for energy, and potential glycogen sparing during repeated high-intensity efforts. This is consistent with findings from previous metabolomic studies demonstrating robust and unidirectional activation of fat metabolism and concurrent reductions in ketogenic amino acids, including lysine, following acute endurance exercise [<a href="#cit0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0031">31</a>]. Such adaptations are critical for sustaining performance and supporting recovery in intermittent, high-intensity team sports such as water polo.</p>
<p>The acylcarnitines comprise a wide range of acyl groups linked via an ester bond to the hydroxyl group of L-carnitine. Among them, 3-methylglutaryl (3MG), also referred to as “C5-3 M-DC”, and glutarylcarnitine (C5-DC) are notable members. Acylcarnitines serve as sensitive biomarkers for mitochondrial dysfunction and function as transporters that shuttle fatty acids into mitochondria, where <em>β</em>-oxidation and ATP synthesis occur [<a href="#cit0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0032">32</a>]. Increased levels of acylcarnitines have also been linked to an individual’s fitness level. For instance, other metabolomic studies have observed that lower-performing male marathon athletes exhibit a significant increase in a wide range of acylcarnitines [<a href="#cit0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0033">33</a>]. In the current study, we observed a decrease in both metabolite levels following one-week training. We hypothesize that this may reflect a training-adapted efficiency in lipid utilisation among elite water polo athletes, a premise that warrants direct validation in future studies.</p>
<p>Creatine (Cr) is synthesised from the amino acids glycine, arginine, and methionine in the kidneys, liver and pancreas [<a href="#cit0034" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0034">34</a>], and plays a central role in cellular energy metabolism through the phosphocreatine (PCr) system. During high-intensity exercise, ATP hydrolysis is initially buffered by PCr via the CK reaction [<a href="#cit0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0035">35</a>,<a href="#cit0036" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0036">36</a>]. Approximately 95% of the total Cr pool is stored in skeletal muscle, predominantly in the form of PCr [<a href="#cit0037" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0037">37</a>]. During short-duration, high-intensity muscle contraction, ATP turnover is markedly increased, leading to a high demand for PCr to support energy supply. In contrast to some previous studies reporting a post-exercise increase in serum Cr, our findings showed a decrease in serum Cr levels following one week of training. One possible explanation is that the reduction in circulating Cr reflects increased tissue utilisation or redistribution to support phosphocreatine resynthesis during repeated high-intensity exercise and early recovery. However, this interpretation remains hypothetical and should be considered and tested in the nearby future.</p>
<p>Previous studies have demonstrated that Cr supplementation can enhance high-intensity exercise capacity, reduce fatigue, improve muscle tone and flexibility, and promote protein synthesis during exercise [<a href="#cit0037" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0037">37–39</a>]. It is particularly effective for activities requiring repeated bursts of high-intensity effort [<a href="#cit0039" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0039">39</a>]. While these findings provide relevant physiological context, the present results do not allow direct conclusions regarding supplementation efficacy. Nevertheless, given the anaerobic and intermittent demands of water polo, we suggest that Cr supplementation may warrant further investigation as a potential strategy to support performance and recovery in water polo athletes.</p></section><section id="s0008-s2003"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.3. Fatty acids</h3>
<p>Butyric acid (BA, <em>n</em>-butyrate) is a major short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) that is commonly reported to be produced through microbial fermentation in the colon. It has been associated with gut barrier integrity, nutrient absorption, and host energy metabolism [<a href="#cit0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0040">40</a>,<a href="#cit0041" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0041">41</a>]. (9E, 12E)-Octadeca-9, 12-dienoic acid (linolenic acid, LA) is an essential fatty acid. Food science research has identified gamma-linolenic acid (GLA) and BA as key anti-inflammatory bioactive components in fermented soybean [<a href="#cit0042" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0042">42</a>].</p>
<p>Existing literature suggests that the gut microbiota-related metabolites are associated with exercise-induced oxidative stress regulation and gastrointestinal hydration homoeostasis [<a href="#cit0043" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0043">43</a>]. In pathological conditions such as Crohn’s disease, reduced gut microbiota diversity is associated with inflammation and metabolic dysfunction, including impaired oxidative stress responses, downregulation of butanoate and propanoate metabolism genes, decreased SCFA levels (e.g. butyrate), and disruptions in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism [<a href="#cit0044" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0044">44</a>]. These findings provide indirect context for interpreting circulating metabolites but do not imply causality in the present study.</p>
<p>Travis’s study [<a href="#cit0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0045">45</a>] reported elevated blood levels of oleic and linoleic acids following prolonged aerobic exercise (180 km), suggesting sustained mitochondrial fatty acid mobilisation, as also evidenced by metabolomics analysis in ultramarathon athletes [<a href="#cit0046" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0046">46</a>]. In contrast, our study revealed a decrease in BA, LA, GLA and the unsaturated fatty acid 9, 10-epoxyoctadecanoic acid after training in water polo athletes. We cautiously speculate this reduction may reflect limited acyl-carnitine availability, which could impair the acyl-CoA conversion of acyl-carnitine required for efficient mitochondrial <em>β</em>-oxidation. Additionally, we observed an elevation in (E)-3-(2, 3-dihydroxyphenyl)-2-propenoic acid after training. This compound is a hydroxycinnamic acid commonly found in food and is known for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunoregulatory properties [<a href="#cit0047" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0047">47</a>,<a href="#cit0048" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0048">48</a>]. Reports of this metabolite in human exercise studies are scarce (e.g. Foodb.ca entry FDB008764), and its physiological relevance remains largely unexplored. Although exercise has been reported to influence phenolic acid metabolism [<a href="#cit0049" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0049">49</a>,<a href="#cit0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0050">50</a>], gut microbiota composition was not assessed in this study. Therefore, the observed increase should be interpreted as a systemic metabolic response to training rather than evidence of microbial modulation. We propose that this compound may serve as a candidate biomarker of training-related metabolic adaptation, warranting further investigation in studies integrating dietary control and microbiome profiling.</p>
<p>L-2-Aminoadipate (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/C00956" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">C00956</a>), also known as 2-aminoadipic acid, is an intermediate in human lysine catabolism and significantly decreased after prolonged water polo competition. During high-intensity exercise, energy production is predominantly sustained by carbohydrate oxidation [<a href="#cit0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0045">45</a>]. However, with increasing exercise duration and training load, a greater reliance on lipid oxidation has been reported in endurance and intermittent sports. To maintain TCA cycle flux under elevated energy demand, additional contributions from lysine and leucine catabolism may have occurred. One possible interpretation of this pattern may reflect exercise-induced shifts toward oxidative phosphorylation of amino acid–derived metabolites, particularly under glycogen-depleted conditions. However, as lysine is an essential amino acid, its metabolic flux is also modulated by dietary intake, a variable not controlled in this study. This finding aligns with previous metabolomic studies demonstrating that acute exercise robustly activates fat metabolism while reducing ketogenic amino acids like lysine [<a href="#cit0051" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0051">51</a>]. Future studies integrating detailed dietary assessment will be key to disentangling the specific contributions of training and nutrition.</p></section><section id="s0008-s2004"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.4. Vitamin B6 metabolism</h3>
<p>The significant increase in 4-pyridoxic acid (4-PA, <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/C00847" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">C00847</a>) (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01) is consistent with an acceleration of vitamin B6 catabolism following the training week, and could serve as an exploratory biomarker of increased amino acid turnover to support energy metabolism. As a key component of the vitamin B6 metabolism pathway (KEGG map), 4-PA reflects the metabolism of dietary protein, the primary source of vitamin B6 in humans. Vitamin B6, particularly its active form pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (PLP), serves as a coenzyme essential for protein and amino acid metabolism and is directly involved in the glycogenolytic pathway, providing energy substrates during exercise [<a href="#cit0052" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0052">52</a>]. During physical activity, plasma PLP levels increase, and PLP is subsequently metabolised to 4-PA [<a href="#cit0053" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0053">53</a>]. This pattern resembles findings in other studies, where elevated levels of vitamin B6 catabolites, including 4-PA, have been observed in plasma following exercise [<a href="#cit0054" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0054">54</a>].</p>
<p>As the principal end-product of vitamin B6 catabolism, the observed increase in 4-PA post-training suggests that current training stimulated amino acid metabolism. However, as vitamin B6 status is co-determined by dietary intake, this change may represent an integrated response to both the training load and nutritional habits. Therefore, future studies integrating controlled dietary protocols with metabolomic profiling are essential to isolate the specific contribution of training, thereby clarifying whether 4-PA dynamics can serve as a robust, training-specific biomarker.</p></section><section id="s0008-s2005"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.5. Metabolic stability as a potential adaptive trait</h3>
<p>It is noteworthy that the vast majority of detected metabolites (330/363) showed no significant alterations after an intensive training week, with only 33 metabolites displaying measurable changes. This overall stability also appeared to be reflected at the pathway level, where five out of the eight enriched metabolic pathways exhibited no statistically significant alterations (<em>p</em> &gt; 0.05).</p>
<p>This proportion appears to be consistent with the exploratory nature of untargeted metabolomics and may align well with previous exercise metabolomics studies. In a foundational study, Pohjanen et al. [<a href="#cit0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0055">55</a>] applied non-targeted GC-MS metabolomics to exercise science, identifying 420 metabolites of which 34 were significantly altered—a pattern broadly analogous to our findings. As non-significant statistical differences tend to be underemphasised in metabolomics research, one study reported that only a single differential metabolite between groups could indicate comparable training backgrounds [<a href="#cit0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0033">33</a>]. In our study, approximately 91% of metabolites remained unchanged, which may similarly point to relatively homogeneous training levels among elite water polo athletes and could further reflect their strong metabolic buffering capacity against intensive training stress.</p>
<p>However, the translational potential of this work may lie in delineating the mechanisms by which stable metabolite profiles directly or indirectly modulate exercise-induced physiological adaptations, and these mechanistic links would warrant further validation in future research.</p></section></section><section id="s0009"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">5. Limitation and future directions</h2>
<p>This exploratory metabolomic analysis provides novel insights into training-associated metabolic responses in elite youth water polo athletes; however, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. (1) The study sample size was not determined by formal sample-size calculation, but constrained to 16 male elite water polo athletes. This limitation arose from practical constraints and the rarity of accessing this distinct high-performance population, with the sample size consistent with that reported in comparable publications. (2) The study was intentionally designed as a real-world training monitoring investigation to capture metabolic responses under routine training conditions rather than under tightly controlled experimental manipulation. Consequently, key physiological and training-load indicators—such as heart rate, external workload metrics, and precise intensity quantification to objectively define the training were not systematically measured. While this approach allowed observation of authentic training adaptations, the absence of these measures limits a more specific mechanistic interpretation of metabolite changes. (3) Additionally, dietary intake, nutrient timing, and gastrointestinal or gut microbiota-related variables were not controlled or assessed. Given the well-established role of nutritional practices in modulating training adaptation and performance in aquatic sports [<a href="#cit0056" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0056">56</a>], this limits the mechanistic interpretation of metabolites, especially those associated with essential amino acid metabolism or potentially modulated by dietary intake and gut-related processes.</p>
<p>Collectively, these limitations underscore the exploratory nature of the present study and highlight the need for future investigations. In this context, future studies should recruit larger cohorts across different genders and adopt more targeted approaches to validate the current findings. Additionally, subsequent research should integrate controlled, training-specific load metrics, physiological biomarkers, and nutritional monitoring to capture more specific metabolic responses, thereby facilitating a more precise characterisation of training-induced adaptations in water polo athletes.</p></section><section id="s0010"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">6. Conclusions</h2>
<p>Taken together, the findings of this exploratory study indicate that one week of high-intensity training induces measurable alterations in the serum metabolomic profile of water polo athletes. Changes in metabolite abundance point to shifts in amino acid and fatty acid metabolic pathways, which likely reflect adaptive adjustments to meet the elevated energetic demands of intensive training. Non-targeted LC–MS/MS metabolomics offers a systems-level view of training-related metabolic responses in elite aquatic athletes. Although this study design precludes causal inferences and practical recommendations, these results provide a robust foundation for future controlled investigations—integrating training load, physiological biomarkers, and nutritional intake—to better guide performance optimisation and recovery strategies for water polo athletes.</p></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgements</h2>
