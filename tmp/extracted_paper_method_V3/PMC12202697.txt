</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="Par1">Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in Taiwan, and has a poor prognosis despite treatment advances. Emerging evidence suggests environmental contaminants, including organophosphate flame retardants (OPFRs), as potential cancer contributors. This study aims to investigate the association between exposure to OPFRs and ESCC. A case–control study with 133 ESCC patients and 133 controls was conducted. Urinary concentrations of 10 OPFRs, including 5 triesters (TDCPP, TCEP, TBEP, TNBP, TPHP) and their corresponding diesters (BDCPP, BCEP, DBEP, DNBP, DPHP), were measured using UPLC-MS/MS, and the associations between OPFR levels and ESCC risk was analyzed. Survival analyses were also performed to assess prognostic impact. In vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted to explore cytotoxic effects and pathological changes induced by OPFRs, with pathways identified via RNA sequencing and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Higher detection rates of BDCPP, BCEP, and TDCPP were observed in ESCC patients, with TDCPP showing the strongest association with ESCC (odds ratio [OR] = 2.58, <em>p</em> = 0.005) and the poorest survival outcome (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.91, <em>p</em> = 0.021). In cell culture experiments, TDCPP induced cytotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inhibited colony formation. In a murine animal model, combined TDCPP and 4-NQO exposure led to a significantly higher incidence of ESCC compared to 4-NQO alone. RNA sequencing and GSEA analysis identified several differentially expressed genes and pathways related to immune response and cellular stress. This study highlights TDCPP as a potential environmental risk factor for ESCC, emphasizing the importance of considering environmental exposures in patients with ESCC, and the need for further research into the carcinogenic impact of OPFRs.</p>

<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10653-025-02599-2.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Organophosphate flame retardants (OPFRs), Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), Bioinformatics analysis</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par2">Esophageal cancer was the sixth leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide in 2018 (Bray et al., <a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">2018</a>). In Taiwan, it ranks as the fifth leading cause of cancer mortality among men (<a href="https://twcr.tw/?page_id=1855&amp;lang=en" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://twcr.tw/?page_id=1855&amp;lang=en</a>). Esophageal adenocarcinomas are more prevalent in the United States, while 90% of esophageal cancer cases in Taiwan are squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) (Chung et al., <a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">2010</a>).</p>
<p id="Par3">ESCC develops through a sequence of histopathological changes, including esophagitis, atrophy, mild to severe dysplasia, carcinoma in situ, and ultimately invasive cancer (Mandard et al., <a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">2000</a>). Major risk factors for ESCC include long-term tobacco and alcohol use, betel quid chewing, hot tea consumption, chronic mucosal irritation, esophageal achalasia, esophageal web, and Plummer-Vinson syndrome (Chung et al., <a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">2010</a>; Dix et al., <a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">2020</a>). Additionally, a history of upper aerodigestive tract cancers, such as head and neck cancer, predisposes individuals to ESCC, with a 10–20% chance of developing a secondary ESCC (Chuang et al., <a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">2008</a>; Chung et al., <a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">2010</a>). The incidence of esophageal cancer in Taiwan increased from 7.78 to 14.12 per 100,000 population from 1996 to 2018, suggesting that environmental factors beyond tobacco and alcohol may contribute to its tumorigenesis. Despite advancements in surgical techniques, radiation, and systemic therapies such as chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors, the prognosis for ESCC remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 20–30% (Dix et al., <a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">2020</a>). Therefore, identifying environmental factors involved in ESCC development is crucial.</p>
<p id="Par4">Recent studies have highlighted the significant role of environmental contaminants, particularly endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), in cancer development (Encarnação et al., <a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">2019</a>; Kahn et al., <a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">2020</a>). EDCs have become ubiquitous in modern life, and are linked to various cancers, including thyroid, skin, liver, and breast cancers (de Wit, <a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">2002</a>; Dishaw et al., <a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">2014</a>; Eales et al., <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">2022</a>; Encarnação et al., <a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">2019</a>; Greaves &amp; Letcher, <a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">2017</a>; Kahn et al., <a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">2020</a>; Man et al., <a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">2011</a>). The use of organophosphate flame retardants (OPFRs), a group of EDCs, has increased in recent years as they are replacing polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs). OPFRs are characterized by their organophosphate ester structure, which contributes to their flame-retardant properties and versatility in industrial applications. OPFRs are widely used in industrial products including polyurethane foams, furniture upholstery, electronic circuit boards, plastics, coatings, and adhesives, leading to significant environmental contamination and human exposure through ingestion, inhalation, and dermal contact (Cequier et al., <a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">2015</a>; Y. Li et al., <a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">2020</a>; L.-Y. Liu et al., <a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">2016</a>; Ma et al., <a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">2017</a>; Vykoukalová et al., <a href="#CR50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR50">2017</a>). Globally, OPFR usage is prevalent in industrialized regions such as North America, Europe, and East Asia, where stringent fire safety regulations necessitate the incorporation of flame retardants in household and industrial products (Li et al., <a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">2019</a>). In Taiwan, the increasing demand for OPFR-containing products is largely driven by the growth of the electronics and manufacturing industries. Studies conducted in Taiwan have reported significant levels of OPFR metabolites in human urine samples, indicating widespread exposure among the general population (Cheng et al., <a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">2023</a>). This rising prevalence of OPFR exposure is attributed to factors such as rapid urbanization, increased use of synthetic materials in construction, and the extensive production and consumption of flame-retardant-treated goods (Chen et al., <a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">2023</a>).</p>
<p id="Par5">The growing concern surrounding OPFR exposure stems from emerging evidence linking these compounds to potential adverse health effects. Research indicates that OPFRs can induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and oxidative stress, contributing to carcinogenesis (An et al., <a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2016</a>; Guo et al., <a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">2020</a>; L. Li et al., <a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">2017</a>; L. L. Xu et al., <a href="#CR54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR54">2016</a>; X. Yu et al., <a href="#CR58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR58">2019</a>). Studies have shown that OPFRs and their metabolites can be found in various human biological matrices, and are potentially carcinogenic ("Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) Toxicological Profiles," <a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">2012</a>; van der Veen &amp; de Boer, <a href="#CR49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR49">2012</a>). Another study indicated that OPFRs were associated with epidermal growth factor receptor expression in lung cancer patients (Chen et al., <a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">2024</a>). These toxicological and biochemical insights raise legitimate concern that chronic OPFR exposure in humans could contribute to ESCC.</p>
<p id="Par6">The association between OPFRs and ESCC remains limited and inconclusive. Given their bioaccumulation and role in inducing ROS and oxidative stress, it is plausible that OPFRs contribute to chronic inflammation and ESCC development. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the potential link between OPFR exposure and the pathogenesis of ESCC.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study population</h3>
<p id="Par7">Patients with ESCC treated at Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital from September 2020 to December 2023 were included in this study. Clinical tumor staging was determined according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging system (Ma et al., <a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">2017</a>). The inclusion criteria for patients in this study were as follows: (1) a pathologically confirmed diagnosis of ESCC; (2) adults aged 20 years or older; (3) agreement to urine collection for OPFR testing; and (4) no treatment received prior to urine collection for OPFR testing. We excluded patients with adenocarcinoma, neuroendocrine tumors, small cell carcinoma, synchronous cancer such as head and neck cancer. Additionally, patients with a history of second primary cancers, either before or after ESCC diagnosis, and those with pregnancy or infectious diseases were excluded. Patients lacking complete data of regular laboratory blood/urine tests, imaging examinations, and survival follow-up were also excluded. Ultimately, 133 patients with ESCC who met the inclusion criteria were included. A control group consisting of 133 sex- and age-matched subjects (± 7 years) without any known history of malignancies was also included. For control group, the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) adults aged 20 years or older, and (2) agreement to provide a urine sample for OPFR testing. The exclusion criteria were: (1) any known history of malignancies; (2) pregnancy or infectious diseases; and (3) hospitalization or surgery within three months prior to enrollment. All study participants were followed from the date of ESCC diagnosis until the date of death or the last known contact, with follow-up concluding on January 31, 2024. The dates of death or last contact were obtained from the patients; medical records.</p></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Measurement of urinary concentrations of OPFRs by ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS)</h3>
<p id="Par8">Urinary concentrations of 10 OPFR compounds were measured to evaluate exposure to OPFRs, including 5 triesters (TDCPP, TCEP, TBEP, TNBP, TPHP) and their corresponding diesters (BDCPP, BCEP, DBEP, DNBP, DPHP). First-void morning urine samples were collected within 1 week of inclusion in the study using polypropylene cups, and samples were stored in 1.5 mL amber microcentrifuge tubes at -80 °C.</p>
<p id="Par9">OPFR measurements were conducted using a Waters Acquity UPLC system with a Waters Xevo TQ-XS mass spectrometer in negative or positive ESI mode (Tsai et al., <a href="#CR48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR48">2022</a>). The separation used a Waters Acquity UPLC BEH Phenyl column with a Waters XBridge BEH C18 Direct Connect HP-isolated column. The mobile phases were 0.5% formic acid in water (A) and 0.5% formic acid in methanol (B). The gradient increased from 5 to 50% solvent B over 0.75 min, then to 100% over 3 min, was held for 4.5 min, and decreased back to 5% over 2 min.</p>
<p id="Par10">Target analytes were identified by retention time and precursor-ion-product ion transitions, compared with standards. OPFR concentrations were quantified using 12-point calibration curves (0.02 ppb to 50 ppb). The limit of quantification (LOQ) was 0.02 ng/mL for all OPFRs except for BCEP, where the LOQ was 0.05 ng/mL BCEP. Recoveries were determined using individual isotope-labeled internal standards.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Ethics statement</h3>
<p id="Par11">The current study was approved by the Chang Gung Medical Foundation Institutional Review Board (IRB number: 202001029B0). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Institutional Research Committee and World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient, and all methods were performed in accordance with approved protocol.</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Cell culture</h3>
<p id="Par12">KYSE-270 and TE11 cells were obtained from the European Collection of Cell Cultures (ECACC), and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 2 mmol/L L-glutamine (Shimada et al., <a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">1992</a>; Takashima et al., <a href="#CR44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR44">2008</a>). Het-1A, a human esophageal squamous epithelial cell line immortalized by SV40 transfection, was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in BEBM medium (Lonza, Walkersville, MD, USA) (Tamagawa et al., <a href="#CR45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR45">2012</a>).</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Cell viability assay and colony formation assay</h3>
<p id="Par13">The viability of sub-confluent cells was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazole-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) reduction assay. Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10<sup>3</sup> cells per well in 96-well plates. The following day, cells were treated with TDCPP (0, 10, and 50 μM) for 72 h. It should be noted that samples with a TDCPP concentration of 0 were designated as the control group. Subsequently, MTT solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After incubation, 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 10 min. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. The optical density (OD) values were normalized to the control group.</p>
<p id="Par14">For the colony formation assay, cells were seeded in 60-mm dishes at a density of 5 × 10<sup>3</sup> cells per dish. The next day, cells were treated with or without TDCPP for 72 h. After 21 days, cell colonies were stained with 0.01% crystal violet and counted.</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunoblot analysis</h3>
<p id="Par15">For cell protein extraction, samples were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS). Protein concentrations were measured using the Bio-Rad Protein Assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Standard immunoblotting procedures were followed. Primary antibodies against FASN, EGFR, SREBP1, SCD1, COP1, ACC, STAT1, and β-actin (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) were used due to their relevance to cancer development (M.-Y. Cui et al., <a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">2022</a>; L.-Y. Li et al., <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">2021</a>; Z. Wang et al., <a href="#CR53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR53">2024</a>). Detection was performed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Bio/Can Scientific, Mississauga, ON, Canada) and Western Lighting Reagent, visualized on X-ray films.</p></section><section id="Sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4-Nitroquinoline 1-oxide (4-NQO)-induced ESCC murine model</h3>
<p id="Par16">4-NQO (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in DMSO (50 mg/mL) and stored at − 20 °C. For administration, the stock solution was diluted with propylene glycol and added to autoclaved drinking water at 100 μg/mL. Six-week-old mice were randomized into 6 groups. One group received 4-NQO in drinking water for 8 weeks, followed by plain water for 20 weeks (n = 20). Another group received 4-NQO for 8 weeks and TDCPP (150 mg/kg) by oral gavage twice a week for 8 weeks, then TDCPP (350 μg/mL) in drinking water for 20 weeks (n = 20). Mice that died before the experiment;s end were excluded from the analysis.</p>
<p id="Par17">At the end of the experiment, mice were autopsied, and the esophagus and stomach were opened and examined for macroscopic lesions. Tissues were fixed in 10% buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Histological analysis, performed by a pathology expert, identified hyperplasia, dysplasia, papilloma, and invasive squamous cell carcinoma based on established criteria (Miyamoto et al., <a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">2008</a>; Tang et al., <a href="#CR46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR46">2004</a>).</p></section><section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by RNA-sequencing in esophageal epithelium from mice treated with or without TDCPP</h3>
<p id="Par18">Total RNA from mouse esophagus was extracted using Trizol® Reagent (Invitrogen, USA), quantified with a ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Nanodrop Technology, USA), and assessed for quality using a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technology, USA) with RNA 6000 LabChip kit (Agilent Technology, USA). Library preparation followed Illumina;s protocol using the SureSelect XT HS2 mRNA Library Preparation kit (Agilent, USA) with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, USA) for size selection. Sequencing was performed using Illumina;s SBS technology (150PE). Data were processed with Trimmomatic for quality trimming and HISAT2 for alignment. Expression levels were normalized to TPM, and differential expression was analyzed using StringTie and DESeq2. Hypergeometric p-values were used for statistical significance.</p></section></section><section id="Sec11"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h2>
<p id="Par19">In this study, an a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) to estimate the required sample size for comparing BCEP detection rates between two groups using Fisher;s exact test. Based on preliminary data (Table <a href="#Tab2" class="usa-link">2</a>) from a previous study (Cheng et al., <a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">2023</a>), and assuming a two-tailed test with an alpha level of 0.05, a power (1–β) of 0.80, and a 1:1 allocation ratio, the estimated total sample size required was 260 participants (i.e., 130 per group).</p>

</div></div></section><p id="Par20">To address the right-skewed distribution of OPFR data, urinary concentrations of OPFRs were reported using the geometric mean (GM) with its 95% confidence interval (CI), as well as the median and interquartile range (IQR). Detection rates of urinary OPFR concentrations exceeding the LOQ were compared between ESCC patients and control participants using the chi-square test. For undetectable values, a substitution of 0.5 times LOQ was employed in the calculation of GMs.</p>
<p id="Par21">To assess distributional differences of OPFRs between the ESCC and control groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. GM differences of OPFRs between groups were evaluated using the independent-sample t-test. Given that control participants were matched to ESCC patients by age and sex, multivariable conditional logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between OPFR concentrations and ESCC, adjusting for additional covariates including alcohol consumption, smoking, and betel nut chewing. Upon identifying OPFRs that significantly distinguished ESCC from controls, survival analysis was employed to explore the relations between specific OPFRs and mortality in ESCC patients. In the multivariable survival analyses, only those variables (e.g., tumor stage) that were significant in the univariate analyses were included as covariates.</p>
<p id="Par22">For cell line experiments, the t-test was used for the statistical analysis. Each experiment was carried out independently at least twice, with 3 repeats each. For 4-NQO-induced ESCC mice experiments, statistical analyses of the incidence of esophageal tumors were performed using the Mann Whitney test.</p>
<p id="Par23">The statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS software, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). All tests were 2-sided, and a <em>p</em>-value of &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant.</p></section><section id="Sec12"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="Sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Comparison of urinary OPFR concentrations between ESCC patients and controls</h3>
<p id="Par24">In this study, although we matched control patients to ESCC patients by sex and age, we also collected additional personal variables, including alcohol consumption, smoking status, betel nut chewing, and family history of cancer. The distribution of these variables between ESCC patients and controls is presented in Table <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S1</a>.</p>
<p id="Par25">A comparison of urinary OPFR concentrations between ESCC patients and control participants (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>) revealed that the detection rates of BDCPP (36.1% vs. 18.0%) and TDCPP (27.1% vs. 12.8%) were significantly higher in ESCC patients compared to control participants. Additionally, the GM concentration of BCEP was significantly higher in the ESCC group (GM = 0.21) than in the control group (GM = 0.12). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the detection rates and concentrations of DBEP, TBEP, and TNBP between the 2 groups. Furthermore, DNBP, TCEP, and TPHP exhibited low detection rates in both groups, with no statistically significant differences. Detailed information on the distribution of urinary OPFR concentrations and creatinine-adjusted OPFR concentrations is presented in Table <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S2</a>. The distributions of urinary OPFR concentrations (excluding missing values) and creatinine-adjusted OPFR concentrations (excluding missing values) in the ESCC and control groups are shown in Table <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S3</a>.</p>

</div></div></section></section><section id="Sec14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Associations between OPFRs and ESCC</h3>
<p id="Par26">Table <a href="#Tab2" class="usa-link">2</a> illustrates the associations between urinary OPFR concentrations and the occurrence of ESCC. In conditional logistic regression models, higher urinary concentrations of BDCPP and TDCPP were associated with an increased risk of developing ESCC. Specifically, the odds ratio (OR) for TDCPP was 2.58 (95% CI: 1.33–5.03, <em>p</em> = 0.005) and for BDCPP was 2.50 (95% CI: 1.40–4.46, <em>p</em> = 0.002).</p>
<p id="Par27">Survival analysis, including the log-rank test (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>) and Cox models (Table <a href="#Tab3" class="usa-link">3</a>), revealed a significant association between TDCPP concentration and survival of ESCC patients after adjusting for other confounders significantly associated with ESCC survival in univariate Cox models (e.g., tumor stage). A TDCPP cutoff of 0.02 (i.e., LOQ) was associated with an increased hazard of mortality in ESCC patients (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.10–3.30, <em>p</em> = 0.021). Detailed results of the log-rank tests and univariate Cox models are shown in Table <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S4</a> and Table <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S5</a>, respectively. Additionally, we also evaluated the dose–response relationship between urinary OPFR concentrations and survival of ESCC by converting the urinary OPFR concentration data into standardized continuous variables (i.e., Z-scores). The results are presented in Table <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S6</a>.</p>

TDCPP also induced ROS production in Het-1A, KYSE270, and TE11 cells (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>B). Additionally, the experiments demonstrated that TDCPP significantly inhibited colony formation in KYSE270 and TE11 cells (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>C). Further analysis revealed that TDCPP led to the downregulation of FASN expression (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>D).</p>

<p id="Par33">Using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and annotation based on Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases, the top 10 significantly upregulated and downregulated pathways in the esophagus of mice treated with TDCPP compared to untreated controls were identified (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>). According to molecular function (MF), TDCPP significantly upregulated pathways such as cytokine binding, G protein-coupled peptide receptor activity, and neurotransmitter receptor activity, while significantly downregulated pathways like ATPase activity, lipase activity, and motor activity. For cellular component (CC), TDCPP significantly upregulated pathways like the external side of the plasma membrane and intrinsic component of the synaptic membrane, and significantly downregulated pathways such as the condensed chromosome and microtubule-associated complex. In terms of biological process (BP), TDCPP significantly upregulated immune response-related pathways including the adaptive immune response, leukocyte-mediated cytotoxicity, and regulation of lymphocyte-mediated immunity, while significantly downregulating pathways related to cellular structure and metabolic processes such as cell killing, muscle hypertrophy, and ciliogenesis. Regarding KEGG pathways, TDCPP significantly upregulated pathways involved in cellular immunity and signal transduction, such as NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity and T cell receptor signaling pathway, and significantly downregulated pathways like vascular smooth muscle contraction and pancreatic secretion.</p>

NES represents normalized enrichment score</p></figcaption></figure></section></section><section id="Sec19"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p id="Par34">This study investigated the potential link between OPFRs and ESCC, a common form of esophageal cancer in Taiwan. The findings revealed that higher concentrations of BDCPP, BCEP, and TDCPP in urine are associated with an increased risk of developing ESCC. Notably, elevated levels of TDCPP are linked to poorer survival outcomes in ESCC patients, suggesting that OPFR exposure may influence both the development and progression of the disease. This study further corroborates the cytotoxic response and pathological changes induced by OPFRs in the development of ESCC through both in vitro cell experiments and an in vivo animal model. Specifically, experimental studies demonstrated that TDCPP induces cytotoxicity, oxidative stress, and promotes apoptosis in ESCC cell lines. In a murine model, the combination of TDCPP and 4-NQO exposure led to a significantly higher incidence of ESCC compared to 4-NQO alone. Additionally, differential gene expression was observed in the esophagus of TDCPP-treated mice, and bioinformatics analysis identified key functional pathways related to upregulated and downregulated genes potentially identifying biological functions and pathways through which OPFRs may influence the development of ESCC.</p>
<p id="Par35">Many studies have found associations between exposure to various environmental chemicals and pollutants and diseases and malignancies, and the amounts of these chemicals in the environment and exposure to them is continually increasing (Fuller et al., <a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">2022</a>; Lewandowska et al., <a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">2019</a>; Sun et al., <a href="#CR43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR43">2022</a>). Previous studies have examined associations between OPFRs and cancers such as breast cancer, renal cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma (Saquib et al., <a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">2022</a>; T. Xu et al., <a href="#CR55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR55">2024</a>; Zhou et al., <a href="#CR60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR60">2022</a>). Zhou et al. (Zhou et al., <a href="#CR60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR60">2022</a>) showed that TDCPP was a risk factor renal cancer using in vitro and in vivo experiments, and Saquib et al. (Saquib et al., <a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">2022</a>) reported that TDCPP is genotoxic and hepatotoxic to human liver cells, as well as being a potential carcinogen. Recently, Xu et al. (T. Xu et al., <a href="#CR55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR55">2024</a>) reported that organophosphate esters (e.g., TCEP) contribute to the proliferation and migration of triple-negative breast cancer cells. However, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the relations between OPFRs and the development of ESCC.</p>
<p id="Par36">Our results showed a significant positive correlation between BDCPP, BCEP, and TDCPP and the development of ESCC, with TDCPP associated with the worse OS of patients with ESCC. Exposure to TDCPP has been shown to induce cell apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and inhibition of <em>cyclin</em> D1, CDK2, CDK6, and Bcl-2 expression, thereby affecting the development of ESCC (D. Cui et al., <a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">2020</a>). For example, Saquib et al. (Saquib et al., <a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">2022</a>) reported that TDCPP is carcinogenic in human liver cells, and the study reported the mechanisms included an increase in intracellular ROS, a decrease of mitochondrial membrane potential, cytoplasmic localization of P53, caspase 3, and caspase 9, and upregulation of 11 genes and downregulation of 2 genes. Similarly, Cui et al. (D. Cui et al., <a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">2020</a>) examined the mechanisms of cytotoxicity of TDCPP in human skin keratinocytes. However, we observed that z-score–standardized TDCPP concentrations had a borderline significant effect on ESCC risk (aHR = 1.17, <em>p</em> = 0.079), suggesting a potential nonlinear relationship (Table <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S6</a>). Previous research has reported a nonlinear dose–response relationship between organophosphate flame retardants and all-cause mortality in adults aged 40 years and older (Yan et al., <a href="#CR56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR56">2023</a>). Although our analyses accounted for multiple confounders, we cannot rule out the influence of unmeasured confounders or chance. Future research is warranted to confirm any nonlinear association between TDCPP exposure and cancer and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.</p>
<p id="Par37">Many reports have indicated that carcinogenesis due to environmental chemicals is in part related to an increase in oxidative stress and ROS caused by the chemicals (Mena et al., <a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">2009</a>; Thanan et al., <a href="#CR47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR47">2014</a>). An et al. (An et al., <a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2016</a>) studied the cytotoxicity of 4 OPFRs (TPP, TBP, TBEP, TCPP) on HepG2 hepatoma cells, A549 lung cancer cells, and Caco-2 colon cancer cells. The results showed that all 4 OPFRs inhibited cell viability, increased the production of ROS, caused DNA damage, and increased LDH leakage in all 3 cells lines. In a recent review, Wang and Song (X. Wang &amp; Song, <a href="#CR52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR52">2024</a>) summarized the mechanisms associated with the neurotoxicity of TDCPP. Similar to the mechanisms by which TDCPP causes malignancies, mechanisms of neurotoxicity include oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, neuroinflammation, and epigenetic regulation. In a murine study, Wang et al. (C. Wang et al., <a href="#CR51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR51">2020</a>) reported that TCEP caused neurotoxicity in mice by inducing oxidative damage and increasing thyroid hormone levels. In this study, we further elucidated the potential mechanisms by which TDCPP affects ESCC cells. Our results in vitro experiments demonstrated that TDCPP increased ROS levels and induced oxidative stress, apoptosis, and cytotoxicity in ESCC cell lines.</p>
<p id="Par38">In recent years, GSEA has become a very valuable tool for identifying the effects of diseases as well as environmental chemicals on genes (upregulation and downregulation), and the molecular pathways and biological functions of the genes that are altered (X. Wang &amp; Song, <a href="#CR52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR52">2024</a>). In brief, our results showed that TDCPP exposure significantly upregulated pathways such as cytokine binding, G protein-coupled peptide receptor activity, neurotransmitter receptor activity, immune response-related pathways including the adaptive immune response, leukocyte-mediated cytotoxicity, and regulation of lymphocyte-mediated immunity, cellular immunity and signal transduction. Cytokines like TGF-β, VEGF, angiogenic chemokines, and inflammatory cytokines (especially Th2-induced cytokines) have been shown to be associated with tumor-induced angiogenesis, inflammation, and immunosuppression (Burkholder et al., <a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">2014</a>). G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) was also found as key players in tumor growth and metastasis. Malignant cells exploit GPCR functions to survive, proliferate, evade immunity, enhance blood supply, invade tissues, and spread to other organs (Dorsam &amp; Gutkind, <a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">2007</a>). Additionally, neurotransmitters influence immune and endothelial cells in the tumor microenvironment, promoting tumor progression, therefore, cancer cells could exploit neurotransmitter-initiated signaling pathways for uncontrolled proliferation and spread (Jiang et al., <a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">2020</a>). In a previous study, ESCC was observed to be enriched with immune-suppressive cells, such as Tregs, exhausted CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, NK cells, and M2 macrophages, which may facilitate immune escape and tumor progression (Zheng et al., <a href="#CR59" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR59">2020</a>). Yu et al. (K. Yu et al., <a href="#CR57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR57">2022</a>) used GO, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes, and GSEA and reported that OPFRs were closely linked to bladder cancer, and that immune-related genes were associated with the prognosis, clinical characteristics, and response to immunotherapies in bladder cancer. The authors concluded that OPFRs and their metabolites could promote the progression of bladder cancer by affecting the expression of immune genes. Studies using similar methods have found that mutations in some molecules of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway are closely associated with survival and prognosis in ESCC patients, and that there are a large number of molecules that can regulate the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway and overexpression of many can cause aberrant activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway and affect the prognosis of ESCC (Luo et al., <a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">2022</a>).</p>
<p id="Par39">On the other hand, TDCPP exposure significantly downregulated pathways like ATPase activity, lipase activity, motor activity, condensed chromosome and microtubule-associated complex, pathways related to cellular structure and metabolic processes such as cell killing, muscle hypertrophy, and ciliogenesis, as well as vascular smooth muscle contraction and pancreatic secretion. Recent studies on colon cancer cells reveal that ATPase inhibitory factor 1 (IF1) blocks mitochondrial ATP synthase, leading to increased aerobic glycolysis and ROS signaling, which boosts cell proliferation and survival (Sánchez-Aragó et al., <a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">2013</a>). This indicates that reduced ATPase activity may promote cancer cell progression.</p>
<p id="Par40">Additionally, ESCC could escape immune destruction by developing mechanisms regularly employed by the immune system to regulate itself (PD-1/PD-L1 pathway) (R. Li et al., <a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">2022</a>). Previous studies also found that muscle function and structure may play a role in ESCC development (M. Liu et al., <a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">2021</a>). Skeletal muscles contain immune cells, engaging in cross-talk with innate immune cells in both normal and disease conditions, such as myopathies, endotoxemia, and muscle injuries. Paracrine/autocrine signaling and direct contact play roles in this interaction (Pillon et al., <a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">2013</a>). Additionally, innate immune receptors, like toll-like and NOD-like receptors, impact muscle metabolism, and muscle cells secrete factors that influence the immune system (Pillon &amp; Krook, <a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">2017</a>).</p>
<p id="Par41">Taken together, the results of our study and those of prior published studies suggest implications for the management of patients with ESCC. Notably, testing for TDCPP and BDCPP and BCEP exposure in persons at risk for exposure can be valuable for early detection and risk assessment for ESCC. The cytotoxicity and oxidative stress induced by TDCPP provide insights into the pathogenesis of ESCC. Notably, the link between OPFR exposure and ESCC highlights the necessity for public health initiatives to reduce OPFR exposure through stricter regulations and increased public awareness. These strategies could improve ESCC management, patient outcomes, and reduce environmental risk factors.</p></section><section id="Sec20"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Strengths and limitations</h2>
<p id="Par42">This study has several strengths, including being the first to explore the potential association between OPFRs and ESCC development through findings from observational epidemiology as well as in vitro and in vivo experiments, thereby providing new insights into environmental risk factors for human cancers. However, the study has limitations, including a relatively small sample size and its observational design. Participants were recruited from a single medical center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader Taiwanese population and restricts the ability to establish causality. Additionally, the urinary concentration of OPFRs in participants was measured only once in this study. However, given the short half-life of OPFRs in the body (typically ranging from a few hours to several days), the measured concentration may only reflect recent exposure and may not adequately represent long-term cumulative exposure prior to the study endpoints.</p></section><section id="Sec21"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p id="Par43">In conclusion, this study provides evidence on the association between OPFRs, particularly TDCPP, and the development and progression of ESCC. BDCPP and TDCPP level in urine are associated with an increased risk of ESCC, while elevated TDCPP levels are correlated to poorer survival outcomes in ESCC patients. The cytotoxic effects of TDCPP, including oxidative stress and apoptosis, were confirmed through in vitro and in vivo experiments. The study also highlights the significant role of differential gene expression and key functional pathways in the esophagus of TDCPP-treated mice, further elucidating the potential mechanisms through which OPFRs may contribute to ESCC development. These findings underscore the importance of considering environmental exposures in the etiology of ESCC and the need for further research into the carcinogenic impacts of OPFRs.</p></section><section id="Sec22"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h2>
