<p id="Par1"><em>Pseudostellaria heterophylla</em> (Miq.) Pax ex Pax et Hoffm. is a medicinally and economically critical plant cultivated for its tuberous roots, with a documented history in traditional medicine spanning three millennia. Under the escalating pressures of global climate change (e.g., drought and soil salinization), ensuring stable and high-yield production of <em>P. heterophylla</em> is imperative for market sustainability. The beneficial fungus <em>T. crassum</em> has demonstrated potential in enhancing crop abiotic stress resistance through multiple biological processes. Critically, <em>Trichoderma</em>-mediated rhizosphere metabolite reprogramming may underpin its role in protecting <em>P. heterophyl</em>la against environmental stresses, thereby ensuring yield stability. Elucidating the metabolic shifts driven by <em>T. crassum</em> is thus essential for harnessing its full agronomic potential. In this study, we employed UHPLC‒MS/MS to analyze the metabolic profiles of <em>Pseudostellaria heterophylla</em> under separate and coculture conditions with different concentrations of <em>T. crassum</em>. The results indicated that 521, 376, and 594 DAMs were identified in each treatment group and the control group, respectively. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed 11 pathways associated with abiotic stress, and DAMs such as cucurbitacin E and secologanin were significantly enriched in these pathways. Additionally, 5 pathways related to component synthesis were identified. Key metabolites such as 5,6-epoxy-8,11,14-eicosatrienoic acid and D-glucose were enriched in these pathways. Fourteen pathways were enriched with metabolites (e.g., sucrose, trehalose, and protocatechuic acid) critical for plant growth. We hypothesize that the differentially abundant metabolites within these pathways may contribute to enhancing the stress resistance of <em>P. heterophylla</em> through putative mechanisms, such as increasing the activity of resistance enzymes, maintaining cellular osmotic balance, promoting hormone synthesis, enhancing cell wall synthesis, and facilitating root cell division and elongation. This study revealed that <em>T. crassum</em> remodels the rhizosphere metabolites of <em>heterophylla</em>, laying the groundwork for its agronomic application to bolster crop hardiness.</p>

<p id="Par2">In recent years, climate change has become an additional threat to global agricultural production. Abiotic stresses such as drought stress and suboptimal nitrogen levels severely limit the productivity of many crops. Compared with normal conditions, drought stress can reduce grain yield by 53% [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>]. A recent study predicted a 4.1–6.4% reduction in wheat yields for each temperature increase of 1 °C due to climate change [<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>]. More than 10% of the total production area is affected by continuous cropping obstacles, which leads to a 20–80% reduction in crop yield, causing billions of dollars in economic losses annually [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>]. Solely relying on a plant’s own defense mechanisms to achieve a satisfactory defensive effect is insufficient because not all plants are capable of self-repair or growth in the face of adverse conditions. Therefore, determining how to improve plant resistance to abiotic stress is particularly important. Rhizosphere microorganisms are a well-known means of alleviating abiotic stress [<a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>, <a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>]. They can release bioavailable nutrients, regulate plant hormone levels, increase enzyme activity or other means to increase plant survival and mitigate the effects of abiotic stress [<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>]. The combined use of <em>Bacillus velezensis</em> ZF336 and CaCN2 significantly increases the activity of enzymes related to carbohydrate metabolism, representing an effective approach for controlling cucumber root rot, increasing yield, and improving soil quality [<a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>]. <em>Bacillus</em> sp. strain RA enhances salt tolerance in tomato by coordinating myo-inositol metabolism [<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>]. Therefore, screening functional probiotics conferring abiotic stress tolerance is the top priority when optimizing for crop cultivation.</p>
<p id="Par3"><em>Trichoderma</em> are free-living and ubiquitous fungi in nature and colonize plant roots, assisting plants in coping with abiotic stressors through multiple mechanisms [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>]. Secondary metabolites probably mediate multiple functions of <em>Trichoderma</em> most directly. Many trichoderma species can produce indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), which is believed to promote <em>Arabidopsis</em> root growth [<a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>]. Zhan et al. reported that antifungal metabolites from <em>Trichoderma pinnatum</em> LS029-3 suppress postharvest stem-end rot in mango [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>]. <em>Trichoderma</em>-synthesized silicon nanoparticles and metabolites alleviate cadmium and bacterial wilt stress in tomato [<a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>]. Unlike with crop maintenance, <em>Trichoderma</em> is rarely used in conjunction with traditional Chinese medicinal herbs. Therefore, exploring the function of <em>Trichoderma</em> is pivotal for the transition toward a more sustainable form of agriculture.</p>
<p id="Par4"><em>Pseudostellaria heterophylla</em> is an herbaceous perennial, a Caryophyllaceae species known as Taizishen, and is officially listed in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia. Its characteristics, such as its excellent pharmacological efficacy, wide range of applications and high demand on the market, prompt its intensive cultivation. However, abiotic stresses severely hinder further development. For instance, temperatures above 30 °C during the spring‒summer transition cause stems and leaves to stop growing and senesce. Simultaneously, the underground tubers reach their peak dry matter accumulation and enter dormancy. This results in a shortened effective photosynthetic period, low cultivation efficiency, and, consequently, low yields [<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>]. <em>P. heterophylla</em> is intolerant to high temperatures and strong light and naturally occurs in cool, shaded environments under forest canopies [<a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>]. However, under cultivation, exposure to environmental stresses, such as heat, intense light, and drought, triggers premature physiological senescence, ultimately reducing tuber yield. <em>Trichoderma</em> has been shown to be among the most effective strategies for improving abiotic stress resistance, and detailing its mechanism of action is important for alleviating the challenges associated with intensive cultivation.</p>
<p id="Par5">Nontargeted metabolomics is a novel analytical tool that allows the simultaneous determination of numerous substance components and the comprehensive analysis of metabolite changes [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. This tool can be combined with multivariate statistical analysis to screen for differentially abundant metabolites. Several recent studies have used this approach. For example, nontargeted metabolomics analysis revealed that 29 compounds underwent significant changes before and after almond roasting, among which 14 were positively correlated with antioxidant activity [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>]. Nontargeted metabolomics combined with chemometrics revealed distinct metabolic profiles during the two fermentation stages of sparkling wine. Increases in the contents of volatile compounds such as linalool, β-damascenone, acetic acid, and ethyl acetate significantly enhanced the characteristic aroma of the sparkling wine [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>]. LC‒MS has the advantages of high precision, high sensitivity, and high output, and it provides robust tool support for comprehensively parsing <em>Trichoderma</em>’s function.</p>
<p id="Par6">To the best of our knowledge, there have been numerous reports on the abilities of <em>Trichoderma</em> spp., but few studies have investigated the involvement of <em>Trichoderma</em> in improving the abiotic stress resistance of <em>P. heterophylla.</em> Most existing studies have focused on the phenotypic and physiological responses of <em>P. heterophylla</em> to microbial inoculation. A critical knowledge gap remains in our understanding of the underlying metabolic reprogramming triggered by <em>Trichoderma</em> symbiosis at the rhizosphere level, which is pivotal for mediating enhanced stress resistance. Specifically, nontargeted metabolomic profiles of the <em>P. heterophylla</em>–<em>Trichoderma</em> interactive system are severely lacking. Such metabolomic insights are crucial for deciphering the precise mechanisms through which <em>Trichoderma</em> modulates host plant physiology. Therefore, in this study, nontargeted metabolomics was used to comprehensively characterize the metabolite profile of <em>Trichoderma crassum</em> (<em>T. crassum</em>) in a coculture system with <em>P. heterophylla.</em> This investigation aimed to decipher the metabolic regulatory pathways and molecular mechanism through which <em>Trichoderma</em> enhances abiotic stress tolerance in <em>P. heterophylla</em>. These findings provide a foundation for targeted application of <em>Trichoderma</em>.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Strain and plant materials</h3>
<p id="Par7">The experimental strain, which was isolated from the root of <em>P. heterophylla</em>, was identified as <em>T. crassum</em> and named WMM-1-7. It has been deposited in the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC), a publicly accessible culture repository, with the accession number M 20,242,688 20,241,202. The seeds utilized in this study were collected from the experimental plots managed by our team in Shibing County, Guizhou Province, China, with authorization from Professor Zhou Tao. The seedlings were cultivated from surface-sterilized seeds in sterile culture bottles. The voucher specimens have been deposited in the publicly accessible Herbarium of the School of Pharmacy, Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, with the accession number QZTM 0008973. The preserved strain was activated on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium at 28 °C before the cultivation experiments. After 3 days, an approximately 5 mm diameter mycelial plug was used to inoculate the fungus into potato dextrose broth (PDB) medium. The culture was grown at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 3 days. Spore suspensions were filtered using three layers of gauze to remove residual mycelium, and the spore concentration was determined using a Thoma chamber. Different concentrations of spore suspensions (L: 7.5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL; M: 3.75 × 10⁶ CFU/mL; H: 6.25 × 10⁶ CFU/mL) were subsequently added to MS liquid medium at a ratio of 1:50. We denoted CK, L, M, and H as the blank control, low-concentration fungal suspension, medium-concentration fungal suspension, and high-concentration fungal suspension, respectively.</p>
<p id="Par8">Three uniform <em>P. heterophylla</em> seedings were also cultured in MS liquid medium. The <em>P. heterophylla</em> seedlings were derived from the same batch of tissue culture subcultures. The plants were maintained under strictly aseptic conditions to ensure the absence of any detectable microbial contaminants other than the applied <em>T. crassum</em>. The plant materials were sterile tissue-cultured seedlings maintained through laboratory subculture, with their original maternal lines obtained via embryo dissection from surface-sterilized seeds. All operations were conducted within a UV- and ethanol-sterilized laminar flow hood, with tools sterilized by autoclaving and flame treatment to ensure aseptic conditions. The fungi and plants were cocultured for 14 days under 14 h of light at 22 °C/10 hours in the dark at 16 °C. After freeze-drying, the culture medium was prepared for sequencing at Major Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All the assays consisted of three technical sample replicates. Metabolite profiling identification was performed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry.</p></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample preparation and extraction</h3>
<p id="Par9">The MS medium from the root‒fungus coculture system without further processing was collected for metabolite extraction. Two hundred microliters of sample was transferred into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Afterward, 800 µL of extraction solution containing methanol: acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) was added and mixed. After vortexing the tube for 30 s and 30 min of low-temperature ultrasonic extraction (5 °C, 40 kHz), the sample was allowed to stand at − 20 °C for 30 min. Following centrifugation at 13,000 g at 4 °C for 15 min, the supernatant was dried under a nitrogen stream. After drying, 120 µL of acetonitrile–water (1:1) was added for reconstitution, followed by vortexing for 30 s; extraction was carried out by 5-min low-temperature ultrasonication (5 °C, 40 kHz). The suspension was centrifuged for 10 min at 13,000 g at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred into a glass vial for LC‒MS analysis. In addition, 20 µL of the supernatant from each sample was mixed as a quality control sample.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LC‒MS conditions</h3>
<p id="Par10">Metabolite extracts were analyzed using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) mass spectrometry (MS) coupled with a Thermo Fisher Scientific Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer. The chromatographic column was an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA). Mobile phase A was 95% water + 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), and mobile phase B was 47.5% acetonitrile and 47.5% isopropyl alcohol + 5% water. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 3 µl. During detection, the QC samples were inserted between every 4 experimental samples to monitor the batches.</p>
<p id="Par11">All mass spectra were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes using electrospray ionization. The following parameters were used: auxiliary gas heater temperature, 425 °C; capillary temperature, 325 °C; sheath gas flow rate, 50; auxiliary gas flow rate, 13; S-Lens voltage, 50 kV; and scanning range, 70–1050 m/z (Table S1). The total run time was 8 min. An internal standard method was employed in this study, and 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine, palmitoyl-L-carnitine (N-methyl-d3), cholic acid-2,2,4,4-d4 and DL-Phe-d5 were chosen as the internal standards. The detailed gradient elution programs for both positive and negative ionization modes are provided in Table S2. The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min.</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Qualitative and quantitative determination of metabolites</h3>
<p id="Par12">For qualitative analysis of the metabolites, the raw data were preprocessed using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA). The mass error tolerance for full-scan MS spectra was set to 10 ppm. The MS and MS/MS mass spectra information was matched with the self-built database and the mainstream public metabolic database (<a href="http://www.hmdb.ca/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.hmdb.ca/</a>, <a href="https://metlin.scripps.edu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://metlin.scripps.edu/</a>). The identification met level 2 (putatively identified compounds) according to MSI guidelines.</p>
<p id="Par13">Metabolites were quantified in relation to the peak area of the internal standard. The peak area of each chromatographic peak represents the relative content of the corresponding metabolite. The raw data, comprising quality control samples, were imported into the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment. This process ultimately resulted in a data matrix characterized by the retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensity. To facilitate data analysis, a series of preprocessing steps were undertaken, primarily involving the simulation of missing values in the raw data and data normalization. Through these preprocessing steps, the influence of variations unrelated to the research objectives on data analysis can be minimized, thereby enhancing the screening and analysis of potential target differentially abundant metabolites. In data processing, preprocessing is systematically conducted through missing value filtering, missing value imputation, data normalization, QC validation, and data transformation. Missing values are imputed using the mean value, whereas data normalization is performed using the sum method to mitigate errors arising from sample handling, concentration differences, and instrument biases. Ions with a relative standard deviation (RSD) greater than 30% are excluded from the dataset, and only ions with an RSD of 30% or less are selected for differential quantitative analysis (Figure S1). The data undergo logarithmic transformation to improve the normality of the data structure. The qualitative and preprocessed quantitative information was ultimately integrated to obtain Table S3.</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="Par14">One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted utilizing SPSS software version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05 serving as the defined threshold for determining statistical significance. The differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) between two biological groups were identified through integrated multivariate and univariate analyses, combining orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) with parametric (Student’s t test) and nonparametric (Mann-Whitney U test) methods. In the OPLS-DA analysis, variables with variable importance in projection (VIP) values exceeding 1 were deemed significant. To mitigate the risk of model overfitting, a method involving 200 permutation test was employed to assess the model’s fitting performance (Fig. S2). Significant variables were selected based on a <em>P</em> value &lt; 0.05 and a VIP &gt; 1.0, with the results visualized in a volcano plot. DAMs were identified using the Hunan Metabolome Database (HMDB), and a pie chart was constructed through the Meiji Bio-Information Cloud Platform. The DAMs were annotated with the KEGG database, and the annotated pathways were then mapped using the Major Bio-Information Cloud Platform. The column graphs were created using Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corp., MA, USA).</p></section></section><section id="Sec8"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="Sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Plant–fungal symbiosis expands the rhizosphere metabolic diversity</h3>
<p id="Par15">A coculture assay (L: low-concentration fungal suspension, M: medium-concentration fungal suspension, H: high-concentration fungal suspension) was performed to determine whether <em>T. crassum</em> significantly increased the quantity of rhizosphere metabolites in <em>P. heterophylla</em> (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>). A previous study demonstrated that compared with the control treatment, <em>T. crassum</em> inoculation increased the number of metabolites in the culture medium by 48.3–53.6% [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>]. These metabolites were classified into 16 categories in the HMDB. The major superclasses included organic acids and derivatives (346, 20.63%), organoheterocyclic compounds (346, 20.63%), lipids and lipid-like molecules (339, 20.21%), organic oxygen compounds (213, 12.70%), benzenoids (172, 10.26%), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (115, 6.86%), and nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogs (68, 4.05%). These functionally diverse metabolites may work together to regulate plant growth and development. Organoheterocyclic compounds such as alkaloids may be related to cadmium tolerance [<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>]. Soil heavy metal pollution is a global ecological and environmental issue that has garnered widespread attention. Low-molecular-weight organic acids help increase the tolerance and accumulation of lead in Centipedegrass [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>]. Lipids and lipid molecules, such as energy sources, membrane construction, and signaling molecules, are crucial for life activities [<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>]. These three types of components account for more than 60% of all the detected metabolites.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/Tab3/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="Sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes annotation and enrichment analysis for DAMs</h3>
<p id="Par25">DAMs interact within biological systems to form distinct metabolic pathways. Using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, we annotated the DAMs in each comparison group and performed enrichment analysis to identify associated pathways. The results revealed that the metabolic pathways differing between the treatment and control groups were enriched primarily in five major categories: metabolism (M), environmental information processing (EIP), organismal systems (OS), human diseases (HD), and cellular processes (CP).</p>
<p id="Par26">The top 20 enriched pathways were selected based on the p value. In the L and CK groups, the DAMs were predominantly enriched in tryptophan metabolism, staurosporine biosynthesis, arginine biosynthesis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, beta-alanine metabolism, penicillin and cephalosporin biosynthesis (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>A). In the M and CK groups, galactose metabolism was the most significantly enriched pathway. The DAMs were primarily associated with arginine biosynthesis, pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism, biosynthesis of terpenoids and steroids, and flavonoid biosynthesis (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>B). In the H and CK groups, the DAMs were predominantly enriched in tryptophan metabolism, staurosporine biosynthesis, penicillin and cephalosporin biosynthesis, nucleotide metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism and lysine biosynthesis (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>C). Some overlapping pathways, such as tryptophan metabolism, staurosporine biosynthesis and ABC transporters, were identified from the various comparisons. The enrichment of tryptophan metabolism might be due to the interference of the treatment group with the auxin biosynthesis pathway, affecting root growth and increasing plant resistance to various abiotic and biological stresses [<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>]. Staurosporine biosynthesis regulates cellular behavior by interfering with kinase activity [<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>]. ABC transporter proteins help plants cope with environmental stress by transporting plant hormones and heavy metals [<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>]. A detailed list of all significantly enriched pathways, filtered by adjusted p value and including the number of mapped metabolites, is provided in Supplementary Table S4. The synergistic effects of these pathways collectively constitute the mechanism of action of <em>T. crassum</em> on <em>P. heterophylla</em>.</p>

The coculture system induced changes in rhizosphere metabolites that promoted microecological amelioration. <em>T. crassum</em> has been shown to exert pleiotropic effects, including stress resistance, growth promotion, quality improvement and other functions (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>).</p>

<p id="Par30">Our metabolomic analysis revealed that the symbiotic treatment specifically induced the significant accumulation of key functional metabolites, including 2-amino-3‑methylsuccinic acid, protocatechuic acid, 5,6-epoxy-8,11,14-eicosatrienoic acid, secologanin, and M-cresol (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>). Enrichment analysis showed that the DAMs were collectively mapped to 11 pathways closely related to plant stress resistance processes across three significantly enriched pathway maps: beta-alanine metabolism, lysine degradation, ABC transporters, catecholamine transferase inhibitors, aminobenzoate degradation, toluene degradation, biosynthesis of terpenoids and steroids, biosynthesis of alkaloids derived from terpenoids and polyketides, drug metabolism–cytochrome P450, linoleic acid metabolism, and arachidonic acid metabolism. When plants respond to abiotic stress, they do not activate a single pathway in isolation but rather coordinate the aforementioned multifaceted physiological and biochemical processes. Such processes include metabolic remodeling and energy/precursor supply (such as beta-alanine metabolism, lysine degradation, and aminobenzoate degradation), highlighted by the accumulation of 2-amino-3-methylsuccinic acid as a key energy-providing intermediate; the synthesis of key defensive compounds (such as the biosynthesis of terpenoids and steroids and the biosynthesis of alkaloids derived from terpenoids and polyketides), supported by the rise in secologanin, a central precursor for defensive alkaloids, and the antioxidant protocatechuic acid; the detoxification of exogenous/endogenous substances (such as drug metabolism–cytochrome P450 and catecholamine transferase inhibitors); membrane lipid metabolism and lipid signaling (such as linoleic acid metabolism and arachidonic acid metabolism), marked by the increase in the signaling lipid 5,6 - epoxy − 8,11,14 - eicosatrienoic acid; and the construction of selective barriers and substance transport (such as ABC transporters). These results strongly suggest that plants may establish comprehensive stress resistance by coordinating these pathways to regulate energy metabolism, synthesize protective secondary metabolites, increase their detoxification capacity, maintain membrane stability, and control the transmembrane transport of substances.</p>

<p id="Par32">Fourteen significantly enriched pathways were predicted to be potentially associated with growth promotion. A functional analysis of these enriched pathways revealed that they may collaboratively act on multiple key aspects of the growth promotion mechanism. First, the central role of amino acid metabolism, including tryptophan metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, arginine and proline metabolism, and lysine biosynthesis, is prominent. These pathways provide not only essential amino acid precursors for protein synthesis but also their metabolites (such as indole-3-acetic acid), which are often reported to have direct bioactive properties or to be involved in signal transduction [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>].</p>
<p id="Par33">Second, the enhancement of energy metabolism and carbon source utilization capacity is another notable feature. Glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and the pentose phosphate pathway provide primary energy (ATP) and reducing power (NADPH) and generate various biosynthetic precursors (e.g., sucrose and pantothenic acid) (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>). Carbohydrate digestion and absorption, the phosphotransferase system (PTS), and galactose metabolism collectively point to the ability of microbes to efficiently uptake and utilize carbohydrates (especially monosaccharides and disaccharides) from the environment, which is fundamental for sustaining vigorous metabolism and growth. Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism links sugar metabolism with the synthesis of cell wall components (such as peptidoglycan and lipopolysaccharide). Furthermore, nucleic acid synthesis is closely related to cell proliferation. Nucleotide metabolism and pyrimidine metabolism provide the essential nucleotides required for DNA/RNA synthesis and are indispensable for the rapid division and proliferation of cells. Finally, the supply of key cofactors and one-carbon units serves as the cornerstone supporting these metabolic networks. Pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis ensures the availability of coenzyme A, which is required for core processes such as fatty acid metabolism; the folate one-carbon pool provides carriers for one-carbon unit transfer reactions, extensively participating in purine synthesis, amino acid interconversion (such as serine–glycine), and methylation reactions, which are crucial for both nucleic acid and amino acid metabolism. In summary, this set of enriched pathways collectively delineates an integrated metabolic network based on efficient energy acquisition and carbon source utilization, enhanced synthesis of critical biomolecules such as essential amino acids and nucleotides, and reliance on important cofactors. This network likely constitutes the core biochemical mechanism underlying its growth-promoting potential.</p></section><section id="Sec15"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Quality improvement</h4>
<p id="Par34">In-depth analysis has revealed that specific metabolites, such as 17-Hydroxyprogesterone, 5,6-Epoxy-8,11,14-eicosatrienoic acid, and D-Glucose, influence the synthesis of secondary metabolites and organ development in plants by modulating endogenous hormone signaling responses. For instance, lipid derivatives like 5,6-Epoxy-8,11,14-eicosatrienoic acid, a metabolite of arachidonic acid, are known to enhance disease resistance by modulating phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and MAPK signaling pathways in plants [<a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>]. Meanwhile, the dynamics of sugars such as D-Glucose are integral to plant development and are regulated through complex transcriptional networks, as demonstrated in multi-omics studies on maize domestication [<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a>]. The metabolism of steroidal substances in plants, including the synthesis of brassinosteroid precursors, directly participates in cell expansion, stress responses, and the biosynthesis of medicinal components. Processes analogous to lipid transport and sterol homeostasis are associated with membrane integrity, signal transduction, and the supply of secondary metabolite precursors. These pathways collectively regulate the enrichment of plant nutrients (such as saponins/polysaccharides) and provide molecular targets for quality improvement.</p></section><section id="Sec16"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Other functions</h4>
<p id="Par35">Furthermore, several pathways were significantly enriched in the molecular network of biological defense. Among them, penicillin and cephalosporin biosynthesis, although not directly producing antibiotics in plants, suggests the potential for the synthesis of β-lactam secondary metabolite analogs, which are possibly involved in the regulation of pathogen cell wall hydrolases. The biosynthesis of siderophore group nonribosomal peptides restricts pathogen growth by chelating environmental iron ions and enhancing their own iron absorption to maintain oxidative stress balance. Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis results in the production of lignin to reinforce the physical barrier of cell walls. Staurosporine biosynthesis generates phytoalexins (such as resveratrol analogs) that directly inhibit fungal infection. Cutin, suberin, and wax form a hydrophobic barrier on the surface of root tubers, blocking the invasion of soil-borne pathogens (such as Fusarium) and reducing water loss. ABC transporters, acting as “molecular pumps” located on the cell membrane, utilize the energy generated from ATP hydrolysis to actively transport exogenous toxic substances (such as toxins secreted by pathogens and heavy metal ions) or endogenous metabolic waste out of the cell, thereby reducing the concentration of toxic substances within the cell. The antifolate resistance pathway may maintain folate metabolic homeostasis by modifying dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), counteracting the toxicity of folate antagonists secreted by pathogens. These pathways synergistically increase the resistance of <em>P. heterophylla</em> to biotic stress (pathogens).</p></section></section></section><section id="Sec17"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p id="Par36"><em>Trichoderma</em> is a group of filamentous fungi that are widely distributed in soil ecosystems and demonstrate significant biocontrol and growth-promoting functions in the microenvironments of plant roots [<a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>]. Modern microbiome research has further revealed that the ability of <em>Trichoderma</em>, either alone or in combination with rhizosphere microorganisms such as <em>Rhizobium leguminosarum</em>, to form synthetic microbial communities (SynCom) significantly increases plant adaptability to adverse conditions [<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>, <a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>]. In the cultivation system of <em>P. heterophylla</em>, an important medicinal plant, strains represented by <em>Trichoderma</em> have been proven to effectively alleviate continuous cropping obstacles and increase tuber yield and medicinal component content, providing key technical support for the development of green planting models [<a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a>]. Notably, the functions of <em>Trichoderma</em> rely not only on direct interactions between living cells and plant roots but also on the secondary metabolites they secrete [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>]. Research has indicated that the metabolome of <em>Trichoderma</em> encompasses a diverse array of bioactive compounds, such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), N,N′-diacetylchitobiose, terpenoids, polyketides, and alkaloids [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>, <a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>, <a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>]. At certain concentrations, these compounds can modulate plant gene expression and metabolic pathways, serving as chemical signaling bridges that connect microbial activity with plant physiological responses.</p>
<p id="Par37">The DAMs associated with multi-layered defense network may enhance the stress resistance of <em>P. heterophylla.</em> These DAMs collectively constitute a metabolic profile of and shaped by the fungal-plant symbiotic interaction, triggered by the fungal inoculation. The core mechanism involves the systemic activation of plant immunity. The cell wall components of <em>T. crassum</em>, including chitin and glucan, as well as secreted elicitors, are recognized by the pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) of <em>P. heterophylla</em>. This recognition triggers early defense signals, such as a burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the MAPK cascade, subsequently inducing the expression of genes associated with the salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid (JA) pathways [<a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a>]. Consequently, the plant enters a “preimmune state”, thereby enhancing its ability to respond rapidly to abiotic stress. Concurrently, rhizosphere metabolites can synergistically increase plant abiotic stress tolerance. The remodeling of rhizosphere metabolites demonstrates the diverse potential functions of <em>T. crassum</em>. For instance, the synthesis of trehalose helps maintain cellular osmotic balance, while sucrose significantly improves plant water retention capacity, aiding plants in coping with drought stress [<a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>, <a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a>]. Research has indicated that microorganisms exhibit stress specificity to drought and salt stress, assisting poplar trees in resisting stress through mechanisms such as osmotic regulation and antibiotic secretion [<a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a>]. Malic acid plays a pivotal role in the aluminum tolerance of <em>Arabidopsis thaliana</em>, and trehalose can effectively alleviate the growth inhibition of <em>Avicennia marina</em> caused by cadmium. As a phenylpropanoid metabolite, protocatechuic acid possesses antioxidant properties and the ability to reduce high-valent heavy metals and is often induced to be synthesized under heavy metal stress [<a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a>–<a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">40</a>]. These substances were all detected in this study, supporting the potential key role of <em>T. crassum</em> in enhancing the abiotic stress resilience of <em>P. heterophylla</em>.</p>
<p id="Par38">The key metabolite classes pointed to a potential mechanism for stress adaptation in <em>P. heterophylla</em>. Secondary metabolites, including terpenoids, polyketides, and alkaloids synthesized by <em>Trichoderma</em>, can directly inhibit pathogenic microorganisms. More importantly, these metabolites activate the secondary metabolic pathways of <em>P. heterophylla</em>, enhancing the activity of key enzymes involved in phenylpropanoid metabolism, such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL). This process promotes the accumulation of phytoalexins, lignin, and antimicrobial phenolic compounds, physically reinforcing the cell wall and obstructing pathogen invasion, thereby providing dual protection. On the other hand, <em>T. crassum</em> may enhances the ability of plants to resist abiotic stress by increasing their active components. The medicinal value of <em>P. heterophylla</em> is highly contingent upon the active ingredients that accumulate in its tuberous roots, which include flavonoids, cyclic peptides, alkaloids, and fatty acids [<a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">41</a>]. Our research demonstrated that cocultivation with <em>T. crassum</em> significantly enriched the pathways associated with “flavonoid biosynthesis”, “tryptophan metabolism” and “biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids” in the roots of <em>P. heterophylla</em>, which aligns closely with the results of this study. In treatments involving reduced chemical fertilizers in conjunction with Bacillus KTS-1-1 (which exhibits a synergistic effect with <em>Trichoderma</em>), the expression levels of proteins involved in flavonoid synthesis, such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), chalcone synthase (CHS), and flavonol synthase (FLS), significantly increased in the tuberous roots of <em>P. heterophylla</em>. This ultimately resulted in a 31.6% increase in total flavonoid content [<a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">42</a>]. This phenomenon supports the general principle that microbial fertilizers increase the antioxidant capacity of plants. Flavonoids, recognized as important free radical scavengers, not only increase the stress resistance of <em>P. heterophylla</em> but also directly improve the intrinsic quality of this medicinal herb.</p>
<p id="Par39">Collectively, our data on the metabolites, their classifications, and pathways enable us to propose a hypothetical model. In this model, <em>T. crassum</em> may enhance plant resilience through a potential mechanism that integrates direct chemical signaling, the reinforcement of key metabolic modules, and the systemic activation of stress-responsive genetic programs. Based on the research achievements of <em>T. crassum - P. heterophylla</em> co-culture, the development of functional agents derived from <em>Trichoderma</em> metabolites presents significant potential. A primary limitation of the current study is that the abiotic stress resilience mechanisms inferred from metabolomic data necessitate further experimental validation. Future research should incorporate multi-omics and molecular biology approaches to clarify the underlying mechanisms, thereby establishing a theoretical foundation for formulating precision cultivation strategies.</p></section><section id="Sec18"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p id="Par40">Untargeted metabolomic profiling revealed that <em>T. crassum</em> significantly reshapes the rhizosphere metabolome of <em>P. heterophylla</em>, promoting the accumulation of key metabolites such as trehalose, protocatechuic acid, cucurbitacin E, secologanin, and IAA. Based on their known functions, these compounds may collectively increase plant growth and stress resistance through multiple mechanisms: trehalose likely acts in osmotic adjustment, protocatechuic acid in antioxidant defense, cucurbitacin E and secologanin in chemical defense, and IAA in root development (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>). The metabolite profile provides a mechanistic hypothesis for how <em>T. crassum</em> may increase abiotic stress resistance, yet the causative role of the fungus in initiating these beneficial effects warrants further confirmation. This study highlights the potential of <em>T. crassum</em> as a sustainable bioresource and provides a metabolic foundation for future mechanistic research and microbial agent development in the green cultivation of medicinal plants.</p>
