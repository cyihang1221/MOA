sample_description: The study used multiple biological samples including endophyte-free and common-toxic endophyte (CTE)-infected seeds of Lolium perenne cv Samson; two-week-old seedlings of Triticum aestivum cv Summit infected with Zymoseptoria tritici (WAI321) via syringe infiltration at 5 × 10⁶ spores ml⁻¹; Escherichia coli DH5α cultures grown in lysogeny broth (LB) or on LB agar supplemented with 100 μg ml⁻¹ ampicillin; Epichloë festucae Fl1 (WT) and derived mutant strains (ΔlgsA, ΔlgsC, and complemented ΔlgsA + pKG40/pII99); apoplastic wash fluid extracted from leaves and pseudostem tissues of L. perenne (mock-inoculated, Fl1-infected, CTE-infected) at 18 weeks post-inoculation; and whole-pseudostem tissue extracts from L. perenne for polar metabolite analysis. All fungal cultures were grown on 2.4% (w/v) potato-dextrose (PD) agar or in PD broth. Genomic DNA was extracted from E. festucae Fl1 using a published protocol (Byrd et al., 1990), and plasmid DNA was isolated using the High Pure Plasmid Isolation Kit (Roche). Apoplastic fluid was harvested via vacuum infiltration followed by centrifugation under defined conditions (e.g., 2000 g for 10 min for L. perenne; 5000 g for 5 min at 4°C for T. aestivum), with subsequent debris removal by high-speed centrifugation (17,000 g for 10 min), and stored at −20°C or freeze-dried. Metabolite extraction involved two-phase (MTBE/methanol/water) partitioning and whole-tissue polar extraction using 50% methanol with mechanical homogenization (FastPrep® FP120 system) and filtration.
software_configuration_description: BioCyc was used as one of three databases (alongside KEGG and an in-house Epichloë secondary metabolite database) within the marvis software suite for metabolite annotation during data mining. Specifically, BioCyc was integrated into the marvis-pathway module to perform metabolite set enrichment analysis based on accurate mass information. The configuration included querying metabolite features against the BioCyc database (http://biocyc.org; Caspi et al., 2012) to assign putative identities, with no custom parameter modifications reported; the tool automatically leveraged BioCyc’s curated metabolic pathways and compound entries to support structural annotation of 203 filtered metabolite features (after FDR < 0.003 filtering and adduct correction) derived from LC-HR-MS datasets. BioCyc was used in combination with KEGG and the in-house database to increase annotation confidence and coverage, particularly for fungal-specific secondary metabolites not fully represented in generic databases.

========== sample_description&software_configuration_description entry ==========
sample_description: The study used human erythrocytes infected with the Plasmodium falciparum 3D7 strain (IRBC) and non-infected red blood cells (NRBC) as biological samples. Four separate biological replicate cultures (0.5 mL each) were prepared for each of three pH-adjusted solvent extraction conditions (pH 2, 7, and 9), resulting in 48 total samples equally distributed across four experimental conditions: (1) NRBC, SLO untreated; (2) NRBC, SLO treated; (3) IRBC, SLO untreated; and (4) IRBC, SLO treated. SLO (streptolysin O, 250 units/mL) was applied for 30 min at 37°C to selectively permeabilize NRBCs without lysis, thereby enriching metabolites from IRBCs. Asynchronous P. falciparum cultures were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 25 mM HEPES, 20 mM sodium bicarbonate, 370 µM hypoxanthine, and 0.5% AlbuMAX at 37°C under 90% N2, 5% O2, and 5% CO2 until parasitemia reached ~10%. Human blood samples were collected under protocols approved by the University of Hawaii’s Human Use Research Committee and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with written informed consent obtained from all subjects.
software_configuration_description: BioCyc was used for pathway analysis via the Pathways add-on in Pathway Architect (within Mass Profiler Professional v12.5). The P. falciparum 3D7 strain pathway data in .owl format was downloaded from BioCyc and uploaded into Pathway Architect. Annotated metabolite IDs from the experimental results were mapped to BioCyc database identifiers using open-source BridgeDB software. The Pathway Analysis feature queried the uploaded pathways to generate matching metabolic pathways, which were graphically rendered by the Pathway Architect engine. HeatStrips were generated from a HeatMap table summarizing identified metabolites in NRBC and IRBC conditions. The analysis produced a summary table listing the total number of metabolites per pathway and the number of matching annotated metabolites, followed by ranking pathways based on the number of possible associations to facilitate biological interpretation.

sample_description: The sample used in the research was the wild-type Bacillus thuringiensis strain 4.0718 (CCTCC no. M200016), isolated from soil in south China. This strain produces two types of parasporal crystals—bipyramidal and cuboidal. Cultivation involved activation on LB solid medium (10.0 g/liter tryptone, 5 g/liter yeast extract, 10 g/liter NaCl, 1.5 g/liter agar) at 30°C for 12 h, followed by transfer into 20 ml of fermentation medium in a 250-ml baffled flask and incubation on a rotary shaker at 30°C and 220 rpm. The fermentation medium (pH 7.3) contained beef extract (5.0 g/liter), NaCl (2.0 g/liter), MnSO₄ (0.05 g/liter), tryptone (10.0 g/liter), MgSO₄·7H₂O (0.3 g/liter), glucose (3.0 g/liter), and K₂HPO₄ (0.3 g/liter). Two independent biological replicates were prepared using the same seed batch and cultured simultaneously in the same incubator. Cells were harvested at three time points—10 h (middle vegetative phase, T₁), 20 h (early sporulation phase, T₂), and 32 h (late sporulation phase, T₃)—for protein extraction and analysis. Cell morphology and developmental stage were confirmed via phase-contrast microscopy (Olympus, Japan) at ×1,000 magnification.
software_configuration_description: BioCyc (http://www.biocyc.org/) was used as one of the bioinformatic resources for functional classification of identified proteins. Proteins were classified into functional categories based on their annotated functions in the UniProt Knowledgebase and further supported by homology and functional assignments from the BioCyc metabolic pathway database. No specific software configuration parameters, command-line options, version numbers, or custom settings for BioCyc were described in the Materials and Methods section; it was employed solely as a reference knowledgebase for pathway annotation and functional inference without explicit computational execution details (e.g., no mention of Pathway Tools usage, local installation, query syntax, or parameterized searches).
