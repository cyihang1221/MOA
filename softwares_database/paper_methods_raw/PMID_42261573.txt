
Methods
Two female cadavers were frozen and serially milled at constant
intervals. The sectioned surfaces were photographed using a digital
single-lens reflex (DSLR) camera of 35 mm charge-coupled device
(CCD) sensor for 2023 dataset and a DSLR of 44 mm medium-
format CCD sensor for 2024 dataset.
Results
The 2024 dataset had 2.78 times higher pixel density (pixel size,
0.03 mm × 0.03 mm) than the 2023 dataset (pixel size, 0.05 mm
× 0.05 mm), enabling clearer visualization of fine structures such
as brain nuclei, fasciculi, vessel walls, meninges, and facial
retaining ligaments. Pathological findings including cardiomegaly,
vascular rupture, atherosclerosis, and endometrial neoplasm were
clearly identified. The consistent intervals allowed accurate tracing
of structures across multiple planes. The accumulated datasets from
twelve cadavers now allow statistically reliable anatomical
interpretation.
Conclusion
Sectioned images produced with modern DSLR technology can
reproduce anatomical structures at a level comparable to cadaveric
dissection. Their high resolution, color fidelity, and digital format
allow repeated analysis without damage. These datasets are now
suitable not only for education but also for clinical anatomy
research, potentially contributing to a paradigm shift from
traditional to digital dissection.

INTRODUCTION
In 1996, the Visible Human Project (VHP) was launched in which a
fresh-frozen cadavers in the United States. The cadavers were
milled serially at 1.0 mm (male) and photographed using a semi-
digital camera.1 Since film cameras dominated the era, a semi-
digital system combining a Hasselblad 553 ELX (film camera) with
a Leaf camera back (digital image processor) was employed to
produce true-color (24 bits color) and high resolution (2,048 ×
2,048 pixels) sectioned images. These were the first digital images
to present the natural coloration of body structures in serial
sections1,2 unlike computed tomography and magnetic resonance
imaging.
In 2000, we recognized the need for new sectioned images because
the image quality of the VHP dataset was insufficient for detailed
study of gross and neuroanatomical structures.3 The semi-digital
camera used in the VHP was the primary factor limiting image
quality. Around that time, full-frame digital cameras were
developed, with the Kodak DCS 560 being the first digital single-
lens reflex (DSLR) camera equipped with a full-frame 35 mm
charge-coupled device (CCD) sensor.
Therefore, we initiated the Visible Korean (VK) Project in 2000
using the Kodak DCS560 equipped with a 35 mm CCD sensor.3
From the first cadaver in 2000 to the ninth cadaver in 2023, we
used a total of four DSLRs with 35 mm CCD sensor.4 Because of
these cameras, we could produce the sectioned images of much
higher quality than the VHP. Based on these images,4 we developed
and presented various useful results; Browsing software,5,6 three-
dimensional (3D) surface models,7,8,9 3D volume models,10,11
and average phantom models.12,13,14,15 Nevertheless, further
improvements in resolution were desired to enable even more
detailed anatomical analysis. Finally, in 2024, FujifilmTM released a
new DSLR equipped with a 44 mm medium-format CCD sensor,
providing the potential for superior image quality.
Furthermore, in clinical anatomy research, human anatomical
morphology has traditionally been studied using cadaveric
dissection. These dissections have revealed not only standard
morphology but also variations within normal function, which have
been published in major anatomical and clinical journals.16,17
However, cadaver dissection has fundamental limitations, for
emerging structures, the surrounding tissues must often be altered
or removed, sometimes even eliminating the target itself.
Decomposition and the need for cremation prevent repeated
examination of the same specimen. To overcome these limitations,
image-based dissection serves as the only viable alternative.
Because it relies on digital data, no anatomical structure is damaged
during analysis. The most critical factor in image-based dissection is
image quality, specifically color fidelity and spatial resolution.
This study aimed to compare and evaluate the resolution of the
sectioned images produced using a new medium-format digital
camera equipped with a 44-mm CCD sensor, against those produced
using previous digital cameras with a 35-mm CCD sensor. Through
this comparison, we sought to demonstrate the feasibility and
practicality of the new sectioned images for studying basic and
clinical anatomy.
METHODS
The sectioned images of third female whole body in
2023
From the two female cadavers, first and second sectioned images
were released in 20157 and 2023, respectively.8 In 2023, we
received a female cadaver (73 years old) who had died of
tracheostenosis. The cadaver and gelatin solution with methylene-
blue were put into embedding box, and frozen immediately after
death. The embedding box was sectioned serially. These methods
and techniques for sectioning the cadaver followed those
established in our previous studies.3,18 The only difference in this
study was that the sectioning intervals of head to trunk and lower
limb were 0.5 mm and 1.0 mm respectively.
The sectioned surfaces of field of view with 434 mm (width) × 289
mm (height) were photographed using a Canon™ EOS 5DsR DSLR of
resolution of 8,688 × 5,792 (50,320,896) pixels (full-frame CMOS
image sensor, sensor size of 36 mm × 24 mm, ISO 200, shutter
speed of 1/250, aperture of F/16, manual focus, released in 2015)
with a Canon™ EF Macro IS USM 50 mm lens. So, the sectioned
images with a pixel size of 0.05 mm × 0.05 mm were acquired
(Table 1).

The sectioned images of fourth female whole body in
2024
In 2024, we received a female cadaver who was 82 years old and
had died of multiple organ failure caused by heart failure. The
processes of freezing and sectioning the cadaver were carried out
using the same methods as in previous studies.3,18 The sectioning
intervals of head to trunk and lower limb were 0.5 mm and 1.0 mm
respectively, which were identical to those used in the 2023
experiment.
We prepared DSLR with 44-mm medium-format CCD sensor which
was a FujifilmTM GFX100S II (medium-format CMOS image sensor,
sensor size of 43.8 mm × 32.9 mm, resolution of 11,648 × 8,736
[102,000,000] pixels, released in 2024) with FujifilmTM GF 63 mm
2.8R WR lens.
The sectioned surfaces of field of view with 442 mm (width) × 332
mm (height) were photographed using the Fujifilm DSLR of
resolution of 11,648 × 8,736 pixels (ISO 100, shutter speed of
1/160, aperture of F/8, manual focus). Consequently, the sectioned
images were produced with 0.03 mm × 0.03 mm pixel size were
made (Table 1).

RESULTS
Feasibility of sectioned images in reproducing
anatomical structures comparable to cadaveric
dissection: unparalleled resolution of 2024 dataset
The sectioned images had a pixel size 0.05 mm × 0.05 mm in the
2023 dataset and 0.03 mm × 0.03 mm in the 2024 dataset. A pixel
area was 0.0025 mm2 in 2023 and 0.0009 mm2 in 2024,
corresponding to approximately 400 pixels/mm2 and 1,111 pixels/
mm2, respectively. Therefore, a resolution of 0.03 mm per pixel
provided approximately 2.78 times more pixels per unit area (1
mm2) than 0.05 mm per pixel (Fig. 1, Table 1), enabling the
detection of much finer anatomical structures.
