
Materials and methods
Study area
The study area is located in the Jianfengling branch (108°36′–
109°05′E, 18°23′–18°52′N) of the HTRNP on Hainan Island in
China. Encompassing 679 km2 of mountainous terrain, it has a
mean elevation of 490 m (range: 23–1,391 m) and mean slope >
20°. This area has a tropical monsoon climate characterized by hot-
humid summers and dry winters, with a mean annual temperature
of 24.5 °C and mean annual rainfall of 1,420 mm (range: 1,147–
1,695 mm; WorldClim data, http://www.worldclim.org).
Precipitation increases along elevational gradients. Dominant soils
include Haplic and Humic Acrisols, with minor Haplic Alisols and
Ferric Acrisols. Jianfengling hosts a typical tropical rainforest
ecosystem featuring diverse microclimates and vegetation types:
tropical evergreen seasonal rainforest (46.53%), tropical mountain
rainforest (45.96%), tropical semi deciduous seasonal rainforest
(5.77%), tropical valley rainforest (1.58%), mountaintop moss
dwarf forest (0.16%), and tropical savanna (0.01%) (Yan et al.
2022). Complex topography creates heterogeneous microhabitats
essential for niche specialization.
Historical distribution of Hainan pangolins on Hainan
Island
A dataset of 131 historical occurrence records of Hainan pangolins
during the 1868–2022 period was compiled from multiple data
sources, including documents from Swinhoe (1870), Allen (1906),
Thomas (1910), Allen (1938), Guangdong Institute of Entomology
and Sun Yat-sen University (1983), Shou et al. (1966), and Yan et
al. (2022). Additionally, we compiled data from field surveys
including the first national terrestrial wildlife survey conducted in
1997–1998, a comprehensive scientific survey conducted in the
Yinggeling National Nature Reserve in 2005 and surveys conducted
in Jianfengling by researchers from South China Normal University
(2016–2022). Occurrence localities were georeferenced based on
survey experience and historical descriptions. This dataset enabled
historical distribution reconstruction.
Multi-temporal survey design
Transect sampling protocol
Systematic transect surveys were conducted during 2 epochs:
September 1997–August 1998 and July–December 2022. Survey
preparation involved interviewing local communities to confirm
Hainan pangolin presence within 2 × 2 km grids (Figure 1).
Transects were then established within occupied grid cells,
strategically covering diverse terrain and vegetation types to ensure
representative habitat sampling. Each transect was 3–6 km long,
surveyed by 2 team members walking at 1–2 km/h, with GPS
recording the trajectory. Upon detecting pangolin individuals or
burrows, GPS coordinates were documented. Hainan Pangolin
burrows were identified by their large size and diagnostic features
distinguishing them from sympatric species. Burrow age
classification relied on multiple indicators: the freshness of soil
deposits both inside and outside the entrance; presence or absence
of moss, ferns, or herbaceous plants growing on exterior soil
accumulations; existence of spider webs within the cavity;
structural integrity of the tunnel (presence of collapses); and the
smoothness of inner chamber walls. Field protocols prioritized
documenting recently occupied burrows over degraded or ancient
ones. In 1997–1998, surveys covered 45 transects (182 km) at 24–
1,248 m elevation. In 2022, surveys covered 280 transects (1,608 
km) at 29–1,368 m elevation.

Camera-trapping framework
The camera-trap survey was initially designed to investigate animal
diversity within the protected area. A hierarchical camera-trap
protocol was deployed to address scale-dependent detection
probabilities:
1. Primary grids: 5 × 5 km tessellation (n = 30 grids) with a
single infrared camera (UVL4-CN) per grid;
2. Secondary grids: nested 1 × 1 km sampling in 6 primary
grids and 3 historical occurrence hotspots;
3. Tertiary grids: high-resolution 0.5 × 0.5 km arrays within 2.5
× 2.5 km core zones.
A total of 390 infrared cameras were deployed (Figure 1). Each
camera was fixed on a tree trunk 30–80 cm above ground, adjusted
to local slope and surface features to maximize capture probability
for animals passing ≤10 m from the lens (Figure 2). Each camera
was powered by 12 AA batteries and worked continuously July–
November 2022 (accumulating 49,790 trap-days). All cameras used
passive infrared (PIR) triggering at medium sensitivity, with 5-s
intervals between consecutive triggers. Each trigger captured one
photograph followed by a 30-s video.

Environmental covariate quantification
Elevation data were digitized from 1:50,000 topographic maps
provided by the Jianfengling branch and used to calculate slope (γ)
and aspect (θ) in degrees using ArcGIS 10.8 (Esri). Aspect values
were converted to coordinates with a range of [−1,1] from south to
