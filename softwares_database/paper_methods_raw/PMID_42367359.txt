
Methods
A cross-sectional experimental study was conducted in a simulated
factory environment with four occupational health physicians.
Three conditions were assessed: (1) on-site inspection with remote
VR guidance, (2) remote VR viewing of on-site filming, and (3)
remote personal computer (PC) viewing of on-site filming. A 360-
degree camera (RICOH THETA X) and VR headset (Meta Quest 2)
were used for 4K live streaming at 5 fps.
Results
Video latency was reduced to 1–3 s, markedly improved from 15 s
in prior studies. Visual acuity decreased to 0.1–0.2 for all
participants when using VR. Participants identified an average of
21.0 inspection points (out of 25) using VR headset compared with
19.25 via PC.
Conclusions
VR live streaming is a viable approach for remote workplace
inspections. Although reduced visual acuity remains a limitation, it
may help balance workplace visibility with confidentiality.
However, environmental factors, such as noise, odor, temperature,
humidity, and glare, were not adequately represented, necessitating
the use of supplementary measurement tools. Further research is
needed to evaluate its applicability across diverse settings and long-
term effectiveness.
Keywords: live streaming, occupational health, remote inspection,
virtual reality, workplace inspection
Introduction
In Japan, occupational hygiene efforts have historically been led by
physicians, partly because infectious diseases, such as tuberculosis,
were a major challenge in workplaces1). Since 1972, this approach
has been codified in the Japanese Industrial Safety and Health
Act2), placing the responsibility for assessing workplace health risks
on physicians3) — a role that may be performed by occupational
hygienists in other countries, such as the United States.
Regular occupational health inspections play a critical role in
identifying and mitigating factors that may pose health risks.
However, the current number of dedicated occupational health
physicians in Japan is insufficient to meet demand4). Their scope of
tasks is extensive, yet the resources available to them and allocated
time for each workplace are limited4). Therefore, it is necessary to
perform the required activities as efficiently as possible.
With the Japanese working-age population projected to decline5),
more efficient occupational health practices are necessary. Among
the activities performed by occupational health physicians, worker
interviews can be conducted online under specific conditions, a
practice that became particularly important during the coronavirus
disease pandemic6,7). Nonetheless, concerns, such as decreased
satisfaction and other challenges, have been reported8). In contrast,
online workplace inspections are currently not permitted, likely
owing to the perceived difficulty of comprehensively assessing
workplace conditions remotely. One potential limitation of online
inspections is a restricted field of view, which may hinder the
identification of occupational hazards. A broad visual perspective is
essential for detecting hazardous factors; however, conventional
online systems do not adequately provide this capability. To address
this issue, in 2019, we conducted a preliminary trial using virtual
reality (VR) live streaming with a 360-degree video feed to enable a
wider field of view9). Although the system allowed visualization of
designated pointers within the VR environment, a key limitation
was an average latency of 15 s9). The trial utilized Facebook Live, a
platform widely employed in medical education10,11). Several
factors may account for the delay, including Facebook Live being a
United States-based service, which may result in slower
communication from Japan. Moreover, the platform likely
implements a buffer period to ensure stable communication.
In recent years, with advancements in technology, lower-latency
streaming is becoming increasingly viable. Successful applications
using WebRTC technology have been reported in the context of low-
latency video streaming12). However, its feasibility for VR-based
workplace inspections remains unclear. Therefore, in the present
study, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility of 360-degree VR-based
workplace inspections with reduced latency to address prior
limitations, such as restricted viewing angles and transmission
delays.
Methods
Research design and participants
This cross-sectional experimental study was conducted in February
2024 at a multipurpose simulated factory (7 m×13.7 m) at the
University of Occupational and Environmental Health, Japan. The
simulated factory is designed to resemble the structure of a typical
Japanese factory, is currently used as a lecture space, and includes
various personal protective equipment, posters, and signs on
display. A simulated workplace inspection environment was
established, and remote inspections via VR live streaming were
performed.
The study participants included four physicians specializing in
occupational medicine, with experience ranging from 2 to 20 years.
Among them, three were residents in social medicine (primary
specialization: occupational medicine), and one was a board-
certified supervisory physician for public health and social
medicine, a senior occupational health physician certified by the
Japan Society for Occupational Health, and an Industrial Health
Consultant. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of
Medical Research, University of Occupational and Environmental
Health, Japan (approval no. ER23-054), and written informed
consent was obtained from all participants.
Experimental setup
The inspection area (5.5 m×10 m) contained 25 designated
inspection points, including areas requiring attention and examples
of best practices. These points were determined through
consultation between two researchers, both of whom are senior
occupational health physicians, certified by the Japan Society for
Occupational Health, and Industrial Health Consultants. To evaluate
visual acuity, a 5-m visual acuity chart using Landolt C optotypes,
which adhere to international standards, such as JIS T 7309 and
ISO 8596, was installed, and distance measurements were
performed using a laser rangefinder (GLM50C; Robert Bosch GmbH,
Gerlingen, Germany). Room brightness and other conditions were
not controlled, and this applied to all measurements. To simulate
real-world working conditions, background noise (a composite of
various machine sounds) was continuously played from a speaker
within the inspection area. As a standardized reference point for
setting the volume, the A-weighted sound level (time-weighting:
Fast) was measured to be 60–80 dBA at a distance of 1 m from the
speaker using an NL-42 (RION Co., Ltd., Kokubunji, Japan). This
was intended to replicate a common level of workplace noise that
would not prevent verbal communication. The designated
inspection points also included an item to assess whether the
ambient environmental noise could be perceived through the
remote system. Figure 1 illustrates the inspection area, and Table 1
lists the observed items during workplace inspection.

Equipment and system configuration
For remote live streaming, we used the RICOH THETA X (Ricoh
Company, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) as a 360-degree camera, connected
to an external battery (Anker 511 Power Bank; Anker Innovations,
Changsha, China) (Figure 2). A custom ABS-made jig was designed
and three-dimensionally printed to securely hold the camera and
battery. The Meta Quest 2 (Meta Platforms, Inc., Menlo Park, CA,
USA) was used as the VR headset for viewing the streamed footage
(Figure 3).
