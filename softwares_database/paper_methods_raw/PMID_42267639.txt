
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study area
Our study frame is the Western Sedimentary Basin (Porter et
al., 1982), a vast portion of the Western Canadian boreal forest
underlain by the world's 3rd‐largest petroleum deposits (Figure 2)
(Government of Alberta, 2023) including the oil sands region,
which aggregate three independently managed oil sands areas
(ABMI, 2014). The oil sands region covers a total area of 140,000 
km2 and is characterized by a mosaic of upland and lowland forests,
wetlands, and anthropogenic features resulting from natural
resource extraction (e.g., energy development and agriculture)
which comprise 15.5% of the total area (ABMI, 2014).

This landscape supports a range of mammals, including gray wolf
(Canis lupus), caribou (Rangifer tarandus), moose, black bear (Ursus
americanus), white‐tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus), Canada lynx
(Lynx canadensis), coyote (Canis latrans), red fox (Vulpes vulpes),
fisher (Pekania pennanti), marten (Martes americana), and snowshoe
hare (Lepus americanus).
Sampling design
Our work was part of the joint Canada‐Alberta Oil Sands
Monitoring program (Roberts et al., 2022). Our design follows the
Terrestrial Biological Monitoring Program's before‐after dose–
response (BADR) framework, which measures ecological responses
to oil sands activities using two approaches: a before‐after
comparison of areas at different phases of oil sands development
(currently developed and not developed), and a dose–response
comparison along a gradient of disturbance (i.e., high and low)
(Bayne et al., 2021). We selected six landscape units (LUs), each
defined by hydrologic boundaries and spanning 700–1800 km2 with
a total sampling area of 6613 km2 (Figure 2). Each LU was assigned
