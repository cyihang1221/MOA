>Materials and methods   Strain and plant materials   The experimental strain, which was isolated from the root of  P. heterophylla , was identified as  T. crassum  and named WMM-1-7. It has been deposited in the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC), a publicly accessible culture repository, with the accession number M 20,242,688 20,241,202. The seeds utilized in this study were collected from the experimental plots managed by our team in Shibing County, Guizhou Province, China, with authorization from Professor Zhou Tao. The seedlings were cultivated from surface-sterilized seeds in sterile culture bottles. The voucher specimens have been deposited in the publicly accessible Herbarium of the School of Pharmacy, Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, with the accession number QZTM 0008973. The preserved strain was activated on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium at 28 °C before the cultivation experiments. After 3 days, an approximately 5 mm diameter mycelial plug was used to inoculate the fungus into potato dextrose broth (PDB) medium. The culture was grown at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 3 days. Spore suspensions were filtered using three layers of gauze to remove residual mycelium, and the spore concentration was determined using a Thoma chamber. Different concentrations of spore suspensions (L: 7.5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL; M: 3.75 × 10⁶ CFU/mL; H: 6.25 × 10⁶ CFU/mL) were subsequently added to MS liquid medium at a ratio of 1:50. We denoted CK, L, M, and H as the blank control, low-concentration fungal suspension, medium-concentration fungal suspension, and high-concentration fungal suspension, respectively.   Three uniform  P. heterophylla  seedings were also cultured in MS liquid medium. The  P. heterophylla  seedlings were derived from the same batch of tissue culture subcultures. The plants were maintained under strictly aseptic conditions to ensure the absence of any detectable microbial contaminants other than the applied  T. crassum . The plant materials were sterile tissue-cultured seedlings maintained through laboratory subculture, with their original maternal lines obtained via embryo dissection from surface-sterilized seeds. All operations were conducted within a UV- and ethanol-sterilized laminar flow hood, with tools sterilized by autoclaving and flame treatment to ensure aseptic conditions. The fungi and plants were cocultured for 14 days under 14 h of light at 22 °C/10 hours in the dark at 16 °C. After freeze-drying, the culture medium was prepared for sequencing at Major Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All the assays consisted of three technical sample replicates. Metabolite profiling identification was performed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Sample preparation and extraction   The MS medium from the root‒fungus coculture system without further processing was collected for metabolite extraction. Two hundred microliters of sample was transferred into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Afterward, 800 µL of extraction solution containing methanol: acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) was added and mixed. After vortexing the tube for 30 s and 30 min of low-temperature ultrasonic extraction (5 °C, 40 kHz), the sample was allowed to stand at − 20 °C for 30 min. Following centrifugation at 13,000 g at 4 °C for 15 min, the supernatant was dried under a nitrogen stream. After drying, 120 µL of acetonitrile–water (1:1) was added for reconstitution, followed by vortexing for 30 s; extraction was carried out by 5-min low-temperature ultrasonication (5 °C, 40 kHz). The suspension was centrifuged for 10 min at 13,000 g at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred into a glass vial for LC‒MS analysis. In addition, 20 µL of the supernatant from each sample was mixed as a quality control sample. LC‒MS conditions   Metabolite extracts were analyzed using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) mass spectrometry (MS) coupled with a Thermo Fisher Scientific Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer. The chromatographic column was an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA). Mobile phase A was 95% water + 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), and mobile phase B was 47.5% acetonitrile and 47.5% isopropyl alcohol + 5% water. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 3 µl. During detection, the QC samples were inserted between every 4 experimental samples to monitor the batches.   All mass spectra were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes using electrospray ionization. The following parameters were used: auxiliary gas heater temperature, 425 °C; capillary temperature, 325 °C; sheath gas flow rate, 50; auxiliary gas flow rate, 13; S-Lens voltage, 50 kV; and scanning range, 70–1050 m/z (Table S1). The total run time was 8 min. An internal standard method was employed in this study, and 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine, palmitoyl-L-carnitine (N-methyl-d3), cholic acid-2,2,4,4-d4 and DL-Phe-d5 were chosen as the internal standards. The detailed gradient elution programs for both positive and negative ionization modes are provided in Table S2. The flow rate was 0.40 mL/min. Qualitative and quantitative determination of metabolites   For qualitative analysis of the metabolites, the raw data were preprocessed using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA). The mass error tolerance for full-scan MS spectra was set to 10 ppm. The MS and MS/MS mass spectra information was matched with the self-built database and the mainstream public metabolic database ( http://www.hmdb.ca/ ,  https://metlin.scripps.edu/ ). The identification met level 2 (putatively identified compounds) according to MSI guidelines.   Metabolites were quantified in relation to the peak area of the internal standard. The peak area of each chromatographic peak represents the relative content of the corresponding metabolite. The raw data, comprising quality control samples, were imported into the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment. This process ultimately resulted in a data matrix characterized by the retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensity. To facilitate data analysis, a series of preprocessing steps were undertaken, primarily involving the simulation of missing values in the raw data and data normalization. Through these preprocessing steps, the influence of variations unrelated to the research objectives on data analysis can be minimized, thereby enhancing the screening and analysis of potential target differentially abundant metabolites. In data processing, preprocessing is systematically conducted through missing value filtering, missing value imputation, data normalization, QC validation, and data transformation. Missing values are imputed using the mean value, whereas data normalization is performed using the sum method to mitigate errors arising from sample handling, concentration differences, and instrument biases. Ions with a relative standard deviation (RSD) greater than 30% are excluded from the dataset, and only ions with an RSD of 30% or less are selected for differential quantitative analysis (Figure S1). The data undergo logarithmic transformation to improve the normality of the data structure. The qualitative and preprocessed quantitative information was ultimately integrated to obtain Table S3. Statistical analysis   One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted utilizing SPSS software version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with  P  < 0.05 serving as the defined threshold for determining statistical significance. The differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) between two biological groups were identified through integrated multivariate and univariate analyses, combining orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) with parametric (Student’s t test) and nonparametric (Mann-Whitney U test) methods. In the OPLS-DA analysis, variables with variable importance in projection (VIP) values exceeding 1 were deemed significant. To mitigate the risk of model overfitting, a method involving 200 permutation test was employed to assess the model’s fitting performance (Fig. S2). Significant variables were selected based on a  P  value < 0.05 and a VIP > 1.0, with the results visualized in a volcano plot. DAMs were identified using the Hunan Metabolome Database (HMDB), and a pie chart was constructed through the Meiji Bio-Information Cloud Platform. The DAMs were annotated with the KEGG database, and the annotated pathways were then mapped using the Major Bio-Information Cloud Platform. The column graphs were created using Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corp., MA, USA). Results   Plant–fungal symbiosis expands the rhizosphere metabolic diversity   A coculture assay (L: low-concentration fungal suspension, M: medium-concentration fungal suspension, H: high-concentration fungal suspension) was performed to determine whether  T. crassum  significantly increased the quantity of rhizosphere metabolites in  P. heterophylla  (Fig.  1 ). A previous study demonstrated that compared with the control treatment,  T. crassum  inoculation increased the number of metabolites in the culture medium by 48.3–53.6% [ 18 ]. These metabolites were classified into 16 categories in the HMDB. The major superclasses included organic acids and derivatives (346, 20.63%), organoheterocyclic compounds (346, 20.63%), lipids and lipid-like molecules (339, 20.21%), organic oxygen compounds (213, 12.70%), benzenoids (172, 10.26%), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (115, 6.86%), and nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogs (68, 4.05%). These functionally diverse metabolites may work together to regulate plant growth and development. Organoheterocyclic compounds such as alkaloids may be related to cadmium tolerance [ 19 ]. Soil heavy metal pollution is a global ecological and environmental issue that has garnered widespread attention. Low-molecular-weight organic acids help increase the tolerance and accumulation of lead in Centipedegrass [ 20 ]. Lipids and lipid molecules, such as energy sources, membrane construction, and signaling molecules, are crucial for life activities [ 21 ]. These three types of components account for more than 60% of all the detected metabolites.   Fig. 1. At least 12 categories of functional substances were detected in all three treatment groups (Tables  1 ,  2  and  3 ). Using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, we annotated the DAMs in each comparison group and performed enrichment analysis to identify associated pathways. The results revealed that the metabolic pathways differing between the treatment and control groups were enriched primarily in five major categories: metabolism (M), environmental information processing (EIP), organismal systems (OS), human diseases (HD), and cellular processes (CP).   The top 20 enriched pathways were selected based on the p value. In the L and CK groups, the DAMs were predominantly enriched in tryptophan metabolism, staurosporine biosynthesis, arginine biosynthesis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, beta-alanine metabolism, penicillin and cephalosporin biosynthesis (Fig.  3 A). In the M and CK groups, galactose metabolism was the most significantly enriched pathway. The DAMs were primarily associated with arginine biosynthesis, pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism, biosynthesis of terpenoids and steroids, and flavonoid biosynthesis (Fig.  3 B). In the H and CK groups, the DAMs were predominantly enriched in tryptophan metabolism, staurosporine biosynthesis, penicillin and cephalosporin biosynthesis, nucleotide metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism and lysine biosynthesis (Fig.  3 C). Some overlapping pathways, such as tryptophan metabolism, staurosporine biosynthesis and ABC transporters, were identified from the various comparisons. The enrichment of tryptophan metabolism might be due to the interference of the treatment group with the auxin biosynthesis pathway, affecting root growth and increasing plant resistance to various abiotic and biological stresses [ 23 ]. Staurosporine biosynthesis regulates cellular behavior by interfering with kinase activity [ 24 ]. ABC transporter proteins help plants cope with environmental stress by transporting plant hormones and heavy metals [ 25 ]. A detailed list of all significantly enriched pathways, filtered by adjusted p value and including the number of mapped metabolites, is provided in Supplementary Table S4. The synergistic effects of these pathways collectively constitute the mechanism of action of  T. crassum  on  P. heterophylla .   Fig. 3.
