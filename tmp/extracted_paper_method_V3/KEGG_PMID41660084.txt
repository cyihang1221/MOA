>Materials and Methods   Study participants and serum collection and processing   This study screens participants based on the World Health Organization (ICD-10) criteria and the Michigan Alcoholism Screening Test (MAST) ( Selzer, 1971 ). Group A (alcohol-dependent patients) inclusion criteria: aged 18–65 years; formally diagnosed with alcohol dependence; exhibiting strong alcohol cravings; experiencing withdrawal symptoms after cessation of drinking; demonstrating loss of control over drinking behavior; willing to cooperate with rehabilitation therapy and providing signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria: current participation in other clinical trials; poor compliance or incomplete data; failure to meet rehabilitation treatment standards; Comorbidities such as liver diseases, tumors, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome. After initial screening, liver function indicators (alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST),  etc. ) and the mean corpuscular volume (MCV) are evaluated. Individuals with abnormal results are excluded. Subsequently, carbohydrate-deficient transferrin (CDT) and phosphatidylethanol (PEth) levels were measured. A positive result in these tests is regarded as alcohol dependence. From January to June 2023, 20 cases were collected from the Third Hospital of Quanzhou. Following screening, seven eligible samples were included in Group A.   Group B (healthy volunteers) criteria: aged 18–65 years; no alcohol consumption or drinking habits within the past 30 days; signed informed consent provided. Exclusion criteria were the same as those for Group A. Blood samples were obtained from individuals with normal liver function and within the normal range for mean corpuscular volume (MCV), along with negative PEth and CDT test results, to meet the inclusion requirements. Four healthy adults were included.   Peripheral blood samples were collected from all participants and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses.   The study was conducted in strict accordance with relevant ethical guidelines. Ethical approval for this biomedical research was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Ningde Normal University (Review No.: NDNU-LL-202101, Approval Date: March 4, 2021). Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants. Mass spectrometry experimental methodology   Low-abundance protein enrichment and peptide digestion   For each sample, 100 µL of serum was subjected to low-abundance protein enrichment using magnetic bead-based technology. Specifically, 100 µL of serum was mixed with four µL of pre-washed magnetic beads (washed twice with wash buffer) and incubated for 2 h. Following incubation, the mixture was placed on a magnetic rack to separate the serum from the beads. The serum was then discarded, and the beads were washed five times using a wash buffer, with each wash consisting of a 5-minute inversion and mixing step. All samples were subsequently digested using trypsin in solution ( Blume et al., 2020 ). After digestion, the peptides from all samples were desalted using a C18 cartridge. The desalted peptides were lyophilized and reconstituted in 20 µL of 0.1% formic acid solution. The peptide concentration in each sample was determined by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm (OD280). Equal amounts of peptides from each sample were pooled to create a composite peptide sample, which was then subjected to reverse-phase (RP) fractionation. The RP-fractionated samples, along with the digested peptides from each individual sample, were spiked with a suitable amount of indexed retention time (iRT) standard peptides before being analyzed by DIA mass spectrometry. Mass spectrometry method   The DIA analysis was performed using the Evosep One system, operating at a nanoflow rate for chromatographic separation. After separation through nanoflow high-performance liquid chromatography (nano-HPLC), the samples were analyzed using a timsTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker) under DIA mode. The analysis was conducted in positive ion mode, with both MS and MS/MS spectra recorded within the mass-to-charge (m/z) range of 100 to 1,700. For MS2 analysis, the DIA data acquisition mode was employed, utilizing four TIMS scan windows. Each window had an accumulation time of 100 ms. In Parallel Accumulation Serial Fragmentation (PASEF) mode, the collision energy varied linearly with ion mobility (1/K0), with collision energies ranging from 20 to 59 eV corresponding to ion mobilities of 1/K0 between 0.85 and 1.30 Vs/cm 2 . Data analysis   The DIA data were processed using Spectronaut software (version 16). The database used for analysis was the same as the one employed for the library construction. The following parameters were set in the software: the retention time prediction type was configured as dynamic iRT, MS2-level interference correction was enabled, and cross-run normalization was activated. All results were filtered based on a Q value cutoff of 0.01, corresponding to a false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 1%. Bioinformatics analysis   Protein clustering analysis   The quantitative data of the target protein set were first normalized to a range of (−1, 1). Subsequently, hierarchical clustering was performed on both the sample and protein expression data using the ComplexHeatmap R package (R version 3.4). The distance metric employed was Euclidean, and the linkage method used was average linkage. A heatmap was generated to visualize the clustering results. For further analysis of the target protein set, the data were normalized, and fuzzy c-means (FCM) clustering was performed using the Mfuzz software. This algorithm allowed for the identification of distinct expression modules based on the trends observed in protein expression, thereby generating different clusters. Subcellular localization analysis   We used the CELLO method ( http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw ) to predict the subcellular localization of proteins. The method uses a type of machine learning called a multi-class support vector machine (SVM), which learns from known protein sequences and their subcellular localization information found in public databases. We used the trained model to determine each protein’s subcellular localization. Protein domain analysis   We conducted protein domain analysis using the Pfam database, which contains multiple sequence alignments and hidden Markov models for protein families. This database includes information on protein domains. The InterProScan software package performed functional characterization of the protein sequences during the analysis. The integrated scanning algorithm from InterPro used the Pfam database to identify the functions of protein domains. GO functional annotation   Blast2GO allowed for the functional annotation of the target protein set  via  its specialized protocol. This process involved four main steps: The analysis included matching sequences using BLAST, then getting GO terms by mapping, followed by a step for GO annotation, and finally adding extra information with InterProScan to enhance the annotations. These steps resulted in the comprehensive functional characterization of proteins through the examination of their corresponding GO terms. KEGG pathway annotation   The target protein set was labeled with KEGG pathway information using the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS). We used this software to link protein sequences to known biological pathways and to categorize their functions using information from the KEGG database. Users uploaded protein sequences to KAAS, which matched them with KEGG pathways by aligning the sequences and assigning them to the right pathways. The chosen method allowed researchers to determine which biological processes and metabolic pathways connected with the target proteins, revealing insights into their cellular functions. Enrichment analysis   The study analysed how certain GO categories, KEGG pathways, and protein domains are spread out in the target protein set compared to the full protein dataset. The study used Fisher’s exact test for statistical analysis to determine which functional categories or pathways showed significant enrichment. This study measured how often each GO term, along with KEGG pathways and protein domains, appeared in the target protein set against the reference protein set. The enrichment analysis revealed biological functions and pathways along with domains that showed overrepresentation in target proteins, which helped us understand their biological roles. Protein-protein interaction network analysis   Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) among the target proteins were identified using data from the IntAct ( http://www.ebi.ac.uk/intact/main.xhtml ) and STRING ( http://string-db.org/ ) databases. Users can find data about direct and indirect protein interactions within these databases. We used Cytoscape software (version 3.2.1) to visualize and analyze interaction data, which enabled them to build an interaction network for the target proteins. We looked at the network to show how the proteins are related and to identify important areas or groups that represent key biological functions or categories. Principal component analysis (PCA)   The study utilized PCA for data dimensionality reduction while simultaneously discovering patterns. We utilized the R programming language to perform the data analysis. Pareto scaling standardized the dataset before PCA by dividing each variable by its standard deviation’s square root. The scaling method reduces the impact of wide-ranging data variations but maintains essential features that aid pattern recognition. The multidimensional statistical analysis performed after preprocessing helped reveal the principal components that account for most of the data variance, visualize sample relationships, and uncover data structure. Differential database analysis   To identify outliers and minimize their impact on the accuracy of the results, Z-score analysis was conducted. Values with a Z-score greater than 3.0 or less than −3.0 were considered outliers and subsequently excluded from further analysis. To compare the expression levels of the SNRPB protein between tumor and normal groups within the liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) dataset, the Wilcoxon test was employed. Additionally, differential expression analysis was performed between high and low SNRPB expression groups. The validation of SNRPB expression was conducted across multiple datasets, including E_TABM_36,  GSE14520 ,  GSE39791 , and  GSE144269 . Clinical data validation   Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using the survival package in R. The optimal cut-off values for SNRPB expression and the high- and low-expression cohorts were determined using the survminer package, ensuring that the minimum proportion of the high- and low-expression groups was not below 0.3. We conducted a log-rank test using the survfit function to assess the statistical significance between high and low expression groups. Patients were stratified into four quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4) based on SNRPB gene expression levels, with Q1 representing the top 25% of expression and Q4 representing the lowest 25%. Chi-square tests were applied to assess significant differences between the composition of these groups. To evaluate the link between SNRPB expression and survival results, a simple Cox survival analysis was done using the inverse variance method for meta-analysis, with the log hazard ratio (HR) as the main measure. We categorized HR values as either less than 1 or greater than 1, signifying tumor-suppressive or tumor-promoting functions, respectively. We conducted statistical analysis and visualization using the Meta package in R (version 4.3.2). Additionally, we conducted a univariate Cox survival analysis using the survival package to evaluate the relationship between the gene of interest and traditional clinical variables. We described the relative risk using the HR and 95% confidence interval (CI), and the forestplotter package facilitated the visualization. Since continuous variables might affect risk in a non-straightforward way, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to analyze these possible non-linear connections. This method was used to examine the link between SNRPB expression and four survival outcomes: overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-free interval (DFI), in order to determine whether the relationship was non-linear. Enrichment analysis   The top 30% of samples based on gene expression levels formed the high-expression group, and the bottom 30% of samples became the low-expression group. The Limma package conducted differential analysis to determine the log2 fold change (log2FC) values for each gene. The genes were arranged in order according to the values of their log2 fold changes. The analysis team executed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) with the clusterProfiler package and analyzed both hallmark gene sets and KEGG metabolic gene sets. The analysis generated enrichment scores (ES) for each gene set while conducting significance testing and applying multiple hypothesis corrections. A bubble plot was used for visualization. Visualization analysis was performed on the five most enriched pathways in the high- and low-expression groups. Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the fgsea function from the fgsea package, based on the KEGG gene set. The ES for each gene set was calculated, and significance testing along with multiple hypothesis correction were performed. The CancerSEA database, which organizes 14 different functional states of cancer cells, was used to assess the activity of specific pathways based on the expression of characteristic genes. The Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) package in R, using the z-score parameter, was employed to calculate the activity of 14 functional state gene sets, yielding combined z-scores. The gene set scores were standardized using the scale function and subsequently calculated for Pearson correlations between individual genes and each gene set score. Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package with multiple gene sets, including Gene Ontology—Biological Process (GO-BP), Gene Ontology—Molecular Function (GO-MF), Gene Ontology—Cellular Component (GO-CC), Reactome, and Wikipathways gene sets. The ES for each gene set was calculated, and significance testing along with multiple hypothesis correction were performed. The top five pathways significantly enriched in both the high- and low-expression groups were selected for visualization. Immune infiltration analysis   Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between SNRPB expression and Tumor Immunophenotype (TIP) scores, as well as the self-correlation of TIP scores. The linkET package was used for visualization of these correlations. Data from the TISIDB database ( http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/download.php ) were utilized, which includes information on immunomodulators, chemokines, immune-stimulating genes, immune-suppressing genes, chemokines, and human leukocyte antigens. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon test to compare the immune-related molecules between high and low SNRPB expression groups. The average expression of each gene in both groups was visualized using a heatmap. Patients were divided into four groups (Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4) based on quartiles of SNRPB gene expression. The heatmap visualized the average immune response scores and genomic state scores for each group, with the rows standardized to ensure that each score was scaled to the same range. This allowed for a comparison of the immune reaction scores across the SNRPB expression quartiles. The publicly accessible TIMER2.0 database provided immune infiltration data for all The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) samples to maintain data quality and consistency. Several algorithms helped to evaluate immune cell content and its connection to gene expression patterns. The study presented correlation coefficients from various algorithms through a heatmap that displayed how different cell types related to gene expression. We created scatter plots to demonstrate the relationships between immune cell types and gene expression clearly. Microbial analysis   The Cancer Microbiome Atlas (TCMA) database supplied microbial abundance data through comprehensive abundance matrices for multiple sample types. We used Spearman correlation analysis to evaluate the connection between microbial abundance and gene expression levels in patients. Through this analysis, we were able to evaluate possible links between specific microbial presence or abundance levels and particular gene expression measurements. This analysis showed how microbial communities might influence gene expression patterns within the patient group to reveal the microbiome’s impact on health or disease mechanisms. Results   Comprehensive proteomic profiling of serum proteins in alcohol dependence patients and healthy controls   DIA proteomics found 1,249 proteins present in alcohol dependence patients and 1,020 proteins in healthy controls. Among them, 996 proteins were shared between alcohol dependence patients and healthy controls ( Fig. 1A ). Overall, 277 proteins were identified as potential biomarkers differentiating alcohol dependence patients from healthy individuals. A bar chart analysis revealed 195 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between the two groups, of which 99 were upregulated and 96 downregulated in alcohol dependence patients compared with healthy controls ( Fig. 1B ). GO enrichment analysis showed that the DEPs were involved in various biological processes, including cellular metabolic processes, biological regulation, response to stimuli, and metabolic processes, with molecular functions primarily related to binding and catalytic activity. The cellular components involved were mainly the cell, cell part, organelle, and extracellular region ( Fig. 2A ). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified that upregulated pathways in alcohol dependence were associated with ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, alcohol intoxication, viral protein interactions with cytokines and cytokine receptors, and ribosome function, while downregulated pathways were involved in porphyrin metabolism, glycan degradation, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis, GlycosylPhosphatidylInositol (GPI)—anchor biosynthesis, and biotin metabolism ( Fig. 2B ). Notably, five protein-coding genes related to the alcohol intoxication pathway were significantly upregulated, including H4C16, H2BC12, MACROH2A1, H2AC13, and H2AC21, with H4C16 and H2BC12 among the top 10 most upregulated proteins. Hierarchical clustering of DEPs revealed distinct clustering of alcohol dependence and control samples, with clear separation based on protein expression profiles ( Fig. 3 ). Among the proteins identified, SNRPB was notably upregulated in alcohol dependence. The heatmap further confirmed consistent clustering of upregulated and downregulated proteins within each group. Overall, these results highlight significant differences in protein expression between alcohol dependence patients and healthy controls, providing valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms of alcohol dependence and potential biomarkers for further investigation. Upregulation of SNRPB in LIHC and its association with poor prognosis   The analysis of SNRPB expression in LIHC demonstrated significant upregulation of this protein in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues. In the TCGA LIHC dataset, a volcano plot analysis revealed that SNRPB was significantly overexpressed in the tumor group ( Fig. 4A ). This finding was further validated in several independent datasets, including  GSE14520 ,  GSE39791 , and  GSE144269 , where SNRPB expression was consistently higher in tumor samples compared to normal samples ( Fig. 4B ). The diagnostic performance of SNRPB expression was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which showed high accuracy in distinguishing tumor samples from normal tissues ( Fig. 5A ). Additionally, calibration curves further confirmed the reliability of SNRPB expression as a potential biomarker for tumor classification ( Fig. 5B ). Survival analysis revealed that higher SNRPB expression was significantly associated with worse prognosis in LIHC patients. Kaplan–Meier survival curves indicated that patients with high SNRPB expression in the highest quartile (Q4) had significantly poorer overall survival (OS), disease-free interval (DFI), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-specific survival (DSS) compared to patients in the lowest quartile (Q1) ( Figs. 5C – 5F ). These findings were consistent across multiple datasets, as a meta-analysis of univariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that SNRPB expression was a significant risk factor for poor survival outcomes in LIHC ( Fig. 5G ).
