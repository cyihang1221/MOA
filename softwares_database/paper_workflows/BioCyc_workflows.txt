related_tool: BioCyc
sample_description: The study used human erythrocytes infected with the Plasmodium falciparum 3D7 strain (IRBC) and non-infected red blood cells (NRBC) as biological samples. Four separate biological replicate cultures (0.5 mL each) were prepared for each of three pH-adjusted solvent extraction conditions (pH 2, 7, and 9), resulting in 48 total samples equally distributed across four experimental conditions: (1) NRBC, SLO untreated; (2) NRBC, SLO treated; (3) IRBC, SLO untreated; and (4) IRBC, SLO treated. SLO (streptolysin O, 250 units/mL) was applied for 30 min at 37°C to selectively permeabilize NRBCs without lysis, thereby enriching metabolites from IRBCs. Asynchronous P. falciparum cultures were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 25 mM HEPES, 20 mM sodium bicarbonate, 370 µM hypoxanthine, and 0.5% AlbuMAX at 37°C under 90% N2, 5% O2, and 5% CO2 until parasitemia reached ~10%. Human blood samples were collected under protocols approved by the University of Hawaii’s Human Use Research Committee and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with written informed consent obtained from all subjects.
complete_workflow_description: The metabolomics workflow involved multiple integrated software tools and analytical procedures. Untargeted data acquisition was performed using Agilent MassHunter™ Acquisition B.03.01 software on an Agilent 6520 Q-TOF LC/MS system. Feature detection and deconvolution were conducted using Agilent MassHunter™ Qualitative Analysis B.03.01 with two algorithms: (1) Molecular Feature Extractor (MFE), an untargeted algorithm identifying co-variant ions per neutral mass, RT, and abundance; and (2) 'Find by Formula' (FbF), a targeted algorithm using a custom malaria-specific Personal Compound Database and Library (PCDL) built from the MPMP database and containing literature-derived elemental formulas. GC/MS data were processed using AMDIS (NIST) spectral deconvolution and Agilent 'Find by Chromatographic Deconvolution' software, with compound identification leveraging the Agilent-Fiehn GC/MS Metabolomics RTL Library. Data filtering, statistical analysis, and visualization were performed in Mass Profiler Professional (MPP) v12.5: projects were structured by extraction pH, ion mode (ESI+/ESI−/APCI+), and polarity; features were binned, aligned, log2-transformed, baselined to zero, and filtered by relative frequency (Rel Freq ≥75% or 100%) and coefficient of variation (CV <100%). Statistical testing included Welch’s unpaired t-test, one-way ANOVA with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (5%), and Tukey’s post-hoc test. Metabolite identification employed accurate mass (<5 ppm), isotopic pattern matching, retention time (RT), and MS/MS spectral library matching against METLIN PCDL (containing ~2000 standards) using Agilent Identification Browser and targeted Q-TOF MS/MS at collision energies of 10, 20, and 40 eV.

========== paper_workflow entry 3 ==========
related_tool: BioCyc
sample_description: The sample used in the research was the wild-type Bacillus thuringiensis strain 4.0718 (CCTCC no. M200016), isolated from soil in south China. This strain produces two types of parasporal crystals—bipyramidal and cuboidal. Cultivation involved activation on LB solid medium (10.0 g/liter tryptone, 5 g/liter yeast extract, 10 g/liter NaCl, 1.5 g/liter agar) at 30°C for 12 h, followed by transfer into 20 ml of fermentation medium in a 250-ml baffled flask and incubation on a rotary shaker at 30°C and 220 rpm. The fermentation medium (pH 7.3) contained beef extract (5.0 g/liter), NaCl (2.0 g/liter), MnSO₄ (0.05 g/liter), tryptone (10.0 g/liter), MgSO₄·7H₂O (0.3 g/liter), glucose (3.0 g/liter), and K₂HPO₄ (0.3 g/liter). Two independent biological replicates were prepared using the same seed batch and cultured simultaneously in the same incubator. Cells were harvested at three time points—10 h (middle vegetative phase, T₁), 20 h (early sporulation phase, T₂), and 32 h (late sporulation phase, T₃)—for protein extraction and analysis. Cell morphology and developmental stage were confirmed via phase-contrast microscopy (Olympus, Japan) at ×1,000 magnification.
complete_workflow_description: The process procedure involved a multi-step proteomic workflow: (1) Whole-cell protein extraction from B. thuringiensis cells harvested at 10, 20, and 32 h — cells were washed with chilled PBS (10 mM, pH 7.8), flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground to powder, lysed in buffer (8 M urea, 4% CHAPS, 40 mM Tris, 65 mM DTT, protease inhibitor cocktail) at 4°C for 1 h, centrifuged (4°C, 13,200 rpm, 30 min), and precipitated overnight with ice-cold acetone containing 10% trichloroacetic acid at −20°C; pellets were lyophilized and stored at −40°C. (2) Protein digestion — pellets were dissolved in reducing solution (6 M guanidine hydrochloride, 100 mM Tris pH 8.3); protein concentration was determined using the 2-D Quant kit (Amersham Biosciences); ~300 μg protein was adjusted to 3 μg/μl, reduced with 1 μl of 1 M DTT at 37°C for 2.5 h, alkylated with 5 μl of 1 M iodoacetamide in the dark at RT for 40 min, precipitated with ice-cold ACN-acetone-acetic acid (50:50:0.1 vol/vol/vol) for >12 h, washed thrice with ice-cold ethanol, resuspended in 100 μl of 50 mM NH₄HCO₃, and digested with trypsin at 1:100 (wt/wt) ratio for 4 h at 37°C with shaking, followed by additional trypsin at 1:50 (wt/wt) ratio overnight at 37°C; digestion was quenched with formic acid and samples were ultrafiltered using 10-kDa Microcon filtration devices (Sartorius, Germany) and lyophilized. (3) 2D nano-LC-MS/MS analysis — performed on an LTQ XL mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher) coupled with two Finnigan quaternary pumps and an autosampler (LC Packings), equipped with a homemade nano-ionization source; first-dimension separation used a BioBasic SCX column (0.32 mm × 100 mm, 5 μm) with 12-step ammonium chloride elution (25–1000 mM, 4% ACN, 0.1% FA); peptides were trapped alternately on two Zorbax enrichment columns (0.3 mm × 5 mm, 5 μm) and subjected to second-dimension reversed-phase separation on a BioBasic-C18 column (0.1 mm × 150 mm, 5 μm) with gradient elution (1 min 100% buffer A [5% ACN, 0.1% FA], 70-min gradient to 65% buffer B [0.1% FA in ACN], 20-min to 80% buffer B, 5 min at 80% buffer B, 1-min back to 100% buffer A, 12-min reequilibration); flow rate was 200 nl/min; electrospray voltage was 2.0 kV; heated capillary temperature was 180°C; capillary voltage was 4.5 kV; MS/MS data were acquired in data-dependent mode scanning m/z 300–2000 for full MS and selecting top 10 most intense ions for CID fragmentation at normalized collision energy 35%; dynamic exclusion was set to repeat count 2, repeat duration 30 s, exclusion duration 90 s; automatic gain control regulated ion trap fill. (4) In-house database search using SEQUEST (integrated in Proteome Discoverer 1.1 software package) — searched against a custom FASTA database built from UniProt Knowledgebase (Swiss-Prot + TrEMBL) containing all B. thuringiensis subspecies (116,723 entries) plus reversed decoy sequences; search parameters: precursor ion tolerance = 1.5 Da, fragment ion tolerance = 1.0 Da; up to two missed cleavages allowed; only b/y ions considered; static modification: carbamidomethyl (C); variable modifications: oxidation (M); target-decoy FDR threshold ≤0.01; results validated manually; protein redundancy resolved by assigning shared peptides to the highest-scoring protein entry, except for isoforms from the same subspecies which were all retained. (5) Bioinformatic analysis — functional classification using UniProt annotations, BioCyc, and KEGG databases; BLASTP queries against UniProt for unknown-function proteins; ClustalW alignment for signature identification; theoretical pI and molecular mass calculated using Expasy’s PI tool. (6) Validation by Western blot — CotJc and glutamine synthetase proteins were selected; genes cotJc and glnA were cloned into pET30a vector, expressed in E. coli BL21, purified via HisTrap FF column on ÄKTA purifier 100 system (GE Healthcare), and used to generate rabbit antisera; whole-cell proteins from T₁, T₂, T₃ were separated on NuPAGE 12% Bis-Tris gels, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked, probed with primary antisera (1:1000 dilution), detected with DyLight 680-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:10,000), and visualized using the Odyssey infrared imaging system (Li-COR Biosciences).

related_tool: BioCyc
sample_description: Urine samples were collected from 11 irradiated C57BL/6 male mice (8–10 weeks old) 24 hours after whole-body exposure to a single 8 Gy dose of gamma radiation, and from 10 unexposed age- and strain-matched control mice. Samples were stored at −80°C and analyzed by UPLC–TOF MS in positive ionization mode. Data preprocessing was performed using MarkerLynx, and samples were normalized to creatinine levels. Only positive-mode data were used, resulting in a dataset of partial- and complete-presence ions (1177 and 765, respectively), filtered with a zeros threshold (Zthr) of 0.90.
complete_workflow_description: MetaboLyzer implements an end-to-end statistical workflow for postprocessed LC-MS metabolomics data. The workflow begins with input of a feature table (e.g., ion abundances per sample), followed by optional data transformation (log or inverse hyperbolic sine) and novel Gaussian normalization applied sample-wise on log-transformed data. Next, zero-filtering is performed using a user-defined zeros threshold (Zthr = 0.90 in the demonstration). Outlier detection is applied using 1.5×IQR filtering. Statistical testing is then conducted separately for partial-presence ions (using Fisher’s exact test) and complete-presence ions (using Mann–Whitney U test), both with p < 0.05 significance threshold. Significant ions undergo putative identification via integration of four databases: KEGG, HMDB, Lipid Maps, and BioCyc. Finally, results are visualized through heatmaps (with Euclidean hierarchical clustering), volcano plots, density plots, correlation maps, and metabolic pathway hit histograms. The entire pipeline is implemented in Python, leveraging SciPy, RPy2, Matplotlib, and R packages including Kernlab (for kernel PCA), gplots (for heatmaps), fastICA, and MASS.

complete_workflow_description: The global metabolomics workflow began with apoplastic wash fluid extraction from 10-week-old Lolium perenne cv Samson plants (endophyte-free controls vs. Epichloë festucae Fl1-infected). Samples underwent dual-phase (polar and nonpolar) solvent extraction, followed by nontargeted LC-HR-ESI-MS analysis in both positive and negative ion modes. Raw data were processed using MarkerLynx XS for peak detection, deconvolution, and alignment, generating four feature matrices (polar+/−, nonpolar+/−). These matrices were imported into MarVis for downstream processing: adduct correction, merging, ANOVA with Benjamini–Hochberg multiple testing correction (FDR < 0.003), and filtering to yield 203 high-confidence metabolite features. MarVis-cluster performed 1D self-organizing map (SOM) clustering to visualize intensity patterns across sample groups. Finally, MarVis-pathway conducted automated metabolite annotation using accurate mass queries against three databases: KEGG, BioCyc, and an in-house Epichloë secondary metabolite database, supported by metabolite set enrichment analysis. Candidate novel metabolites—including a Leu/Ile glycoside—were prioritized via comparative MS analysis of wild-type vs. lgsA deletion mutant-infected plant apoplasts, followed by tandem MS and genetic validation.

complete_workflow_description: The paper describes an end-to-end computational workflow for mapping chemical compound libraries onto genome-scale metabolic networks. The workflow begins with a metabolite library containing compounds annotated with InChI or InChIKey identifiers (e.g., MetaboHub's PeakForest and Glasgow Polyomics libraries). These identifiers are programmatically submitted via web services to the MetExplore metabolic network repository. Prior to mapping, MetExplore augments network metabolites with structural identifiers by querying external services — specifically Chemical Translation Service (CTS) and UniChem — to retrieve ChEBI, KEGG, and PubChem IDs from common metabolite names, then infers InChIs/InChIKeys where possible. The core mapping step uses InChIKey-based matching (by default, only the first block of the 27-character InChIKey) against the augmented network metabolite set via a dedicated RESTful web service endpoint (http://metexplore.toulouse.inra.fr:8080/metExploreWebService/mapping/launch/inchikey). Results include quantitative coverage statistics (e.g., percentage of library compounds matched to the network) and network-contextual visualization within MetExplore. The architecture follows a Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) model enabling remote, programmatic, and up-to-date interaction without local database mirroring. While InChI layer-aware comparison is implemented in MetExplore for fine-grained matching, the default production service relies on InChIKey hashing for efficiency and URL compatibility.
source_paper: Benjamin et al. (2016), Frontiers in molecular biosciences, DOI: 10.3389/fmolb.2016.00002, PMID: 26909353
