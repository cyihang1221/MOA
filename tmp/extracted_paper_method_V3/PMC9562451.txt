</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abs0010"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>In Southeast Asian countries, nitrosamine compounds and the liver fluke <em>Opisthorchis viverrini</em> have long been identified as carcinogens for cholangiocarcinoma (CHCA). In order to effectively treat <em>O. viverrini</em> infections and prevent the development of CHCA, methods for disease detection are needed. This study aims to identify biomarkers for <em>O. viverrini</em> infection and CHCA. In the discovery phase, technical triplicates of five pooled plasma pools (10 plasma each) of healthy control subjects (noOVCCA), <em>O. viverrini</em> subjects (OV), and cholangiocarcinoma subjects (CCA), underwent solution-based digestion, with the label-free method, using a Thermo Scientific™ Q Exactive™ HF hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer and UltiMate 300 LC systems. The noOVCCA, OV, and CCA groups demonstrated different profiles and were clustered, as illustrated by PCA and heat map analysis. The STRING and reactome analysis showed that both OV and CCA groups up-regulated proteins targeting immune system-related proteins. Differential proteomic profiles, S100A9, and polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (PIGR) were specifically expressed in the CCA group. During the validation phase, another 50 plasma samples were validated via the PIGR sandwich ELISA. Using PIGR &gt;1.559 ng/ml as a cut-off point, 78.00% sensitivity, 71.00% specificity, and AUC = 0.8216, were obtained. It is sufficient to differentially diagnose cholangiocarcinoma patients from healthy patients and those with <em>Opisthorchiasis viverrini</em>. Hence, in this study, PIGR was identified and validated as a potential biomarker for CHCA. Plasma PIGR is suggested for screening CHCA, especially in an endemic region of <em>O. viverrini</em> infection.</p>
<section id="kwrds0010" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Plasma proteome, LC-MS/MS, Cholangiocarcinoma, <em>Opisthorchis viverrini</em>, S100A9, PIGR</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abs0015"><hr class="headless">
<p>plasma proteome; LC-MS/MS; Cholangiocarcinoma; <em>Opisthorchis viverrini</em>; S100A9; PIGR.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Significance of the study</h2>
<p id="p0010">By differentiating the proteomic profiles of healthy control subjects, <em>Opisthorchiasis viverrini</em> subjects, and cholangiocarcinoma subjects, the S100A9 and polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (PIGR) were identified as potential biomarkers for cholangiocarcinoma. The plasma PIGR concentration was further validated for its potential as a diagnostic biomarker. The results demonstrate that the plasma PIGR concentration was significantly higher in cholangiocarcinoma subjects than in patients only infected with <em>O. viverrini</em>, as well as control subjects (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05). The sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were obtained. This suggests plasma PIGR is a potential biomarker for screening cholangiocarcinoma, especially in an endemic area of <em>O. viverrini</em>.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0015">Cholangiocarcinoma (CHCA), a bile duct cancer, is a nefarious disease due to its initial asymptomatic nature, resulting in a high mortality rate (<a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4">Banales et al., 2020</a>). Globally, CHCA is an uncommon disease; high prevalence and incidence rates have been reported in Southeast Asian countries, including Thailand, owing to the endemic liver fluke <em>Opisthorchis viverrini</em> (<a href="#bib52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib52">Steele et al., 2018</a>). Infections due to <em>O. viverrini</em> and its sister taxa <em>O. felineus</em>, as well as <em>Clonorchis sinensis</em>, have been verified as carcinogens for CHCA (<a href="#bib22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib22">IARC, 1994</a>). In non-endemic areas, however, primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), chronic inflammation, is a premalignant condition that leads to CHCA (<a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4">Banales et al., 2020</a>). <em>O. viverrini</em> infections are present in raw or undercooked fishes containing the parasite’s metacercariae. Chronic infections with <em>O. viverrini</em> result in DNA damage due to reactive oxygen species, inflammation, accumulated mutations, and, ultimately, CHCA (<a href="#bib42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib42">Pinlaor et al., 2003</a>, <a href="#bib43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib43">2009</a>; <a href="#bib44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib44">Prakobwong et al., 2011</a>). To prevent CHCA, it is vital that <em>O. viverrini</em> infections be diagnosed early and treated with the antihelminthic drug praziquantel. Furthermore, the early detection of CHCA in its asymptomatic stages allows for better chemotherapeutic response and overall survival. Detecting host-responsive proteins may help detect <em>O. viverrini</em> infections and/or early-stage CHCA. To identify specific proteins for use as biomarkers, the proteomics-based LC-MS/MS approach was applied. In this study, differential plasma proteomic profiles of non-<em>O. viverrini</em> infection and non-cholangiocarcinoma subjects (noOVCCA), <em>O. viverrini</em> subjects (OV), and cholangiocarcinoma subjects (CCA) were investigated. The Thermo Scientific™ Q Exactive™ HF hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer was used to identify the potential biomarkers for <em>O. viverrini</em> infections and CHCA.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="sec3.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Chemicals and reagents</h3>
<p id="p0020">Trypsin was obtained from Promega (Promega Co., Madison, WI, USA). UV-Vis spectroscopy was performed using the Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek Corp., HT, USA). Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), acetonitrile (analytical grade), acetic acid (glacial), acetone (AR), HEPES sodium salt hydrate, potassium hydroxide (KOH), ammonium bicarbonate, Tris HCl, and formic acid (LC-MS grade) were all obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. An iodoacetamide (IAA) and dithiothreitol (DTT) were purchased from GE Healthcare. RapidGest SF Surfactant was obtained from Waters. The acetonitrile (ultra-LC-MS) and LC-MS-grade water were purchased from J.T. Baker (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Loughborough, UK).</p></section><section id="sec3.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Subjects</h3>
<p id="p0025">The protocol of this study was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of Udonthani Cancer Hospital, Udon Thani, Ministry of Public Health, Thailand, protocol number UCH-CT 11/2563. The fecal examination for <em>O. viverrini</em> eggs, liver ultrasonography, and histopathology were used to classify subjects. During the discovery phase, 50 subjects of each group were analyzed using LC-MS/MS-based proteomics. During the validation phase, another 50 subjects in each group were analyzed using the sandwich ELISA method. The subjects were divided into three groups based on their health statuses. Subjects with a normal physical examination, no liver enlargement or jaundice, and negative for <em>O. viverrini</em> eggs on fecal examination were assigned to the healthy control group (noOVCCA). Subjects positive for <em>O. viverrini</em> eggs on fecal examination with normal liver by ultrasonography were recruited to the <em>O. viverrini</em>-infected group (OV). Subjects with hepatomegaly and/or jaundice on physical examination, abnormal liver ultrasonography, and CHCA confirmed on liver tissue histopathology were assigned to the cholangiocarcinoma group (CCA). Subjects in the noOVCCA and CCA groups were recruited from Udonthani Cancer Hospital, Udon Thani, Thailand. Subjects in the OV group were recruited during the annual health check-ups at health promotion hospitals in Nong Khai province, Thailand. All subjects in the OV group were asymptomatic with low intensity of infection or light infection (&lt;1000 EPG) (<a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7">Boondit et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#bib32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib32">Maleewong et al., 1992</a>). Recruitment criteria, group allocation, and a summary of the clinical statuses of subjects are summarized in <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a> and <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/tbl1/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="sec3.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3. Blood collection and plasma preparation</h3>
<p id="p0030">Blood was collected from the median cubital vein using a 21 G needle. EDTA was used as an anti-coagulant. Plasma was collected by mixing by inverting the blood before spinning at 3000x <em>g</em> for 10 min at room temperature. The plasma was then collected and aliquoted to avoid freeze–thaw cycles and kept at −80 °C until used.</p></section><section id="sec3.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.4. Sample preparation for gel-free-based proteomes</h3>
<p id="p0035">A triplicate of 5 pooled plasma pools (10 plasma each) of each group (technical triplicate of 5 biological replicates) was used in this study (Supplementary File 1). Protein concentrations of plasma were measured using Lowry’s method (<em>DC</em> Protein Assay, Bio-Rad, USA) with bovine serum albumin as the standard reference (2–10 μg/μL). The pooled plasma was prepared by pooling equal amounts of protein from each subject (100 μg each). For each group, the 5 pooled plasma samples were prepared by pooling 10 individual plasma samples each. Finally, 5 pooled plasma samples/group of all 3 groups were prepared (15 pooled plasma samples in total). The plasma proteins were prepared and analyzed as previously described (<a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25">Krobthong et al., 2021</a>) with minor modifications. Briefly, 50 μL of pooled plasma proteins were mixed with 50 μL of fresh lysis buffer solution (0.2% SDS, 20 mM DTT, in 10 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 8.0). The protein solution was precipitated by using 500 μL of cold acetone at −20 °C for 24 h. The protein pellet was collected and reconstituted in 0.2% RapidGest SF (Waters Co., UK) in 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate. The protein concentration was re-measured, and 25 μg of protein was then subjected to trypsin digestion with the following protocol with minor modifications. Briefly, 5 mM DTT was used to reduce proteins by incubating at 60 °C for 20 min. Alkylation of the reduced proteins was conducted using 20 mM IAA for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The alkylated proteins were cleaned up to remove the remaining DTT and IAA by a 7 kDa molecular weight cut-off (Zeba™ Spin Desalting Columns, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Proteolytic digestion was performed using 500 ng of trypsin (Promega, Germany) and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. The tryptic peptides were dried, resolubilized in 0.1 % formic acid, and objected to a TruView LC-MS vial (Waters).</p></section><section id="sec3.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.5. LC-MS/MS setting for protein identification and quantification</h3>
<p id="p0040">Proteomics data analysis was conducted in data-dependent mode using LC-MS/MS, Thermo Scientific™ Q Exactive™ HF hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer, and an UltiMate 3000 LC system. The tryptic peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS with minor modification (<a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26">Krobthong et al., 2022</a>). Briefly, the tryptic peptides were desalted (C18 PepMap-100 trapping column) and resolved by C18 PepMap™ capillary column (25 cm long) with a 70 min long gradient of acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 0.3 μL/min. The MS spectra were acquired under the following conditions: <em>m/z</em> 400–1600, AGC target set at 3 × 10<sup>6</sup> ions, and a 60 k resolution. MS2 scan was initiated when the ACG target set reached 1 × 10<sup>5</sup> ions. An ion selection was completed within 12 s of the dynamic exclusion window. Proteome Discoverer software version 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), including SEQUEST, Percolator, and Minora algorithms, were used to analyze the raw spectrum files. The peptide was compared with the Human UniProtKB database (20,394 sequences on 12/02/2020). The following parameters were used for protein identification and quantification: two trypsin missed cleavages as maximum, 10 ppm of precursor mass tolerance, 0.1 Da of fragment mass tolerance, carbamidomethylation (cysteine, static modifications), and oxidation (methionine, dynamic modifications). The false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.01 was selected and used for peptide and protein identification. Total peptide amount was used for normalizing relative protein abundance ratios. All proteome data were submitted to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository (<a href="#bib55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib55">Vizcaino et al., 2013</a>). Dataset identifier: PXD028152; Project Name: Serum proteomics analysis of CCA; Project accession: PXD028152; Project DOI: 10.6019/PXD028152; Username: <a href="http://mailto:reviewer_pxd028152@ebi.ac.uk" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">reviewer_pxd028152@ebi.ac.uk</a>; and Password: IHy85xct.</p></section><section id="sec3.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.6. Bioinformatics and identification of potential biomarkers</h3>
<p id="p0045">To investigate the heterogeneity of plasma variation in noOVCCA, OV, and CCA subjects, principal component analysis (PCA) was used to observe the differences by Proteome Discoverer. Three-dimensional PCA was conducted by Plotly (R Studio). The heatmap was constructed using the Proteome Discoverer software for hierarchical clustering. Jvenn was used to plot the Venn diagram (<a href="#bib5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib5">Bardou et al., 2014</a>). The specific proteins of each proteome were then used for predicting protein–protein interactions by STRING (<a href="#bib53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib53">Szklarczyk et al., 2021</a>). Reactomes were used to analyze biological networks based on the Reactome Pathway Database (<a href="http://www.reactome.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.reactome.org</a>). The significant threshold for altered biological pathways was set at a <em>P</em> &lt; 0.01 (<a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">Haw et al., 2011</a>). The proteins found in the CCA group by the Venn diagram were found in all 15 replicates of the CCA group and were selected and identified as potential biomarkers.</p></section><section id="sec3.7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.7. Measurement of plasma PIGR by sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay</h3>
<p id="p0050">Validation of identified proteins for CHCA was performed on plasma PIGR using the sandwich ELISA method (Human PIGR ELISA Pair Set, Sino Biological Inc., Beijing, China) following the company-provided protocol. Plate washing of the following step was conducted using 300 μl of washing buffer (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4, and 0.05% Tween®20) (microplate washer, APW-100, Allsheng) 3 times each. Another 50 plasma samples in each subject group were used to verify the potential as a diagnostic biomarker for CHCA using plasma PIGR. For each sample, plasma diluted to 1:50 was used and performed in duplicates. The standard human PIGR of 0–312.5 ng/ml was prepared and used for a standard curve plot. The capture antibody was diluted by coating buffer pH 9.6 (0.05 M Na<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub>, 0.05 M NaHCO<sub>3</sub>, and filter sterile) to 2 μg/ml, and 100 μl of diluted capture antibody was then added to a 96-well ELISA plate (Maxisorp, NUNC) and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The plates were washed, blocked for non-specific binding using 300 μl of blocking buffer (2% bovine serum albumin in washing buffer), and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The plates were washed, and each 100 μl of the diluted plasma samples and standards in sample dilution buffer (0.1% BSA in washing buffer) were added and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. The plates were washed, and 100 μl of 1 μg/ml detection antibody diluted in detection antibody dilution buffer (0.5% BSA in washing buffer) was then added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The plates were washed, 200 μl of TMB substrate solution (0.1 mg/ml 3,3,5,5-tetramethylbenzidine [Sigma] diluted in 10 ml of 0.05 M citrate phosphate buffer pH 5.0, and 2 μl of 30% H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>) was added, and they were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 20 min. To stop the reaction, 2 N H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> was added, and the absorbance values were measured at 450 nm. The absorbance of each sample and standards were subtracted with zero standard absorbance. The log (PIGR standard concentration, ng/ml) values were plotted against subtracted OD450 and used as a standard curve for PIGR concentration calculation. PIGR concentrations (ng/ml) were then calculated using the linear equation shown in the graph below.</p></section><section id="sec3.8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.8. Statistical and diagnostic analyses</h3>
<p id="p0055">The IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) was used. Statistically significant differences between the PIGR concentration of each group were determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a subsequent Scheffe post-hoc test. GraphPad Prism 9 was used for analyzing the dot plot and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Diagnostic parameters were calculated using the diagnostic test evaluation calculator (MedCalc Software, available at <a href="https://www.medcalc.org/calc/diagnostic_test.php" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.medcalc.org/calc/diagnostic_test.php</a>), including the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy.</p></section></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Results</h2>
<section id="sec4.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.1. Analysis of plasma proteomes</h3>
<p id="p0060">The proteomics data was submitted to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD028152 (<a href="#bib55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib55">Vizcaino et al., 2013</a>). PCA and heatmap analyses of the triplicate of 5 pooled plasma pools of each group of noOVCCA, OV, and CCA showed differences in proteome profiles. The PCA results (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>A, 2B, and 2C) were consistent with the heatmap (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>D); within-group protein differences were smaller than between-group differences. By plotting PC1 against PC2 and PC1 against PC3, noOVCCA (orange circle) was more closely related to OV (green circle) than CCA (blue circle) (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>A, 2B, and 2C), similar to the results found in the heatmap (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>D). Differences in expressed plasma proteins among each group were clustered hierarchically, as presented in <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>D. The heatmap depicts their relative abundance with color coding (down- and up-regulated levels: green to red, respectively). The patterns of down- and up-regulation of protein profiles in CCA differed from the noOVCCA and OV groups. The green vertical line remarks the node of CCA down-regulated proteins, and red vertical line remarks the node of CCA up-regulated proteins (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>D).</p>

<p id="p0065">To investigate the pathogenesis-related proteins of <em>O. viverrini</em> infections and cholangio-carcinogenesis, differential plasma proteomes, including noOVCCA vs. OV, noOVCCA vs. CCA, and OV vs. CCA, were analyzed further by jvenn (Venn diagram), STRING, and reactome analysis. STRING and reactome analysis results were in agreement. The STRING results of CCA-specific proteins were targeted to the immune system, coagulation factors, and motor-related proteins (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>), while OV-specific proteins were targeted to the motor and chromosome-related proteins and coagulation factors (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>). Interestingly, the OV and CCA shared proteins were targeted to cell adhesion and activation of T cells (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>). For reactome analysis, specific proteins of both OV and CCA groups belonged to the hierarchy of the immune system, homeostasis, signal transduction, and metabolism (Supplementary Files 2 and 3). For the comparison of OV to healthy controls, the immune system hierarchy (MHC class I, interferon, antigen processing, and presentation), metabolism (fat-soluble vitamins, especially vitamin A), and disease are shown (Supplementary File 2). Comparisons between CCA and healthy controls revealed specific pathways of the immune system (TLR, cytokine, antigen processing, and presentation), signal transduction (Rho GTPases and JAK/STAT), and disease (TLR signaling cascade of the immune system). The significant pathways are shown in Supplementary File 3. Overall, results suggested that OV- and CCA-specific proteins were targeted to the immune system and immune system-related signaling pathways.</p>

Identification of potential biomarkers</h3>
<p id="p0070">In this study, the “present” and “absent” criteria were used to identify potential biomarkers for <em>O. viverrini</em> infection and cholangiocarcinoma. Unfortunately, no proteins specific to <em>O. viverrini</em> infection were identified in this study. However, polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (PIGR) and S100A9 were explicitly found in the CCA group proteome with potential as a cholangiocarcinoma biomarker (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>E). S100A9 has been identified and validated as a potential biomarker for CHCA (<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">Duangkumpha et al., 2019</a>). Therefore, the PIGR was focused on in this study. From LC-MS/MS results, five peptides of PIGR were specifically identified in all CCA proteome replicates, including 123-YLCGAHSDGQLQEGSPIQAWQLFVNEESTIPR-154, 193-LDIQGTGQLLFSVVINQLR-211, 232-NADLQVLKPEPELVYEDLRGSVTFHCALGPEVANVAK-268, 297-ILLNPQDKDGSFSVVITGLR-316, 526-LVSLTLNLVTR-536. These five peptides exclusively belonged to the CCA proteome; therefore, PIGR was identified as a potential biomarker for CHCA and further validated by sandwich ELISA.</p></section><section id="sec4.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.4. Validation of plasma PIGR concentration as a potential biomarker</h3>
<p id="p0075">Plasma PIGR concentration based on sandwich ELISA was calculated from a linear equation (R<sup>2</sup> = 0.9789) of PIGR to a standard log (concentration) curve against an OD of 450 nm. The result showed that the concentrations of plasma PIGR were lowest in the OV group and highest in the CCA group (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>). The average concentrations (ng/ml) ± SD of plasma PIGRs of noOVCCA, OV, and CCA were 1.4936 ± 0.6085, 1.3309 ± 0.5248, and 6.0942 ± 7.5840, respectively (Supplementary File 4). One-way ANOVA revealed that at least two groups had significantly different concentrations of PIGR <em>(P</em> &lt; 0.01). The Scheffe test for multiple comparisons further revealed that the mean plasma PIGR concentration of the CCA group was significantly different from the OV group <em>(P &lt;</em> 0.01; 95% CI: 2.585, 6.941) and the noOVCCA group <em>(P &lt;</em> 0.01; 95% CI: 2.422, 6.778). However, there were no significant differences between the OV group and the noOVCCA group <em>(P =</em> 0.98; 95% CI: −2.340, 2.014). The AUC of noOVCCA and OV vs. CCA was 0.8216 (<a href="#fig5" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>). The highest AUC (0.8778) was obtained from OV vs. CCA (<a href="#fig5" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>). Using &gt;1.559 ng/ml PIGR cut-off, 78.00% sensitivity, 71.00% specificity, 57.35% positive predictive value, 86.59% negative predictive value, and 73.33% accuracy were obtained (<a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). However, using plasma PIGR &gt;3.850 as a cut-off, the 100% specificity of the test was obtained with limited sensitivity (<a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). Hence, the LC-MS/MS, Venn diagram, and ELISA results were consistent and indicated that PIGR was highly specific to CHCA.</p>

<p id="p0080">Proteome analysis by LC-MS/MS has been widely used for the characterization and quantification of proteins and peptides, especially in the evaluation of cancer biomarkers, including CHCA (<a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8">Cevenini et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">Duangkumpha et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18">Hoshino et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#bib19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib19">Hristova and Chan, 2019</a>; <a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21">Huang et al., 2017</a>; <a href="#bib23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib23">Janvilisri, 2015</a>; <a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24">Kim et al., 2009</a>; <a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27">Krug et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#bib36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib36">Nunez-Naveira et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#bib41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib41">Phanaksri et al., 2022</a>; <a href="#bib45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib45">Prasopdee et al., 2022</a>). An individual and pooled sample were used with different advantages and disadvantages. It has been estimated that thousands of individual samples are needed to reduce the biological variability and false biomarker numbers. Pooled samples can help reduce the biological variability of samples, resulting in data with a high degree of confidence (<a href="#bib39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib39">Orton and Doucette, 2013</a>). The time and cost required to acquire individual samples were significantly higher than pooled samples. Based on the incidence of CHCA, sample variability, time, and cost, pooled plasma samples were used in this study to identify potential biomarkers for diagnostic CHCA. Two potential biomarkers for CHCA were identified—S100A9 and the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (PIGR). S100A9 has been identified as a biomarker for several cancers (<a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">Gunaldi et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#bib16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib16">Hermani et al., 2005</a>; <a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20">Huang et al., 2018</a>; <a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24">Kim et al., 2009</a>; <a href="#bib28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib28">Lim et al., 2016</a>; <a href="#bib30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib30">Liu et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#bib31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib31">Lv et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#bib33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib33">Meng et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#bib36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib36">Nunez-Naveira et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#bib60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib60">Zhou et al., 2019</a>), including both non-<em>O. viverrini</em>-related CHCA (<a href="#bib50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib50">Shi et al., 2013</a>) and <em>O. viverrini</em>-related CHCA (<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">Duangkumpha et al., 2019</a>). Meanwhile, PIGR is a biomarker for CHCA in a non-endemic area of <em>O. viverrini</em> (<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">Arbelaiz et al., 2017</a>). S100A9 expression was also high in PSC (<a href="#bib48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib48">Reinhard et al., 2012</a>). Therefore, in this study, only PIGR was identified and further validated as a potential biomarker for diagnosing CHCA in the endemic area of <em>O. viverrini.</em></p>
<p id="p0085">Duangkumpha and colleagues identified serum S100A9 as a potential biomarker for CHCA against premalignant periductal fibrosis (PDF) and healthy subjects (<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">Duangkumpha et al., 2019</a>). Shi and colleagues demonstrated that S100A9 is a biomarker for CHCA by 2D-DIGE. The S100A9 immunohistochemical results demonstrated 92.6% sensitivity and 75% specificity in CHCA diagnosis (<a href="#bib50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib50">Shi et al., 2013</a>). Puetkasichonpasutha and colleagues reported that up-regulation of S100A9 gene expression was correlated with longer survival in patients with CHCA (<a href="#bib46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib46">Puetkasichonpasutha et al., 2020</a>). S100A9 promotes phagocytotic action in neutrophils via the PI3K/AKT pathway, which agrees with previous reports on the up-regulation of proteins in the PI3K/AKT/MTOR pathway in CHCA (<a href="#bib34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib34">Moolthiya et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#bib49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib49">Rizvi et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#bib54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib54">Tiemin et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#bib57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib57">Wu et al., 2020</a>).</p>
<p id="p0090">PIGR has also been previously identified as a biomarker for pancreatic cyst and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (<a href="#bib40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib40">Park et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#bib51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib51">Sogawa et al., 2016</a>). Recently, PIGR was identified from enriched serum extracellular vesicle as a biomarker for non-<em>O. viverrini</em>-related CHCA (<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">Arbelaiz et al., 2017</a>), where a chronic inflammatory condition, i.e., PSC, has been identified as a premalignant lesion for CHCA. However, in non-<em>O. viverrini</em> endemic areas, PIGR western analysis using sensitivity, specificity, and AUC of 83.3%, 100%, and 0.844, respectively, could not differentiate CHCA from PSC. Thus, using PIGR in combination with other potential biomarkers has been suggested (<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">Arbelaiz et al., 2017</a>). In this study, patients with the <em>O. viverrini</em> infection, a risk factor for CHCA, were recruited to identify the potential of PIGR as a biomarker. The results of this study show promise of using plasma PIGR as a biomarker in detecting CHCA in simple <em>O. viverrini</em> infections and healthy subjects with substantial sensitivity, specificity, and AUC. From our results, a plasma PIGR cut-off at &gt; 1.559 ng/ml can be used for disease screening, while a plasma PIGR of &gt;3.850 ng/ml strongly suggests a diagnosis of CHCA. Nevertheless, asymptomatic CHCA at an early stage, unavailable effective diagnostic methods, and only a few early stages of CHCA cases were enrolled in this study; thus, the detection of an early stage of CHCA case by PIGR may be limited. However, plasma PIGR is promising for further investigation of its efficacy in early-stage CHCA detection, especially during the transition from <em>O. viverrini</em> infection to an early-stage CHCA.</p>
<p id="p0095">Therefore, PIGR was used in parallel with stool examination during annual health check-ups to screen for asymptomatic CHCA with a history of <em>O. viverrini</em> infection before ultrasonography of the liver. Presently, liver ultrasonography is used to screen CHCA patients, causing a burden on ultrasonography specialists. Moreover, using PIGR for prognosis is promising, as it has been related to chemoresistance and/or poor prognosis in cancers (<a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6">Berntsson et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12">Fristedt et al., 2014b</a>; <a href="#bib29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib29">Liu et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#bib38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib38">Ohkuma et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#bib56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib56">Wang et al., 2014</a>). This must be further investigated for efficacy in CHCA before or after surgery and chemotherapy.</p>
<p id="p0100">Using PIGR to differentiate healthy/<em>O. viverrini</em> infection and CHCA is more suitable in endemic areas of <em>Ov,</em> such as Southeast Asian countries, while S100A9 could be used to differentiate normal/periductal fibrosis and CHCA (<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">Duangkumpha et al., 2019</a>). Differential diagnosis of PSC and CHCA by S100A9 was limited (<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">Arbelaiz et al., 2017</a>). Although the AUC of PIGR was lower than S100A9, 100% specificity can be obtained using PIGR at the cut-off at 3.850.</p>
<p id="p0105">PIGR is a receptor for polymeric immunoglobulins such as IgA and IgM and plays a critical role in the transcytosis of IgA and IgM from the basolateral to apical surface in response to immunogens. Aberrancies in PIGR have been reported to correlate with an increase and decrease in gene expression and gene polymorphism, as found in several malignancies (<a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">Arumugam et al., 2017</a>; <a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9">Chang et al., 2005</a>; <a href="#bib13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib13">Gologan et al., 2008</a>; <a href="#bib17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib17">Hirunsatit et al., 2003</a>; <a href="#bib37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib37">Ocak et al., 2012</a>; <a href="#bib58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib58">Xiao et al., 2005</a>). Serum levels of PIGR were high in cancer patients before surgery and decreased in the same patients post-operatively (<a href="#bib51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib51">Sogawa et al., 2016</a>). Nevertheless, higher levels of PIGR expression were correlated with longer survival, while the low or absent expressions of PIGR were usually found in patients with lymph node metastases (<a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1">Ai et al., 2011</a>; <a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">Arumugam et al., 2017</a>; <a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6">Berntsson et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">Fristedt et al., 2014a</a>; <a href="#bib13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib13">Gologan et al., 2008</a>; <a href="#bib35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib35">Niu et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#bib47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib47">Qi et al., 2016</a>; <a href="#bib56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib56">Wang et al., 2014</a>). Conversely, high levels of PIGR expression resulted in chemoresistance and poor prognosis in patients with pancreatic cancer (<a href="#bib38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib38">Ohkuma et al., 2020</a>). Yue and colleagues demonstrated that PIGR promoted cell transformation and induced oncogenic growth by Yes activation in the MEK/ERK cascade (<a href="#bib59" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib59">Yue et al., 2017</a>).</p>
<p id="p0110">In conclusion, proteomic analysis using solution-based tryptic digestion, a label-free method, Orbitrap analysis followed by high-energy collisional dissociation, and technical triplicates with five biological replicates was demonstrated to identify S100A9 and PIGR as potential biomarkers for CHCA. The plasma concentrations of PIGR showed substantial sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy as a diagnostic and screening tool for CHCA. Therefore, PIGR has great potential as a biomarker in differentiating patients with CHCA from patients with <em>O. viverrini</em> infections and healthy subjects. The application of plasma PIGR concentration holds promise in future CHCA screening and diagnostic protocols, especially in areas endemic to <em>O. viverrini</em>.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Declarations</h2>
