>Materials and methods   Study participants   This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Foshan Women and Children Hospital. Informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants.   Inclusion criteria   1. Acutely febrile children aged between 6 months and 5 years; 2.Informed consent obtained from the parents or legal guardians; 3.Complete baseline clinical data, including but not limited to: age at onset, gender, primary diagnosis, peak body temperature, duration of fever, timing of seizure occurrence, body temperature and manifestations during seizures, number of seizures per illness episode, laboratory test results, imaging findings, treatment regimens, and family history. Exclusion criteria   Patients were excluded in cases of intracranial infection, epilepsy, head trauma, fluid or electrolyte imbalance, inborn errors of metabolism, rheumatic or autoimmune diseases, immunodeficiency disorders, malignancies, or other significant neurological or systemic comorbidities.   A total of 62 children from Foshan Women and Children Hospital were enrolled in this study. Based on the presence or absence of seizures, they were categorized into two groups: the febrile seizures group (FS group) and the febrile without seizures group (NFS group).   FS group: This group consisted of 31 children who met the diagnostic criteria for FS as defined by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) [ 23 ].   NFS group: This group comprised 31 age- and gender-matched febrile children without seizures. Sample collection and processing   Peripheral blood collection was performed on eligible pediatric patients. For cases of FS, blood draws were conducted within 6 h post-seizure.The collected samples were then processed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C within 2 h to obtain plasma, which was stored at -80 °C.   After all samples were collected, untargeted metabolomic analysis of the 62 human plasma samples (including quality control samples) was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (HPLC-HRMS) in both positive and negative ionization modes. The acquired mass spectrometry data were subsequently interpreted through integrated bioinformatic analysis.   Experimental procedure   Metabolite extraction was performed using an organic solvent-based protein precipitation method. Concurrently, quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots of all prepared experimental samples. The extracted samples were then subjected to instrumental analysis in a randomized sequence. To monitor technical reproducibility, QC samples were inserted at the beginning, middle, and end of the analytical run. All samples were analyzed by mass spectrometry in both positive and negative ionization modes (Fig.  1 ).   Fig. 1.     Open in a new tab   Experimental flow chart Sample pretreatment   Samples were thawed at 4 °C. Subsequently, 100 µL of each sample was aliquoted and mixed with 400 µL of ice-cold methanol. The mixture was allowed to stand at -20 °C for 30 min to precipitate proteins. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 20,000 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. Additionally, QC samples were prepared by combining equal volumes of supernatant from each individual sample. Chromatography   An ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm×2.1 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters) was used for separation. The mobile phase consists of phase A (5 mmol/L ammonium acetate + 5 mmol/L acetic acid + water) and phase B (acetonitrile). Gradient elution conditions were set as follows: 0 ~ 0.8 min, 2% ~ 70%B; 0.8 ~ 2.8 min, 70% ~ 90% B; 2.8 ~ 5.3 min, 90% ~ 99% B; 5.3 ~ 5.9 min, 99% B; 5.9 ~ 7.5 min, 99% ~ 2% B; 7.5 ~ 7.6 min, 2% B;7.6 ~ 10.0 min, 2% B; The flow rate is 0.35 mL/min. The injection volume for each sample was 4 µL. The column oven was maintained at 40℃. Mass spectrometry   A high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer Q-Exactive Plus (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to detect metabolites eluted form the column. Each sample was operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization mode. ESI temperature is 350℃. The voltage is + 3800 volts in positive ion mode and − 3400 volts in negative ion mode. The sweep gas pressure of the ion source is 0 Arb, Gas 1 (Auxiliary gas) pressures set to 15 Arb, Gas 2 (Sheath gas) pressures set to 50 Arb. The mass spectrometric data were obtained with full scan and data-dependent acquisition (DDA) modes. In one acquisition cycle, the full scan acquisition range is 70-1050 Da, and the resolution is 70k, AGC target is set to 3E6, Maximum IT is set to 100 ms. Then, the top 5 signal ions with a signal accumulation intensity of more than 100,000 were selected from the full scan for DDA scanning, the DDA resolution is 17.5k, Maximum IT is set to 50 ms. Dynamic exclusion is set to 6 s. Information analysis   MS data were converted into readable data mzML by MS Convert software of Proteowizard. Peak extraction and peak quality control were carried out by XCMS software. The extracted substances were added ion annotation and primary identification by MetaboAnnotation. Mass spectrum primary information was used for identification and mass spectrum secondary information was matched with in-house standard database respectively. Metabolite annotation of candidate identification substances was performed using databases such as HMDB and KEGG to explain the physicochemical properties and biological functions of metabolites. R language was used to quantify and screen differential metabolites (Fig.  2 ).   Fig. 2.     Open in a new tab   Information Analysis Flowchart Data analysis   The acquired MS data pretreatments including peak picking, peak grouping, retention time correction, second peak grouping, and annotation of isotopes and adducts was performed using XCMS software. LC − MS raw data files were converted into mzML format and then processed by the XCMS, MetaboAnnotation toolbox implemented with the R software. Each ion was identified by combining retention time (RT) and m/z data. Intensities of each peaks were recorded and a three dimensional matrix containing arbitrarily assigned peak indices (retention time-m/z pairs), sample names (observations) and ion intensity information (variables) was generated.   The online KEGG, HMDB database was used to annotate the metabolites by matching the exact molecular mass data (m/z) of samples with those from database. If a mass difference between observed and the database value was less than 10 ppm, the metabolite would be annotated and the molecular formula of metabolites would further be identified and validated by the isotopic distribution measurements. We further validated metabolite identification using an in-house metabolite fragment spectral library.   XCMS software was used to extract the intensity information of each metabolite in every sample. The extracted data were then subjected to quality control using R packages. First, low-quality peaks were removed-specifically, ions missing in more than 50% of QC samples or more than 80% of biological samples were excluded. Missing values in the remaining peaks were imputed using the k-nearest neighbor (kNN) algorithm to further improve data quality. Subsequently, Probabilistic Quotient Normalization (PQN) was applied.   Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the preprocessed dataset to detect outliers and evaluate potential batch effects. To minimize signal intensity drift over time, a quality control–based MetNormalizer model was fitted to the QC data according to the injection order. Additionally, the relative standard deviations (RSDs) of metabolic features across all QC samples were calculated, and features with RSD > 30% were removed.   Differential metabolites were identified by integrating univariate and multivariate statistical analyses. Specifically, the fold-change (FC) derived from univariate analysis and the p-values obtained from t-tests were combined with the variable importance in projection (VIP) scores from orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). Metabolites were considered significantly different if they simultaneously met the following criteria: (1) FC ≥ 1.5 or FC ≤ 1/1.5; (2)  p -value < 0.05; and (3) VIP > 1. Result   Clinical and demographic characteristics of study participants   The clinical and demographic characteristics of the study participants were summarized in Table  1 . Both the FS and NFS groups included 31 patients each, were primarily diagnosed with respiratory tract infections, and had mean ages of 26.68 and 28.65 months, respectively. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of gender, age, peak body temperature, white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, or etiology of infection(all  p  > 0.05). However, a significantly higher proportion of children in the FS group had a positive family history and a previous history of febrile seizures compared to those in the NFS group ( p  < 0.05). Open in a new tab   PCA score plot Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) results   To more accurately identify potential differential metabolites between the FS and NFS groups, OPLS-DA was performed. The resulting model is shown in Fig.  6 . The horizontal axis represents the first predictive component (predictive component 1), while the vertical axis corresponds to the first orthogonal component (Orthogonal component 1). Each point in the plot represents an individual sample, and the spatial distribution of the two colored symbols reflects the separation trends of the two sample groups along these component axes.The model yielded high-quality metrics, with  R²  = 0.918 and  Q²  = 0.612. The  R²  value (coefficient of determination) indicates the goodness-of-fit of the model to the observed data, whereas  Q² —derived through cross-validation—reflects its predictive ability. Values closer to 1 for both parameters signify better model performance. In this study, both  R²  and  Q²  exceeded 0.5, demonstrating satisfactory model robustness. To further evaluate the reliability of the OPLS-DA model, a permutation test was conducted. This test assesses the risk of overfitting by repeatedly shuffling the sample class labels and reconstructing the model. Overfitting would be indicated by either a significantly lower  Q²  in the permuted models compared to the original model or a positive intercept of the regression line in the permutation plot. In this study, the permutation test yielded a  Q²  value of − 0.743, confirming the absence of overfitting and supporting the high reliability of the constructed model.   Fig. 6.     Open in a new tab   OPLS-DA score plot and permutation test validation plot Differential metabolite screening results   Differential metabolites were identified by integrating fold change (FC) values from univariate analysis,  p -values from  t -tests, and variable importance in projection (VIP) scores derived from OPLS-DA. Based on these criteria, a total of 241 differential metabolites were screened. Among these, 144 metabolites were downregulated. The top 10 downregulated metabolites, ranked by fold change, were: levorphanol, (1R,2R,3 S,1′R)-nepetalinic acid, (−)-fumigaclavine B, thymol sulfate, elymoclavine, methylglutaric acid, 4-(aminomethyl)benzoic acid, octocrylene, tetranorprostanedioic acid, and 6-(3,4-methylenedioxyphenyl)-3,5-hexadien-2-one. Conversely, 97 metabolites were upregulated. The top 10 upregulated metabolites, ranked by fold change, were: camphorsulfonic acid, phenobarbital, 2,2′,4,4′-tetrahydroxybenzophenone, (2 S)-2-[(4-methylphenyl)sulfonylamino]-3-{[4-oxo-5-(2-piperidin-4-ylethyl)-7,8-dihydro-6 H-pyrazolo[1,5-a] [ 1 ,  4 ] diazepine-2-carbonyl]amino}propanoic acid, (R)-N-[2-(1 H-indol-3-yl)-1-(4-(4-methoxybenzyl)-5-phenethyl-4 H-1,2,4-triazol-3-yl)ethyl]-2-aminoacetamide, TG(16:1/18:0/24:0), MGDG(O-28:3/9:0), PE(O-15:1/18:2), 15(R)-hydroperoxy-EPE, and TG(O-22:1/14:0/16:0) (Fig.  7 ). We observed that several metabolites exhibiting substantial concentration changes showed functional clustering. Among the downregulated metabolites, compounds such as levorphanol, (1R,2R,3 S,1′R)-nepetalinic acid, and (−)-fumigaclavine B were primarily associated with neuromodulation and microbial metabolic processes. In contrast, among the upregulated metabolites, in addition to the known pharmaceutical agent phenobarbital, the presence of camphorsulfonic acid and 2,2′,4,4′-tetrahydroxybenzophenone suggested potential exposure to exogenous substances. Concurrently, the elevation of various lipid species—such as TG(16:1/18:0/24:0) and MGDG(O-28:3/9:0)—reflected perturbations in lipid metabolism during the acute phase. These findings reveal the complex systemic metabolic disturbances underlying FS. A volcano plot illustrating the distribution of all differential metabolites was presented in Fig.  8 .   Fig. 7. The top 20 most significantly enriched pathways-ranked by ascending  p -value were displayed in Fig.  10 . Enrichment significance was evaluated based on the enrichment factor (also referred to as the “rich factor”), the  p -value, and the number of metabolites mapped to each pathway. A higher enrichment factor indicated a greater degree of pathway enrichment, while a  p -value closer to 0 (within the range [0, 1]) reflected stronger statistical significance. Significantly enriched pathways included necroptosis, glycerophospholipid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, the sphingolipid signaling pathway, phagosome, ferroptosis, sphingolipid metabolism, drug metabolism-cytochrome P450, vitamin digestion and absorption, the neurotrophin signaling pathway, and the gonadotropin-releasing hormone signaling pathway, among others. In the map, lipid metabolism–related pathways (e.g., glycerophospholipid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, and sphingolipid metabolism) and inflammation- and cell death–related pathways (e.g., necroptosis and ferroptosis) accounted for a substantial proportion. This suggests that these pathways may play critical roles in the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying FS.   Fig. 10.     Open in a new tab   Bubble plot of KEGG enrichment analysis for differential metabolites (Top 20 Pathways) Screening of potential biomarkers   A classification model for distinguishing between FS and NFS patients was constructed using the random forest algorithm in R. Differential metabolites from significantly enriched pathways ( p  < 0.05) were selected as feature variables. The samples were randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%) at a 7:3 ratio. The model results revealed the following ranking of metabolite importance: SM(d18:1/24:1), LysoPC(22:0/0:0), SM(d18:0/18:0), Cer(d18:1/16:0), LysoPC(24:0/0:0), 12,13-DHOME, diethanolamine, pantothenic acid, arachidonic acid, and 3-carbamoyl-2-phenylpropionaldehyde (Fig.  11 ). Notably, metabolites that exhibited high importance in the classification model-such as various sphingolipids, lysophosphatidylcholines (LysoPC), and arachidonic acid-were consistent with the lipid metabolism and inflammatory pathways identified in the aforementioned pathway enrichment analysis.
