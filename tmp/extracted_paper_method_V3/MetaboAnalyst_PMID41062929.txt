>Material and methods   Research subjects   Our study was approved with reference number IEC/425/07.06.19, RP-21/2019 by the Institutional Ethics Committee at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, New Delhi, India. The guidelines of ICH-GCP (International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use—Good Clinical Practice) were followed. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants following the Helsinki Declaration. Our study was designed to focus on identifying early protein signature biomarkers for CAD diagnosis from saliva-sEVs proteomics compared to healthy controls. To achieve this, we included a total of forty participants with diagnosed CAD (n = 20) and healthy controls (n = 20) matched for age and gender.   Saliva samples were collected from patients diagnosed by the physician having CAD and healthy controls from 2019 to 2021. All recruited patients were evaluated through patient history, blood tests, Electrocardiogram (ECG), Computed Tomography (CT) or Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans, and physical examinations. The included patients were of (1) age: 18–65 years, (2) both male and female, (3) having Acute myocardial infarction (AMI)/ past MI, and CAD, documented as per Angiography (≥ 50% narrowing in at least one of the major coronary arteries), and (4) Positive stress test or cardiac MRI or CT angiography. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age less than 18 years or more than 65 years, (2) chronic inflammatory disease, (3) Carotid artery disease or stroke and peripheral artery disease, (4) malignancy, (5) non-atherosclerotic myocardial ischemia (coronary embolism or vasculitis), (6) patients with a previous history of heart, renal, or any other recent surgery in the past 3 months. Blood samples were collected for testing lipid profile at the time of saliva collection. During sample collection, we conducted a lipid profile test and monitored basic clinical parameters of blood pressure, weight, and height. We gathered data on hypertension, diabetes, alcohol consumption, statin drug use, as well as fasting lipid profile measurements such as high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), total cholesterol, and triglycerides. The cartoon images used are modified and used after downloading from the source  https://smart.servier.com/ Isolation of saliva-sEVs   A standard operating procedure was followed for the collection and storage of unstimulated saliva from participants. We collected 2 mL of unstipulated saliva from each participant. The samples were collected preferably in the morning between 8:00 am and 12:00 pm. Participants were asked to refrain from eating, drinking, or smoking for at least one hour before saliva collection. After rinsing their mouths with deionized water, participants were instructed to sit upright in a chair and to avoid speaking or swallowing. They were then asked to allow saliva to collect on the floor of their mouths and to spit the liquid into a sterile tube that was kept on ice. A minimum of 2 mL of saliva was collected from each participant. The salivary flow rate was recorded by measuring the volume of saliva collected and the time taken for the collection. Only clear saliva samples of good quality with a pH greater than 6.0 were used. Participants with oral infections, autoimmune disorders, or oral malignancies were excluded from saliva collection.   After sequential centrifugation at 2000 g  for 10 min and 15,000 g  for 10 min at 4 °C, 1 mL clear supernatant was filtered through a pre-sterilized PES membrane syringe 0.22 µm filter. Collected filtrate into ultra-clear tubes for ultracentrifugation at 120,000 g  for 2 h at 4 °C using Optima Max Instrument fixed angle rotors (Beckman Coulter, Thermo Scientific). The upper solution was discarded, leaving the 100-150ul at the bottom as micro-vesicles. The isolated sEVs were further ultracentrifuged using swing rotors with sucrose density gradient steps at 110,000 g  for 2 h at 4 °C. The saliva-sEVs thus collected were further utilized for their characterization and proteomics. 1D SDS PAGE and western blotting   Total protein was obtained using TCA-Acetone precipitation and protein was quantified using Pierce BCA Protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific). 20 µg of each sample were dissolved in 2× Laemmli sample buffer and 12% SDS-PAGE mini gel was run. The See Blue Plus2 pre-stained protein standard was used to track protein migration. The gels were run first at 60 V for 30 min and at 100 V for 120 min until the blue dye reached the end. The resulting gels were stained with Colloidal Coomassie Stain. Dry western blotting transfer was performed by transferring protein onto the polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (PVDF, from Invitrogen). A sandwich setup was created with an anode, filter paper, membrane, gel, filter paper, and cathode in the transfer buffer, and the process was run at 25 V for 25 min. The membrane was blocked using a 5% non-fat milk solution (Biobasic) in TBST buffer for 30 min on a rocking platform. The PVDF membranes were then probed separately with exosome marker primary antibodies mouse anti-human TSG101 (Abcam, # ab30871), mouse anti-human flotillin-1 (Abcam, # ab41927), anti-CD63 (Invitrogen, 10628D), control anti-GAPDH (ITT5052, G Biosciences), and atherosclerotic marker anti-APOA1 (ab64308, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking at dilutions ranging from 1:1000 to 1:2000. The membranes were washed three times in TBST for 10 min each. Appropriate secondary Anti-Rabbit and Anti-mouse IgG H&L (ab205718, Abcam Cambridge, UK) were used. These antibodies were diluted from 1:5000 to 1:10,000 and allowed to incubate for one hour. The membranes were washed three times in TBST for 10 min each. The signal was detected and developed by exposure to Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) plus Western blotting detection reagents (Amersham Biosciences, USA). Images were captured using the Azure Systems 400 (Azure Biosystems, California, USA). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)   The concentration and size distribution of saliva-sEVs were measured using Zeta View NTA (Particle Metrix software version 8.05). The samples were diluted 1:1000 fold in PBS, injected, and viewed under 11 different positions at temperature − 28 °C, pH 7.0 with a 405-nm laser. Camera settings ranged from 12 to 14 and the rest of the auto-settings in default mode. Zeta potential   The Malvern zeta sizer software was used for checking the stability of saliva-sEVs particles in suspension. 1 µL of isolated saliva sEVs sample was diluted in 1 mL of distilled water and analyzed using NanoSightNTA software. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM)   The size and morphology of saliva-sEVs were assessed using TEM and SEM. The 10 uL from freshly isolated saliva-sEVs was fixed, washed, and incubated with formvar-coated copper grids (200 mesh size) for 5 min. The samples were viewed under the Tecnai G2-20 microscope (Fei company, The Netherlands). A 100 uL solution of the samples diluted in distilled water was mounted on a glass cover slip and dried under a desiccator. Sputter coating with gold particles was applied to the samples. The samples were viewed under the SEM Zeiss Evo-18 vs 5.09 instrument and analyzed using SMART-SEM software. Label-free protein quantitation using mass spectrometry   Label-free quantitation of mass spectrometry protein analysis was conducted in pooled triplicate groups and included technical repeats. Protein samples were dissolved in lysis buffer (8 M Urea, 3 M Thiourea, and 4% CHAPS) and added 1X protease inhibitor cocktail (Promega). Equal amounts of protein (200ug each) from pooled sEVs samples were diluted in 50 mM Ammonium bicarbonate (pH ≥ 8) with urea concentration ≤ 1 M. The proteins were reduced with 20 mM DTT (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature and alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The trypsin (Promega) to protein ratio was 1:20, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. The enzymatic reaction was stopped using a final concentration of 1% Formic acid in samples. Finally, the samples were desalted using Pierce’s Peptide Desalting Spin Columns (Thermo Scientific). The samples were dried under SpeedVac and stored at -20 °C until LC–MS/MS.   We utilized an Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometry platform (Thermo Scientific) for analyzing peptide fractions with precision. Sample peptides were introduced using an Easy nano-LC 1000 system (Thermo Scientific) and a meticulous loading process onto trapping and analytical columns. The column was heated to 45 °C using an Agile SLEEVE column oven. Elution was performed for 125 min with non-linear gradients of mobile phase A (water and 0.1% formic acid) to mobile phase B (acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 250 nL/minute, followed by a 15-min wash reaching 99% mobile phase B. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent mode with the number of dependent scans 20 with multi-notch synchronous precursor selection MS3. The LC–MS/MS analysis was performed in biological triplicates and technical repeats. Data analysis was performed using Proteome Discoverer software version 3.0 and Metaboanalyst 6.0. MS2 spectra for Ion trap-detected higher energy collisional dissociation (HCD) spectra were searched using SequestHT against the Uniprot human proteome database appended to the list of common contaminants. The parameters were set as enzyme trypsin, precursor mass tolerance 10 ppm, fragment mass tolerance 0.6 Da, 2 missed cleavage allowed, peptide length 6–144 amino acids, static modification Carbamidomethyl + 57.021 Da (C), oxidation of methionine. Regression settings with non-linear regression and course parameter tuning were used. Decoy proteins and contaminant proteins were removed from the list of all the proteins identified before further analysis. Bioinformatics and enrichment analysis for the identified proteins   Gene functional enrichment analysis, gene ontology (GO), and pathways were obtained for saliva-sEV DEPs after identification. The terms corresponding to biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) were analyzed using the web-accessible program DAVID (The Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery;  https://david.ncifcrf.gov/ ) [ 21 ]. Additionally, pathways enrichment from Shiny GO online platform;  http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/  [ 22 ] was carried out. We compiled the main pathways related to CAD together and obtained pathways information from KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) [ 23 ]. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed for proteins that were ≥ twofold up-regulated and down-regulated using STRING version 11.5; ( https://string-db.org )) with a significance level of  p -value < 0.001. Statistical analysis   Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8 Software (San Diego, CA). Descriptive statistics were used to compare CAD patients and healthy control groups based on demographics, age, gender, blood pressure, body mass index (BMI), smoking, obesity, and fasting glucose levels. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess variable distribution. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using the student  t -test for independent variables. Non-normally distributed data was represented as median and interquartile range. For the baseline data of patients, the classified variables were displayed in numbers and presented as percentages. Correlations were determined using the Spearman-Rho test, and logistic regression analysis was performed to find associations among variables. Mixed effect models were used to analyze the significance of identified proteins. A statistical significance level of  p -value < 0.05 was considered for all tests. Results   Characteristics of participants   Patients recently diagnosed with CAD (n = 20) and healthy controls (n = 20) aged 18–65 years were included. The mean age of CAD patients was 47.6 ± 9.3 years, and age-gender matched control group (47.1 ± 9.2 years; Pearson correlation coefficient (r) = 0.98,  p  < 0.0001) had 75% males in both groups. The patients with CAD had various conditions such as myocardial infarctions (MI) affecting the coronary arteries, including anterior wall myocardial infarctions (AWMI), left anterior descending artery (LAD) involvement, and single, double, or triple vessel disease, with moderate to severe left ventricle dysfunction. Further, these patients had opted for surgical or medical treatment based on their conditions. Study patients were inclusive of risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, and smoking, as previously described [ 24 ]. SD= Standard deviation; # present smokers; ns = non-significant; The  p -values are observed at 95% Confidence Isolation and characterization of saliva-sEVs   Two samples each from patient and healthy control saliva-sEVs were used to standardize NTA, western blotting, and electron microscopy experiments. After isolating saliva-sEVs, we used NTA to determine their size distribution and concentration [ 25 ]. We found that patients had a comparatively greater concentration of sEVs than controls, with an average of 3.1 × 10 10  particles/mL for patients and 1.3 × 10 10  particles/mL for controls. The mean diameter of the patient’s sEVs was 166 ± 62 nm, while the mean diameter of control sEVs was 157 ± 80 nm. The healthy control and patient characterization results are represented in Fig.  2 a (i & ii, respectively). In Fig.  2 b, we depicted the apparent zeta potential for control saliva sEVs as + 34 ± 3.3 mV. The sEVs demonstrated initial stability along with suitable dispersion capabilities. They had rapid coagulation or flocculation-like properties. We used TEM and SEM (Fig.  2 c) to characterize the shape, size, and overall morphology of the saliva-sEVs, which corroborated the presence of sEVs with diameters ranging from 50 to 130 nm. We observed round-shaped morphology in SEM for control samples, while CAD patients showed mixed shapes and morphology [ 20 ]. The sizes in NTA were observed to be larger than that of TEM due to the method being fundamentally based on the Brownian motion of analyte particles and inclusive of solvation shells. Further, the saliva-sEVs were characterized using western blotting showing the presence of exosome markers TSG 101, Flotillin 1, and CD63 (Fig.  2 d)]. Also, the presence of APOA1 has been observed in both patients and controls. Taken together, these results confirm the isolation of sEVs from saliva samples.   Fig. 2.
