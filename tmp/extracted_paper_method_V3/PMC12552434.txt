</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="Par4">Narrow-gap all-position girth welding of pipelines requires accurate seam tracking to handle spatial complexity and dynamic arc behavior. Since existing methods struggle with signal boundary extraction and real-time control, this study introduces a weld-tracking system that integrates torch oscillation synchronization with current feature-region analysis. Pulse (P) and direction (D) signals were used to dynamically partition the welding current into three regions (left edge, middle, and right edge), which resolved boundary uncertainties. Vertical tracking used a binary linear regression model that correlated the wire feed speed and contact tip-to-work distance (CTWD) with middle-region current. Horizontal tracking used trapezoidal integration to quantify current differences between edge regions. The experimental results showed a steady-state accuracy of 97% and a dynamic response time under 50 ms, achieving a precision of ± 0.10 mm. Cosine-curve displacement control enabled overshoot-free adjustments with steady-state errors below 2.2%. This work enhances automated welding systems by combining dynamic signal synchronization with algorithmic integration, offering a new framework for real-time control in narrow-gap welding applications.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Automatic welding, Weld horizontal tracking, Weld vertical tracking, Circumferential welding, Narrow gap beveling, Arc sensors</p></section><section id="kwd-group2" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Subject terms:</strong> Design, synthesis and processing; Mechanical engineering</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par5">During the girth welding of long-distance pipelines, seam tracking plays a crucial role in ensuring weld quality due to the complexity of arc control during all-position welding. This process requires precise monitoring and adjustment to maintain alignment between the welding torch and the weld seam. In this context, various sensing and control methods have been developed to improve accuracy. Among them, Park and Moon<sup><a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a></sup>developed a voltage-based moving average algorithm for seam tracking that improved torch alignment by smoothing voltage signals to reduce noise. However, its reliance on historical data weighting leads to lagging responses during sudden disturbances and a failure to adapt to nonlinear variations in dynamic arc behavior. To address sidewall fusion defects in narrow-gap gas metal arc welding, Liu et al<sup><a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a></sup>. proposed a two-stage tracking algorithm that adjusted the torch swing center using principal component analysis (PCA), which achieved a tracking precision of 0.1 mm. However, its sensitivity to outliers limited its applicability in complex operational conditions. Le J<sup><a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a></sup>investigated the input-output mapping of rotating arc stability sensors and revealed the mechanism by which arc stability affected sensing signals, but their model did not address signal partitioning during dynamic oscillation. Lei et al<sup><a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a></sup>. developed an arc voltage tracking system based on DC pulse square waves and a two-dimensional fuzzy controller. However, the voltage signal-to-noise ratio degraded during narrow-gap welding due to arc compression effects. Seo et al<sup><a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a></sup>. proposed a three-region segmentation method (peak, average, and reference) for welding signals and established a linear relationship between current area differences and lateral deviations. However, static partitioning of feature regions cannot adapt to dynamic torch oscillations. Li et al<sup><a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a></sup>. employed a dual-cycle integral difference method to decouple torch height deviation and working angle deviation, but the fixed integration cycles introduced phase errors during variable-speed oscillation. Liu et al<sup><a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a></sup>. introduced a narrow-gap pulsed welding tracking method that used current multi-feature fusion with PCA and support vector machines (SVM). The method enhanced the sensitivity at the cost of computational complexity and real-time performance. Hölscher et al<sup><a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a></sup>. used contact tip-to-work distance (CTWD) detection during wire arc additive manufacturing, but they did not integrate it with seam tracking control. Kim et al<sup><a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a></sup>. optimized arc sensing using current median clustering, but their algorithm exhibited limited adaptability in unstructured environments. Liu et al<sup><a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a></sup>. analyzed arc initiation stability during pulsed welding but neglected signal synchronization during dynamic tracking. Lin et al<sup><a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a></sup>. developed a variable-gap root-pass tracking method using magnetically-controlled arc sensors, but its reliance on external magnetic fields limited its universality. Gao et al<sup><a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a></sup>. designed a gas metal arc welding tracking system for container girders, but did not optimize the algorithms for narrow-gap groove complexity. Chen et al<sup><a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a></sup>. modeled rotating arc-based variable-groove narrow-gap weld formation, but the model lacked real-time tracking integration.</p>
<p id="Par6">The approaches above face two critical limitations, including a dynamic signal boundary partitioning deficiency. Conventional algorithms do not precisely segment current signal feature regions during torch oscillation, leading to cumulative deviation extraction errors. They also face a trade-off between dynamic response and accuracy, as complex feature fusion techniques enhance precision but incur high computational loads incompatible with real-time control requirements. To address these challenges, this study proposes a dynamic synchronization and feature-region fusion system for seam tracking. By integrating pulse (P) and direction (D) signals from the torch stepper motor, welding current is dynamically partitioned into three regions (left edge, middle, and right edge) to resolve the boundary ambiguities of conventional methods. A binary linear regression model was used to link the wire feed speed and CTWD to the middle-region current for vertical tracking, while trapezoidal numerical integration was used to quantify lateral deviations via edge-region current differences for horizontal tracking. By leveraging dynamic signal synchronization and discrete-time system mapping, this framework provides a method for intelligent control during narrow-gap all-position welding.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Experimental materials, equipment, and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials</h3>
<p id="Par7">An X80 pipeline steel was used as the experimental material, with an outer diameter of Φ1219 mm and a wall thickness of 30 mm. Since thick-walled and thin-walled tubes have similar narrow-gap welding principles, X80 pipeline steel with a wall thickness of 30 mm was chosen to balance experimental efficiency with industrial relevance. The mechanical properties included a yield strength of 555–705 MPa, a tensile strength of 625–825 MPa, and an elongation of ≥ 18%. The chemical composition of the pipeline steel is detailed in Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>. The microstructure was characterized by an acicular ferrite structure (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>), as determined by optical microscopy. The welding consumables were SG8-Pand Ф1.0 mm solid wire (BOHLER, Kapfenberg, Austria).</p>

<p id="Par18">The following parameters were monitored and evaluated: the dynamic signal, welding current (across three zones), and the P/D signal (at 5 V CMOS level), and performance indicators such as vertical tracking error (CTWD deviation), horizontal tracking error (left and right swing displacement), steady-state accuracy (%), overshoot (%), and dynamic response time (ms).</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data analysis</h3>
<p id="Par19">A 256th -order finite impulse response (FIR) low-pass filter was applied to suppress noise. Statistical analysis was performed using MATLAB, using 30 experimental sets for vertical tracking and 24 sets for horizontal tracking. X80 pipeline steel was selected as the representative material due to its extensive use in long-distance pipeline construction. Its electromagnetic properties (resistivity: 220 nΩ·m; thermal conductivity: 38 W/m·K) showed minimal deviation (± 5%) from adjacent steel grades (X70–X100) under arc welding conditions, as confirmed in rotating arc modeling studies<sup><a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a></sup>. As shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>, the acicular ferritic microstructure further ensured consistent arc stability between high-strength pipeline steels.</p></section></section><section id="Sec8"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Method to determine the current characteristic regions</h2>
<p id="Par20">By extracting PD signals from the stepper motor controller, changes in the swing position of the torch within the groove were accurately synchronized with variations in welding current. This synchronization enabled the identification of edge and middle regions during the swing process and monitored the value changes in P and D signals throughout the start-stop-reverse swing cycle of the torch. The corresponding welding current regions were identified based on PD state changes after value modifications. By defining the logical transformation of PD signals “0” and “1”, the condition where <em>P</em> = 0 and D = 1 corresponded to the right-edge region. <em>P</em> = 0 and D = 0 represented the left-edge region. The rest represented the middle region. As illustrated in Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>, the current was divided into three characteristic regions, where the welding current was approximately 200 A, and PD signals were CMOS-level with a 5 V output. These results revealed the relationship between the P signal, D signal, and welding current regions without requiring actual values. The control logic code for the P signal and D signal, as well as the division of intervals for the P signal, D signal, and welding current, are displayed in Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>, wherein the <em>y</em>-axis uses data scaling. The P signal was changed from 5 V to 1 V, the D signal changed from 5 V to 1.5 V, and the original welding current signal data remained unchanged. The scaling ratio was set to 1:100 A to ensure optimal visual representation. Accurate extraction of currents in different regions provided a basis to control vertical and horizontal tracking, supporting further research on tracking algorithms.</p>

<section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Weld vertical tracking algorithm</h3>
<p id="Par22">The weld shape during circumferential welding is significantly influenced by the stability of the welding current, which is directly influenced by the wire feed speed (<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IEq1"><img class="inline" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/bd48/12552434/ad95098c55f9/d33e494.gif" loading="lazy" id="d33e494" alt="Inline graphic"></span>) and contact tip to work distance (CTWD). To precisely control the current, this study combined the characteristic region delineation method (Sect. 3) with an investigation of the effects of the independent and interacting effects of linear velocity and CTWD on the middle-region current. The torch oscillation period during circumferential welding is generally in the range of 2–4 Hz, and the welding process is often accompanied by noise and high-frequency signal interference. To suppress high-frequency noise, we applied a 256-order finite impulse response (FIR) low-pass filter to smooth the main component of the current signal. The middle-region current data were extracted using the characteristic region determination method proposed in Sect. 3.</p>
<p id="Par23">Using 30 sets of experimental data (Table <a href="#Tab3" class="usa-link">3</a>), we established a binary linear regression model to quantify the linear contributions of <em>V</em><sub>f</sub> and CTWD to the middle region current (<em>I</em><sub>middle</sub>). The experimental conditions included wire feed speeds of 8.0–12.0 m/min and CTWD values of 8.0–13.0 mm. The mean values under each condition were statistically analyzed to optimize the control strategy.</p>

<p id="Par59">The experimental platform used a high-speed camera mounted perpendicular to the welding cart’s travel direction for vertical tracking tests and parallel for horizontal tracking tests. <p id="Par74">Table <a href="#Tab10" class="usa-link">10</a> shows that, based on referenced data under potentially different test conditions, the proposed dynamic synchronization and feature fusion method has advantages across multiple key metrics. This includes a reduction in its dynamic response time to &lt; 50 ms, a 67% improvement over the multi-feature PCA + SVM method (150 ms, Ref. 7) because dynamic signal partitioning minimizes phase errors from cycle variations in static methods (e.g., Ref. 5). The steady-state accuracy reached 97% (vs. 85% in Ref. 1 and 94% in Ref. 17), as supported by a binary linear regression model (<em>R</em>² = 0.99) that captured coupling between linear velocity and CTWD.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/Tab10/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section><p id="Par75">The applicability of this method was verified only for narrow-gap V-groove welding and not general grooves (e.g., Ref. 1) or variable gaps (e.g., Ref. 11) due to geometric sensitivity during feature partitioning. The 256th -order FIR filter suppressed high-frequency noise, but it may have also increased hardware resource use in embedded systems, thus requiring a balance between precision and efficiency.</p>
<p id="Par76">This tracking system has been deployed in multiple long-distance pipeline projects, including the Sichuan Gas East Transmission Line Phase II and the East Africa Crude Oil Export Pipeline, accumulating experience from over 200,000 circumferential welds. After operating routinely for 8 h per day with an average output of approximately 20 welds daily, it has demonstrated long-term stability and a first-time pass rate exceeding 95%. This provides practical evidence of its reliability under long-term working conditions.</p>
<p id="Par77">Although validated only on X80 steel with a narrow-gap 5° V-groove, the following parameter adjustment guide is provided so that the method can be applied to other materials (e.g., X70/X65) or groove types (U/J-grooves). For different materials, the heat input should be adjusted based on their carbon equivalent (CET), considering compatibility between the pipe material and welding consumables. For alternate groove geometries, the number of weld layers and the oscillation width should be modified according to the groove angle and root radius to ensure compatibility with the tracking algorithm and avoid step loss. Dimensionless scaling based on groove size is recommended for initial parameter estimation, followed by real-time manual corrections. Validation using small-scale specimens, non-destructive testing, and microstructural analysis is advised before full application.</p>
<p id="Par78">This study synergistically optimized the real-time performance and precision in narrow-gap, all-position welding using dynamic signal synchronization and algorithmic fusion. The innovation was mainly the coupling mechanism between dynamic feature region partitioning and discrete system mapping. Future work should focus on developing lightweight models to reduce computational complexity for industrial edge devices, and extending validation to U/J-groove configurations for method generalizability. Additional work may also include integrating digital twins for closed-loop simulation optimization to enhance the system’s robustness.</p></section></section><section id="Sec22"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p id="Par79">This study proposed a dynamic synchronization and feature zone fusion weld seam tracking method, specifically developed and validated for X80 pipeline steel, 5° narrow-gap compound V-groove geometry, and all-position girth welding. The core elements of the method exhibit scenario specificity. Dynamic feature zone partitioning relied on the real-time synchronization of specific P/D signals from the welding torch stepper motor and the welding current. The validity of this partitioning logic was strictly established based on the groove geometry and the molten pool behavior of X80 steel. The coefficients of the vertical tracking model binary linear regression model (β<sub>0</sub> = 74.61, β<sub>1</sub> = 21.39, β<sub>2</sub> = −6.234) were fitted using experimental data within the specific material, groove geometry, and process parameter ranges of <em>V</em><sub>f</sub> = 8.0–12.0 m/min and CTWD = 8.0–13.0 mm. Based on this, we quantified the relationship between <em>V</em><sub>f</sub>, CTWD, and the intermediate zone current (<em>I</em><sub>middle</sub>) under these specific conditions. The horizontal tracking threshold current difference threshold triggering lateral adjustment (|<em>I</em><sub>diff</sub>| &gt; 1 A) was empirically determined by analyzing the characteristics of current difference distributions across different welding positions (flat, vertical, or overhead) and weld layers within the specific X80 steel groove. This was significantly influenced by gravity and groove width variations. The cosine curve displacement control strategy was optimized for the actuation system used in this setup.</p>
<p id="Par80">The direct applicability of this method is confined to scenarios involving long-distance pipeline all-position girth welding with similar material properties (e.g., X80 pipeline steel or similar grades) and similar narrow-gap V-groove geometries (~ 5° compound V-groove). For other scenarios, the core components of this method (dynamic partitioning logic, model parameters, and empirical thresholds) may require recalibration or fundamental modifications because their effectiveness has not been validated within this study.</p>
<p id="Par81">Future research should explore the potential of applying this method to other material systems (e.g., different high-strength steels) with similar groove geometries. Efforts should also focus on establishing more generalized physical models to guide parameter adaptation. For applications with substantially different geometries (e.g., U/J-grooves), it may be necessary to develop new feature zone definitions or sensing strategies.</p></section><section id="Sec23"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p id="Par82">This study presented a dynamic synchronization and feature-region fusion framework to address critical challenges in narrow-gap all-position girth welding. Using dynamic feature-region partitioning, the integration of pulse (P) and direction (D) signals enabled the real-time segmentation of welding current into three characteristic regions (left-edge, middle, and right-edge). This eliminated phase errors caused by static partitioning. This method also enhanced boundary clarity and reduced the signal processing latency by 67% compared with conventional approaches. The precision tracking algorithms during vertical tracking were used to develop a binary linear regression model that established a coupling relationship between wire feed speed, CTWD, and middle-region current (<em>R</em>² = 0.99), which achieved a 97% steady-state accuracy. During horizontal tracking, trapezoidal numerical integration quantified lateral deviations via edge-region current differences, achieving ± 0.10 mm tracking precision. Real-time performance was achieved using a cosine-curve displacement control strategy to ensure overshoot-free adjustments with dynamic response times &lt; 50 ms and steady-state errors &lt; 2.2%. Experimental validation confirmed the system’s robustness across flat, vertical, and overhead welding positions. According to GB/T 31,032 − 2023<sup><a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a></sup>, this method was applicable to narrow-gap V-grooves during automatic welding for long-distance oil/gas pipelines and a groove angle range of 5–7°. Limitations of this method include material incompatibility with non-conductive materials such as polymers or highly reflective metals such as aluminum, as well as process instability, such as scenarios involving significant dynamic groove width variations.</p></section><section id="notes1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Author contributions</h2>
