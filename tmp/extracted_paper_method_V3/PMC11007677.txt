<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=11007677_ac3c05576_0010.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/fa7a/11007677/ddaebc0e02bb/ac3c05576_0010.jpg" loading="lazy" id="ab-tgr1" height="230" width="729" alt="graphic file with name ac3c05576_0010.jpg"></a></p>
<p>PubChem serves as a comprehensive repository, housing
over 100
million unique chemical structures representing the breadth of our
chemical knowledge across numerous fields including metabolism, pharmaceuticals,
toxicology, cosmetics, agriculture, and many more. Rapid identification
of these small molecules increasingly relies on electrospray ionization
(ESI) paired with tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), particularly by
comparison to genuine standard MS/MS data sets. Despite its widespread
application, achieving consistency in MS/MS data across various analytical
platforms remains an unaddressed concern. This study evaluated MS/MS
data derived from one hundred molecular standards utilizing instruments
from five manufacturers, inclusive of quadrupole time-of-flight (QTOF)
and quadrupole orbitrap “exactive” (QE) mass spectrometers
by Agilent (QTOF), Bruker (QTOF), SCIEX (QTOF), Waters (QTOF), and
Thermo QE. We assessed fragment ion variations at multiple collisional
energies (0, 10, 20, and 40 eV) using the cosine scoring algorithm
for comparisons and the number of fragments observed. A parallel visual
analysis of the MS/MS spectra across instruments was conducted, consistent
with a standard procedure that is used to circumvent the still prevalent
issue of mischaracterizations as shown for dimethyl sphingosine and
C20 sphingosine. Our analysis revealed a notable consistency in MS/MS
data and identifications, with fragment ions’ <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values exhibiting the highest concordance between
instrument platforms at 20 eV, the other collisional energies (0,
10, and 40 eV) were significantly lower. While moving toward a standardized
ESI MS/MS protocol is required for dependable molecular characterization,
our results also underscore the continued importance of corroborating
MS/MS data against standards to ensure accurate identifications. Our
findings suggest that ESI MS/MS manufacturers, akin to the established
norms for gas chromatography mass spectrometry instruments, should
standardize the collision energy at 20 eV across different instrument
platforms.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Electrospray ionization (ESI) combined
with tandem mass spectrometry
(MS/MS) has profoundly influenced small molecule analysis and identification
across an array of disciplines including the omic sciences are routinely
identified.<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a>−<a href="#ref10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref10">10</a></sup> Its high sensitivity and ability to analyze a vast array of small
molecules and peptides make it an invaluable technology. ESI-MS/MS
is widely used in fields such as metabolomics, proteomics, pharmaceutical
analysis, cosmetics, agriculture, toxicology, and exposomics owing
to its ability to sensitively probe small quantities of a broad array
of molecular entities. Notably, ESI-MS/MS enables the reliable detection
of subfemtomole quantities of analytes, making it well-suited for
identifying trace levels of small molecules in biological samples
or environmental matrices. Moreover, ESI-MS/MS’s strength in
achieving high selectivity through precursor-to-fragment ion transitions,
coupled with its capacity to provide structural insights via fragmentation
patterns, underscores its analytical capabilities. The technique’s
compatibility with high-throughput analysis, its versatility in integration
with separation techniques like liquid chromatography (LC–MS/MS),
and ESI’s gentle ionization process,<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a></sup> which preserves the integrity of small molecule structures, further
contribute to its utility.</p>
<p>Of particular significance is ESI-MS/MS’s
applicability
to nonvolatile analytes, especially those with polar characteristics.
This attribute proves valuable for analyzing small molecules that
are labile or thermally sensitive, a challenge often encountered by
traditional ionization methods such as electron ionization (EI) in
gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Taking
advantage of this utility, ESI-MS/MS has widespread use for database
matching where the acquired MS/MS spectra can be compared to databases
containing known spectra of small molecules, enabling the identification
of unknown compounds based on spectral similarity.<sup><a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a>−<a href="#ref17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref17">17</a></sup> However, unlike GC–MS with EI, ESI-MS/MS has yet to be standardized
across different MS/MS instrument platforms. The objectives of this
report were to determine if significant MS/MS variability occurred
across instrument types, evaluate the consistency in identifying molecules
using MS/MS data from any single platform, and identify which collision
energies were generally optimum for cross-platform identification
performance. The latter could also significantly boost data sharing
and reusability. To address these questions, we predominantly focused
on fragment ion variability and collision energy dependence on the
observed fragments across the major ESI-MS/MS platforms available.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>

<section id="sec2.3.1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Agilent HPLC-Agilent 6546 Q-TOF</h4>
<p>Prior to analysis,
the instrument was calibrated in the low mass range mode, and the
isolation window was set to narrow. The standard mix was acquired
in triplicate using an Agilent, ZORBAX Extend-C18, 2.1 × 50 mm,
1.8 μm column with a flow rate of 100 μL/min. Mobile phases
A and B were water + 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile + 0.1% formic
acid. The gradient started from 5% B for 3 min, 90% B at 23 min, held
at 90% B for 3 min, and back to 5%B at 28 min. Let the column equilibrate
for 7 min, and the total run time was 35 min. The data acquisition
was collected in auto MS/MS mode at 0, 10, 20, and 40 CE.</p></section><section id="sec2.3.2"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Agilent HPLC-Agilent 6520 Q-TOF</h4>
<p>Prior to the analysis,
the instrument was calibrated in the low mass range mode and the isolation
window was set to narrow. Each standard was infused directly into
the instrument without column with 50% acetonitrile–water at
the flowrate of 100 μL/min for 3 min. The data acquisition was
collected in auto MS/MS mode at 0, 10, 20, and 40 CE.</p></section><section id="sec2.3.3"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Agilent UHPLC-Bruker Impact II</h4>
<p>Ten mM sodium formate
(10 mM) was infused with a syringe pump at a flow rate of 180 μL/h
and let the signal stabilize for 10 min, then calibrated the instrument.
The standard mix was acquired in triplicate using a Waters ACQUITY
UPLC BEH C18 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm column, and a flow rate
of 150 μL/min. Mobile phases A and B were water + 0.1% formic
acid and acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. The gradient started from
1% B for 2 min, ramped up 99% B for 10 min, held at 99% B for 3 min,
back to 1% B for 0.1 min, and held at 1% B for 2.9 min. The data acquisition
was collected in auto MS/MS mode at 0, 10, 20, and 40 eV of CE.</p></section><section id="sec2.3.4"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Waters ACQUITY UPLC-Waters Synapt G2-Si</h4>
<p>After the instrument
was turned on and the signal was allowed to stabilize for 10 min,
the Synapt G2-Si MS was calibrated with sodium formate (Reservoir
C). The standard mix was acquired in triplicate using Waters ACQUITY
UPLC BEH C18 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm column, flow rate 400
μL/min. Mobile phase A and B were water + 0.1% formic acid and
acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. The gradient began with 1% B for
2 min, 99% B at 11 min, held for 3 min, and back to 1% B at 15 min.
Let equilibrate for 2 min, and the total run time was 17 min. The
data acquisition was performed in FastDDA mode at 0, 10, 20, and 40
CE. The leu-enk LockMass was infused with a flow rate of 10 μL/min
throughout the data acquisition as an internal reference.</p></section><section id="sec2.3.5"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Thermo Scientific Easy Nano LC-Thermo Scientific Q Extractive
Plus</h4>
<p>The instrument was calibrated weekly. The standard mix
was acquired in triplicate using the in-house-made column, flow rate
300 nL/min. Mobile phases A and B were water + 0.1% formic acid and
80% acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. The gradient started from 1%
B for 5 min, ramped up 100% B for 55 min and held at 100% B for 10
min. The data acquisition was collected in full MS data-dependent
MS/MS mode at 10, 20, and 40 eV of CE.</p></section><section id="sec2.3.6"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Eksigent (Sciex) Ekspert NanoLC 425-Sciex TripleTOF 5600</h4>
<p>Mobile phases A and B were 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic
acid in acetonitrile, respectively (Honeywell, Burdick &amp; Jackson).
A 3 μL volume of the sample, or the beta galactosidase digest
for calibration, was loaded onto the trap column at 2 μL/min
flow rate, preceded and followed by approximately 2 min of wash with
mobile phase A. For the samples, the elution gradient at 270 nL/min
flow rate began at 1% mobile phase B, held for 1 min, ramped up to
99% over 70 min, held for 5 min, and ramped back down over 3 min.
For calibration runs, the ramp ran from 5% to 35% over 15 min and
washed at 80% for 9 min. For each sample, data were acquired 3 times
in data-dependent mode at 0, 10, 20, and 40 eV of CE.</p></section></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results and Discussion</h2>
<section id="sec3.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Cosine Scoring across Collision Energies and Platforms</h3>
<p>Cosine scoring (<sup><a href="#ref9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref9">9</a>−<a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a>,<a href="#ref18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref18">18</a>−<a href="#ref20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref20">20</a></sup>) is a prevalent scoring algorithm for comparing MS/MS data, which
calculates the cosine of the angle between two vectors—here,
representing experimental and reference mass spectra—to assess
similarity. Scores range from 0 (no similarity) to 1 (very high similarity),
with a higher score indicating closer alignment and, by implication,
greater spectral similarity. However, given the sensitivity of MS/MS,
even subtle misalignments can substantially impact molecular identification.
Consequently, despite the analytical value of cosine scoring, visual
verification against known standards remains imperative to mitigate
the risk of false positives.</p>
<p>MS/MS data generation, while consistent
in its fundamental principles, can exhibit variability across different
instrumentation. This variability stems from distinctive features
of each platform, including ion source design, mass analyzer configurations,
and detector sensitivities, which collectively influence the resulting
spectra.<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a>−<a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup> The central challenge lies in determining whether observed spectral
differences are intrinsic to the specific instrument or reflective
of the sample’s properties<sup><a href="#ref6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref6">6</a>−<a href="#ref17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref17">17</a></sup> this is where cosine scoring proves useful. By offering a quantifiable
measure of spectral comparison, it enables researchers to systematically
investigate and attribute the sources of disparity in the data.</p>
<p>In this study, we implemented cosine scoring as well as the number
of fragments observed to analyze the compatibility of data across
different instrument platforms. Each data set was compared to a collection
of reference spectra from METLIN generated on an Agilent QTOF, allowing
for a standardized benchmark of similarity. In this process, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> ratio serves as a separate dimension,
with the intensity of each <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> ratio
populating the value within that dimension. The cosine similarity
is then derived from the dot product of the experimental and reference
spectra vectors; this method is extensively utilized in metabolomics
to match compounds with established spectral libraries, facilitating
compound identification.</p>
<p>We collected data from over 100 standard
compounds using five different
instrument manufacturers’ platforms: Bruker QTOF, Sciex QTOF,
Waters QTOF, Agilent QTOF, and Thermo QE with each set of results
cosine scored against the data generated on an Agilent QTOF instrument.
The analysis was designed to provide comparability of the platforms
and the reliability of cosine scoring as a tool for cross-platform
spectral analysis. The Agilent QTOF was used as a reference since
the METLIN database (currently containing experimental MS/MS data
on 931,401 molecular standards) has been built using this instrument
thus facilitating direct comparison of the data.</p>
<p>In the comparative
analysis of MS/MS data across the instruments
(<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>A–D),
there were two examples of MS/MS data at high and low similarity scores
(<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>C,D). Cosine
similarity scores were utilized as a metric to gauge the agreement
of spectral data with reference spectra obtained from an Agilent QTOF
instrument. These scores were plotted against collision energy values
(0, 10, 20, and 40 eV) to determine the influence of collision-induced
dissociation on spectral congruence.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="fig1"><h4 class="obj_head">Figure 1.</h4>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=11007677_ac3c05576_0001.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/fa7a/11007677/6ca6b7858f7c/ac3c05576_0001.jpg" loading="lazy" id="gr1" height="327" width="639" alt="Figure 1"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/fig1/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>Comparative analysis of cosine scores
from MS/MS spectra across
multiple instrument platforms. (A,B) Cosine similarity scores obtained
from the MS/MS data of 100 molecular standards. Five different platforms
were evaluated: Waters, SCIEX, Thermo, Bruker, and Agilent, with the
latter serving as a control. The scores were calculated at four collision
energy levels: 0, 10, 20, and 40 eV. The dashed line in (B) represents
the average. (C) MS/MS data of γ-Glu-Leu (20 eV) and (D) probenecid
(20 eV) provide examples of MS/MS data with high (0.93) and low (0.63)
cosine scores.</p></figcaption></figure><p>The data reveal a subtle relationship between the
number of fragments
and the average cosine score across the instruments tested. It was
observed that the average cosine scores did not necessarily increase
with a greater number of fragments, and instead, the scores plateaued
or slightly decreased beyond a certain number of fragments, which
may indicate an optimal range of fragmentation for maximizing the
spectral similarity.</p>
<p>Moreover, this plateau suggests that very
little fragmentation
is necessary to achieve informative spectra; beyond this point, additional
fragments may not contribute to an increased match quality with the
reference spectrum. It is possible that when the number of fragments
becomes too high, the resulting spectral complexity could lead to
discrepancies in the matching process, potentially due to the increased
likelihood of noise or the presence of nondiagnostic fragments.</p>
<p>This analysis also underscores the importance of fragment ion optimization
in MS to ensure that high-quality, comparable spectra are obtained.
The average cosine scores across different instruments imply a degree
of consistency in spectral quality, with variations likely reflecting
the inherent characteristics and performance of each mass spectrometer
for particular molecules.</p>
<p>While our observations indicated no
significant trend in the relationship
between the collision energy and the cosine similarity scores. An
interesting observation was that even at 0 eV scores were generally
very high, which was also consistent with the scores at 10 eV. An
explanation for this observation lies in the possibility that the
cosine scoring algorithm is weighting relative intensity higher compared
to the number of fragments (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a> and <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Tables <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>–<a href="#tbl4" class="usa-link">4</a>). This indicates that even with a low number of fragments,
the MS/MS spectra are sufficiently distinctive. The higher collision
energies of 20 and 40 eV also provided high cosine scores across all
instruments examined. A result further suggests that the number of
fragments is not necessarily important for the identification process.</p>

the limitations of cosine scoring, and the importance of orthogonal
separation dimensions.</p></section><section id="sec3.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Empirical Assessment across Collision Energies and Platforms</h3>
<p>The reliability of cosine scoring as a metric for compound identification
in MS/MS is complex; while a high score exceeding 0.9 indicates strong
spectral similarity, it does not guarantee the precision of the identification.
This limitation arises due to the potential for small discrepancies
in fragment ion <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values and intensities
to adversely affect the identification process as well as structurally
different molecules (e.g., isobaric dimethyl sphingosine and C20-sphingosine
in <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>) giving
similar MS/MS data. To address this issue, a visual comparison with
verified standards is typically preferred to confirm the accuracy
of the results. Analysis of the data from the seven standards (depicted
in <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figures <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>–<a href="#fig9" class="usa-link">9</a> and <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Tables <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>–<a href="#tbl4" class="usa-link">4</a>) allowed us to examine the variation in results attributable to
different instrument settings and operational parameters.</p>

presents a varied landscape of cosine similarity scores at different

<p>The variation
in cosine scores, particularly at higher collision
energies, highlights the importance of tailoring MS methods to the
specific characteristics of the analyte as well as the capabilities
and limitations of the instrument used. For caffeine, a common and
well-studied molecule, these insights are crucial for ensuring accurate
and reliable mass spectrometric analysis in both clinical and research
settings. These data on caffeine will be synthesized with the findings
from the other molecules to provide an overarching view of spectral
consistency and method optimization.</p>
<p>The variability in cosine
scores across different instruments and
collision energies emphasizes the nuanced nature of interpretation
of these metrics. This underscores the point that cosine scoring is
not an exact science; rather, it requires careful consideration of
the context, including the specificities of the instruments and molecules
being analyzed. Such data demand a cautious approach to drawing conclusions,
as scores may be influenced by a multitude of factors beyond mere
spectral similarity including instrument calibration, data processing
algorithms, and the inherent chemical properties of the analytes.</p>
<p><a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Tables <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>–<a href="#tbl4" class="usa-link">4</a> offer a more comprehensive summary of the cosine
similarity scores and the number of matched fragment peaks for each
of the seven molecules analyzed by different instruments. These detailed
synopses facilitate a multidimensional comparison of the performance
of each instrument with respect to spectral matching.</p>
<p>A key
observation from the tables is the generally high cosine
scores at lower collision energies, which indicate that most instruments
provide similar fragmentation patterns at these settings. As the collision
energy increases, there is a notable diversity in the scores, with
some molecules showing a decline in similarity at the highest energies
across certain instruments. This could be attributed to the instruments’
differing capabilities to handle high-energy fragmentation.</p>
<p>Furthermore, the number of matched fragment peaks, indicated alongside
the cosine scores, provides additional insights into the robustness
of the spectral data. For example, high cosine scores with a greater
number of matched fragments suggest a more reliable and informative
spectral comparison.</p>
<p>The tables underscore the careful calibration
and interpretation
required when utilizing cosine scoring in MS. They highlight that
while cosine scores can serve as a valuable tool for comparing spectral
data, the scores must be contextualized with the number of matched
fragments to fully understand the instruments’ performance
and the reliability of the spectral match. This multifaceted approach
to data analysis is critical for the accurate application of MS in
complex molecular characterizations.</p></section></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p>In summary, our evaluation of MS/MS data
across different high-resolution
platforms underscores the importance of collision energy in achieving
consistent identifications. While lower collision energies (0 and
10 eV) generally yield higher cosine similarity scores, indicative
of cohesive fragmentation patterns, the total number of identifications
across the platforms was low (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>). The higher energy (40 eV) introduced notable variability
and potential spectral discrepancies. This divergence is primarily
attributed to differences in relative ion intensities rather than <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values, suggesting that accurate identifications
do not require many fragment ions, where the precursor ion and a few
fragments are sufficient for identification purposes.</p>
<p>The data
from these data sets underscore the necessity for uniform
method standardization and stringent data collection protocols. <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a> elucidates two critical
considerations. First, it demonstrates that even high cosine scores
and matching spectra potentially may lead to misidentification, as
shown by the distinct structures of dimethyl sphingosine and C20 sphingosine.
This underscores the vital need for molecular identification to be
validated with reference standards, ensuring the accuracy of small
molecule characterization. Second, the data draw a parallel to the
established standard electron energy in GC/MS, highlighting inefficiencies
in ESI MS/MS due to the requirement of conducting multiple collision
energy experiments. These additional experiments at multiple collision
energies not only increase sample consumption and analysis time but
also affect sensitivity and comparability. The optimal congruency
observed at 20 eV suggests that standardizing collision energy at
this level across all LC ESI MS/MS platforms could significantly enhance
uniformity and sensitivity, ultimately boosting the sharing and reproducibility
of scientific data.</p></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
