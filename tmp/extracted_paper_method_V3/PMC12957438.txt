</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="Par1">Sea cucumbers are widely exported from the Middle-East to East Asia being consumed as traditional food. Due to paucity of knowledge concerning biochemical analysis of Red Sea Holothurians, we studied the distribution of triterpene saponins in the external muscular body walls and internal visceral organs of three edible sea cucumbers of the Red Sea using LC-qTOF-MS/MS. Saponins in <em>H. atra</em> and <em>Bohadschia vitiensis</em> were generally localised in the walls whereas they showed higher relative abundance in the viscera of <em>Holothuria impatiens</em>. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and Sulforhodamine B (SRB) assays showed superiority of <em>H. impatiens</em> viscera in reducing the viabilities of all tested cancer cell lines. Bioassay-guided fractionation revealed strong cytotoxicity for the dichloromethane (DCM) fraction of <em>H. impatiens</em> visceral extract. Bioactive molecular networking showed triterpenes to contribute to the cytotoxic activity of the DCM fraction. LC-qTOF-MS/MS analysis and molecular networking of the bioactive fraction showed enrichment of the triterpenes bivittosides C and D. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that bivittosides most probably affect cancer pathways such as PI3K/Akt, interleukins signaling, and receptor tyrosine kinases. Molecular docking showed good binding scores for bivittosides C and D on STAT3, Akt1, and EGFR proteins.</p>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h3>
<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39441-3.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> <em>H. atra</em>, <em>H. impatiens</em>, <em>B. vitiensis</em>, LC-qTOF-MS/MS, Cytotoxicity, Molecular networking</p></section><section id="kwd-group2" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Subject terms:</strong> Cancer, Drug discovery, Chemistry</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par2">The Holothurians (sea cucumbers) are marine invertebrates belonging to phylum Echinodermata (class Holothuroidea) and they are mostly disseminated on sea floors from warm tropical water to cold deep sea trenches<sup><a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a></sup>. Over 1200 species of Holothurians were so far identified. Their full body (including the viscera and the outer muscles) are widely consumed in China and other Asian countries as delicacy tonic food and for their nutritional value<sup><a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>–<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a></sup>. The Holothuriidae family (class Holothuroidea; order Holothuriida) comprises five genera and 187 species of which 150 species belong to the genus <em>Holothuria</em>, 17 species belong to the <em>Actinopyga</em>, 11 species belong to Bohadschia, eight species belong to <em>Labidodemas</em>, and one species belongs to <em>Pearsonothuria</em><sup><a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a></sup>. <em>Holothuria atra</em> Jaeger (black sea cucumber or Lollyfish) is widely disseminated in the West Indo-Pacific region and on coral reefs<sup><a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a></sup>. <em>Bohadschia vitiensis</em> and <em>H. impatiens</em> are also among the important species present in the Hurghada area of the Red Sea<sup><a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a></sup>. Holothurians are also used as functional foods owing to their high protein and low fat level, their richness in vitamins such as vitamin A, B<sub>1</sub>, B<sub>2</sub>, and B<sub>3</sub> as well as minerals specially for calcium, magnesium, iron, and zinc along with mucopolysaccharides such as chondroitin sulfate and other glycosaminoglycans GAGs<sup><a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>,<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a></sup>. Holothurians extracts and their bioactive compounds, particularly for polysaccharides<sup><a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>,<a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a></sup> and peptides<sup><a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>–<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a></sup>, were reported to improve immunity and gut microbiota<sup><a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a></sup> and for treatment of ulcerative colitis<sup><a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a></sup>. They showed antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities hence used to relieve diabetic neuropathy via suppressing oxidative stress<sup><a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>–<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a></sup>. They used to relieve constipation<sup><a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a></sup>, hyperuricemia<sup><a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a></sup>, and demonstrated neuroprotective therapeutic effects in Alzheimer’s<sup><a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>,<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a></sup> and Parkinson’s diseases<sup><a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>,<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a></sup> as well as antiviral and anticoagulant activities<sup><a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>,<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par3">Triterpene saponins are highly abundant in Holothurians and they are composed of a holostane-type aglycone in which the D-ring contains a <em>γ</em>-18(20)-lactone<sup><a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a></sup>. A 7(8)- or 9(11)-double bond is usually present with oxygen substituents at carbons 3, 12, or 17. The oligosaccharide chain is composed of up to six sugar residues including D-xylose, D-quinovose, 3-<em>O</em>-methyl-D-glucose, 3-<em>O</em>-methyl-D-xylose, and D-glucose which are usually linked to the aglycone at position 3. Other saccharide residues that could likewise exist include 3-<em>O</em>-methyl-D-glucuronic acid, 3-<em>O</em>-methyl-D-quinovose, and 6-<em>O</em>-acetyl-D-glucose<sup><a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>,<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a></sup>. The first sugar in the oligosaccharide chain is always D-xylose while the terminal sugar is usually a methylated saccharide residue<sup><a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a></sup>. Sea cucumber saponins are either sulfated or non-sulfated where the sulfate group could be positioned in the aglycone or the oligosaccharide part especially at xylose, glucose, or methylglucose residues. Monosulfated saponins are highly common with few exceptions of the di- and tri-sulfated analogs<sup><a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a></sup>. Although the exact pharmacological benefits of saponins are still speculative, several studies reported their cytotoxic effects when tested on different types of cancer cells<sup><a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>,<a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>–<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a></sup>. Some saponins were isolated from the cucumber body wall<sup><a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>,<a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a></sup>, and others were detected in the viscera especially in the Cuvierian tubules<sup><a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>,<a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>–<a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par4">Despite the richness of the Red Sea with Holothurians, only very few biochemical and biological studies were conducted on species like <em>H. impatiens</em> and <em>B. vitiensis</em>. In the current study, LC-qTOF-MS/MS analysis was performed on the extracts of three underexplored Red Sea Holothurians to provide a detailed view to their chemical metabolome and to study the distribution of triterpene saponins between their body walls and internal viscera. Cytotoxicity studies were conducted in vitro to assess the most active extracts which were further fractionated and the obtained fractions were re-screened for their cytotoxicity using CCK-8 and SRB assays. The cytotoxic fractions were re-analyzed using LC-qTOF-MS/MS to identify their bioactive metabolites. Bioactive molecular networking was conducted to correlate the obtained activity with the <em>m/z</em> ions present in the bioactive fractions. Network pharmacological studies were performed to provide an overview to the central genes that could be potential targets for the compounds identified in the cytotoxic fractions. This was further supported by docking investigations to study the possible interactions and binding affinities between the bioactive metabolites and their targets.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample collection, extraction, and fractionation</h3>
<p id="Par5">Three fresh sea cucumber species named <em>Holothuria atra</em> (533.48 g), <em>Holothuria impatiens</em> (495.48 g), and <em>Bohadschia vitiensis</em> (338.41 g) were collected by SCUBA diving off the coast of Hurghada in October 2023. The species were taxonomically identified by Prof. Sayed Abd-ElAziz at the National Institute of Oceanography and Fisheries. Samples were deposited at the Department of Pharmacognosy, Faculty of Pharmacy, Ain-Shams University with registration codes of PHG-M-HA-579, PHG-M-HI-580, and PHG-M-BV-581 for <em>H. atra</em>, <em>H. impatiens</em>, and <em>B. vitiensis</em>, respectively. Samples were transported in ice boxes to the lab of Faculty of Pharmacy, Ain Shams University, Cairo. The samples were washed with running water, the body walls were mechanically separated from the internal viscera, then cut into smaller pieces for easy handling. The viscera and walls of each species were individually homogenized and weighed to yield 415.8 g, 117.7 g, 291.8 g, 203.7 g, 302.7 g, and 35.7 g of <em>H. atra</em> wall, <em>H. atra</em> viscera, <em>H. impatiens</em> wall, <em>H. impatiens</em> viscera, <em>B. vitiensis</em> wall, and <em>B. vitiensis</em> viscera, respectively. The homogenized extracts were freeze-dried then extracted with 80% aqueous ethanol for 6 days at room temperature in the dark (solvent to sample ratio = 3:1). The extracts were filtered, concentrated under reduced pressure, and lyophilized to yield 14.5 g of <em>H. atra</em> wall extract (yield = 3.5%), 8.31 g of <em>H. atra</em> viscera extract (yield = 7%), 29.5 g of <em>H. impatiens</em> wall extract (yield = 10.1%), 15.9 g of <em>H. impatiens</em> viscera extract (yield = 7.8%), 2.5 g of <em>B. vitiensis</em> wall extract (yield = 0.8%), and 6.6 g of <em>B. vitiensis</em> viscera extract (yield = 18.5%). The extracts were stored at -20 ℃ for further analysis. Bioactive extracts of <em>H. atra</em> wall and <em>H. impatiens</em> viscera were redissolved in 80% ethanol and were separately partitioned against n-hexane, dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, and butanol. The fractions were concentrated under reduced pressure and stored at -20 ℃ for LC-qTOF-MS/MS analysis. The extraction was adopted in a way similar to that previously described by Wijesinghe et al.<sup><a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a></sup> with slight modifications.</p></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LC-qTOF-MS/MS analysis and data processing</h3>
<p id="Par6">Chromatographic analysis of the extracts and the bioactive fractions were performed on a SHIMADZU LCMS-9030 system (SHIMADZU Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Chromatographic resolution was achieved on a C<sub>18</sub> column of SHIMADZU Shim-pack GIST (2 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) using water (solvent A, containing 0.1% formic acid) and MeOH (solvent B, containing 0.1% formic acid) as the mobile phase implementing a gradient elution protocol (0–7 min: 10–40% B, 7–15 min: 40–100% B, 15–20 min: 100% B). The column temperature was adjusted at 40 °C in an oven and the flow rate was fixed at 0.4 ml/min. The mass-to-charge (<em>m/z</em>) was set between 100 and 1800 to cover all possible masses of triterpene glycosides. In the MS<sup>2</sup> scans, the automatic data-dependent acquisition (DDA) method was employed, using a collision energy of 35 eV. Measurements were acquired in the positive and negative modes. In the positive mode scan, the interface voltage was set to 4 kV, the interface temperature was 300 ℃, the desolvation temperature was 526 ℃, the nebulizing gas flow rate was adjusted at 3 l/min, and the heating gas flow rate was at 10 l/min. In the negative mode scan, the interface voltage was adjusted at -3 kV with an interface temperature of 300 ℃ and desolvation temperature of 526 ℃. The nebulizing, heating, and drying gas flow rates were 3, 10, and 10 l/min, respectively. The mass spectra were recorded within <em>m/z</em> mass range between 100 and 1800 Da and the analyses were performed in the negative and positive modes. Peak areas were used to compare the relative percentage of saponins between the body walls and viscera in the three species. Raw mass files were converted to .mzML (using MSconvert software) which were processed using MZmine 2 for data centroiding, filtering, deconvoluting, deisotoping, and aligning. The obtained feature list was exported as feature quantification table (.csv) and MS/MS spectral summary file (.mgf) which were uploaded on GNPS (<a href="https://gnps.ucsd.edu" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gnps.ucsd.edu</a>) for FBMN (feature based molecular network) analysis. Identification of compounds was based on detailed MS/MS interpretation, review of literature, and online platforms such as massbank, MarinLit, metabolomics workbench, m/z cloud, GNPS libraries, and Sirius in silico tools.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay</h3>
<p id="Par7">Lovo (Cat# 60148) and DLD-1 (Cat# 60132) were obtained from the Bioresource Collection and Research Center (Hsinchu, Taiwan). The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1 × antibiotic-antimycotic at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO<sub>2</sub>. The cells were treated with DMSO or the indicated extracts for 48 h then incubated with the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA) at 37 °C for 2 h. The cell viability was determined by a spectrophotometer at 450 nm. HBSS (Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution) was used as the medium for the assay (pH 7.4). Each experiment was operated under 37 °C and performed in triplicate. The CCK-8 is a widely used kit for assessing cell viability and cytotoxicity based on the reduction principle of a water-soluble tetrazolium salt (WST-8).</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Cell culture</h3>
<p id="Par8">Human prostate cancer cell line (PC3), fibroblast cell line (HFB-4), and human lung cancer cell line (H-460) (provided by the tissue culture unit, center for drug discovery research and development, Faculty of Pharmacy, Ain-Shams University) were maintained in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 100 µg/mL streptomycin, 100 units/mL penicillin and 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum in a humidified, 5% (v/v) CO<sub>2</sub> atmosphere at 37 °C.</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay</h3>
<p id="Par9">Cell seeding was done at a density of 2000 cells/ well in 96-well plates. Cells were treated for 72 h with five different concentrations (0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000 µg/ml) of tested samples. Cytotoxicity was assessed at the end of drug exposure using SRB assay as previously described<sup><a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">39</a></sup>. Absorbance was measured at 545 nm using microplate reader (BioTek instruments, Vermont, USA). Results were expressed as the relative percentage of absorbance compared to the control. Experiments were done in triplicates. Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC<sub>50</sub>), the drug concentration at which 50% growth inhibition is achieved, was calculated using GraphPad Prism software version 5.00 (GraphPad Software, Inc. La Jolla, CA, USA).</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Bioactivity-based molecular network</h3>
<p id="Par10">The MS/MS data were processed on MZmine 2.53 (an open-source software for mass spectrometry data processing allowing the downstream analysis of the processed data on different platforms through multiple export modules). The masses were first centroided and the noise levels were set to 4000 for MS and 100 for MS<sup>2</sup>. The chromatograms were built using the ADAP algorithm (the minimum number of scans was set to 3, the group intensity threshold and the minimum highest intensity were set to 10,000 and 150,000, respectively, and the mass tolerance was set to 0.005 or 15 ppm). The resulting feature lists were deconvoluted, deisotoped, and aligned. The aligned feature list was gap filled (intensity tolerance = 10%, <em>m/z</em> difference = 0.005, rt difference = 0.25) and exported as CSV file. In the resulting CSV file, blank features were removed using a threshold of 0.5. The raw data of the fractions of <em>H. impatiens</em> visceral extract were uploaded to the GNPS platform to construct classical networks with precursor and fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.02 Da. Edges were formed when the cosine score exceeded 0.7 and with six minimum matched fragment ions. Network for <em>H. impatiens</em> visceral fractions could be accessed at (<a href="https://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=708f21e9317143b0abab954aeb50e032" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=708f21e9317143b0abab954aeb50e032</a>), and was visualized on Cytoscape. The CSV file was exported from MZmine, showing the relative quantities of mass ions, was modified to incorporate the cell viability results of the bioactive extract and its fractions. The file was then uploaded to the “bioactive molecular network” workflow (<a href="https://github.com/DorresteinLaboratory/Bioactive_Molecular_Networks" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://github.com/DorresteinLaboratory/Bioactive_Molecular_Networks</a>) where a CSV file containing the Pearson correlation scores (<em>r</em> values) (between the bioactivities of the mass ions and their relative quantities) and their significance (<em>p</em> values) is downloaded and imported to Cytoscape for network visualization. Large nodes with high bioactive scores (<em>r</em> &gt; 0.7 and <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) are suggested to be high cytotoxic candidates.</p></section><section id="Sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Network pharmacology studies</h3>
<p id="Par11">Targets of the main bioactive components identified in <em>H. impatiens</em> bioactive fractions were collected from Swiss prediction (<a href="http://swisstargetprediction.ch/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://swisstargetprediction.ch/</a>) and binding databases (<a href="https://bindingdb.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://bindingdb.org/</a>). Genes related to cancer were gathered from the GeneCards platform (<a href="https://www.genecards.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.genecards.org/</a>). The shared genes between the components and the disease were obtained from biotools (<a href="https://www.biotools.fr/misc/venny" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.biotools.fr/misc/venny</a>). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed on the String database (<a href="https://string-db.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://string-db.org/</a>). GO terms (including BP, MF, and CC) and pathway enrichment analysis were performed using Enrichr (<a href="https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/</a>) and gprofiler (<a href="https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost</a>) databases. Cytoscape (<a href="https://cytoscape.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://cytoscape.org/</a>) was used to visualize and analyze the networks.</p></section><section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Molecular docking investigations</h3>
<p id="Par12">Molecular docking was conducted on bivittosides C and D which are the abundant triterpenes detected in the bioactive dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, and aqueous ethanolic fractions of the visceral extract of <em>H. impatiens</em>.Docking was performed on AKT1 (PDB ID: 3MVH; 2.01 Å), STAT3 (PDB ID: 6NJS; 2.7 Å), SCR (PDB ID: 3F3V; 2.6 Å), and EGFR (PDB ID: 1M17; 2.6 Å). The co-crystallized inhibitors were used as a control WFE (for AKT1), KQV (for STAT3), 1BU (for SCR), and AQ4 (for EGFR). The X-ray crystal structures of the targets were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) and were processed by removing water molecules on USF Chimera X 1.7.1. The compounds were drawn on Chemdraw and their energies were minimized using Avogadro v.1.2.0. The least energy conformer was converted to (.pdb) files separately. Docking was performed on the DINC 2.0 webserver (<a href="https://dinc.kavrakilab.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://dinc.kavrakilab.org/</a>) which is a parallelized meta-docking method for large molecular sized ligands or those with high number of torsions using Autodock vina. Compounds were submitted to DINC as “.pdb” file. The DINC web server uses Autodock vina scoring function to calculate binding affinities. The binding site of each protein was identified from the co-crystallized inhibitors to set the binding box parameters. The complexes were visualized on the Discovery studio visualizer v.21.1.0.20298 and the interactions of the highest binding energies were studied.</p></section></section><section id="Sec11"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results and discussion</h2>
<section id="Sec12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LC-qTOF-MS/MS-based analysis of triterpene saponins in the Red Sea Holothurians</h3>

Their constitution is almost similar except for a glucose moiety in the saccharide chain of holothurin D that is substituted with quinovose in holothurin A<sub>3</sub>. The dihydro analog of holothurin A<sub>3</sub> was likewise detected (peak 6, t<sub>R</sub>=9.62 min) with a desodiated ion at <em>m/z</em> 1215.5115. Holothurin A (peak 18, t<sub>R</sub>=10.36 min) and its desulfated derivative (peak 20, t<sub>R</sub>=10.50 min) showed <em>m/z</em> at 1197.5063 [M-Na]<sup>−</sup> and <em>m/z</em> 1117.5394 [M-H]<sup>−</sup>, respectively. The MS/MS spectra of peak 18 (Fig. 1S) showed the initial loss of a methyl hexose (-176 Da) at <em>m/z</em> 1021, followed by the loss of a hexose and quinovose moieties (-308 Da) to yield a fragment peak at <em>m/z</em> 713. The loss of a sulfated xylose (-230 Da) resulted in a fragment ion at <em>m/z</em> 483. The desulfo analog of holothurin A showed similar MS<sup>2</sup> pattern to holothurin A (Fig. 2S) with the loss of methyl hexose, hexose, quinovose at <em>m/z</em> 941, 779, 633 respectively, yet there was a loss of xylose (instead of sulfo xylose as in case of holothurin A) at <em>m/z</em> 501. The dihydroxy analog of holothurin A (peak 11, t<sub>R</sub>=9.93 min) showed a desodiated ion peak at <em>m/z</em> 1181.4993 and it was detected in all studied species. A series of non-sulfated saponins named holothurinosides were detected predominantly in the extracts of <em>H. impatiens</em>. Holothurinoside B (peak 25, t<sub>R</sub>=11.10 min), previously isolated from the sea cucumber <em>Holothuria forskalii</em>, showed MS<sup>2</sup> ions attributed to the loss of a methyl hexose (-176 Da) at <em>m/z</em> 1145 followed by the loss of a hexose (-162 Da) at <em>m/z</em> 983, a loss of quinovose (-146 Da) at <em>m/z</em> 837, and the loss of a hexose (-162 Da) to yield the aglycone moiety at <em>m/z</em> 675 (Fig. 3S). Holothurinoside X (peak 30, t<sub>R</sub>=11.58 min) showed MS<sup>2</sup> fragment at <em>m/z</em> 619 attributed to the loss of the aglycone moiety (-484 Da). Other holothurinosides were similarly identified in their deprotonated form at <em>m/z</em> 1293.6266 (t<sub>R</sub>=9.56 min), 1263.5967 (t<sub>R</sub>=9.65 min), 1101.5452 (t<sub>R</sub>=10.32 min), and 1279.5984 (t<sub>R</sub>=11.86 min) corresponding to holothurinosides L (peak 5), E (peak 8), C (peak 16), and A<sub>1</sub> (peak 33). Impatienside A (peak 21, 10.60 min) showed MS/MS spectra with sequential loss of methyl hexose (-176 Da), hexose (-162 Da), methyl hexose (-176 Da), hexose (-162 Da), and quinovose and xylose (-278 Da) (Fig. 4S). Similarly, impatienside B (peak 22) showed the loss of sugar moieties from its glycone part consistent with its structure (Fig. 5S). Peak 31 showed [M-Na]<sup>−</sup> ion at <em>m/z</em> 859.3887 and was identified as holothurin B/B<sub>4</sub> with MS/MS fragments at <em>m/z</em> 713 and <em>m/z</em> 483 attributed to the loss of quinovose (-146 Da) and sulfated xylose (-230 Da), respectively (Fig. 6S). Bivittosides C (peak 32) and D (peak 24) were identified based on their MS/MS spectra (Figs. 7S and 8S) which showed loss of sugar moieties consistent with what have been reported in the literature<sup><a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">42</a></sup>. In the positive ion mode, deglycosylated holostane triterpenes were the predominantly detected molecular species and their MS/MS spectra showed characteristic decarbonylation (-28 Da) and dehydroxylation (-18 Da) as previously reported<sup><a href="#CR43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR43">43</a></sup>.</p></section><section id="Sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LC-qTOF-MS/MS profiling of the body wall and visceral extracts of <em>Holothuria atra</em>
</h3>
<p id="Par15">The average masses of sea cucumber-specific saponins ranged between 600 and 1500 Da. In most cases, sulfated saponins displayed [M-Na]<sup>−</sup> ion in the negative mode whereas non-sulfated saponins displayed [M-H]<sup>−</sup> and [M + Na]<sup>+</sup> ions in the negative and positive modes, respectively<sup><a href="#CR44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR44">44</a>,<a href="#CR45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR45">45</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par16">The abundant saponins in <em>H. atra</em> wall extract were sulfated triterpenes that mostly ionize in the negative mode (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>). These include leucospilotaside C ([M-Na]<sup>−</sup> at <em>m/z</em> 713, peak 27; Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>), holothurin B<sub>2</sub> ([M-Na]<sup>−</sup> at <em>m/z</em> 860, peak 26), 17-dehydroxyholothurin A or 24-dehydroechinoside A ([M-Na]<sup>−</sup> at <em>m/z</em> 1181, peak 11), holothurin A ([M-Na]<sup>−</sup> at <em>m/z</em> 1197, peak 18), holothurin B or B<sub>4</sub> ([M-Na]<sup>−</sup> at <em>m/z</em> 859, peak 31), holothurin B<sub>3</sub> ([M-Na]<sup>−</sup> at <em>m/z</em> 843, peak 34), 17-hydroxyfuscocineroside A ([M-Na]<sup>−</sup> at <em>m/z</em> 1255, peak 10), holothurin A<sub>3</sub> or D ([M-Na]<sup>−</sup> at <em>m/z</em> 1213, peak 1), and fuscocineroside A ([M-Na]<sup>−</sup> at <em>m/z</em> 1239, peak 7) (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>). The feature at 10.3 min with <em>m/z</em> of 1147.5685 remained unidentified suggesting it to be a new triterpene. In the positive mode, <em>H. atra</em> wall extract displayed [M + H]<sup>+</sup> peaks at <em>m/z</em> 469 (peak 15) and 467 (peak 12) corresponding to holosta-7,22,24-triene-3,17-diol and its dehydrogenated derivative, respectively. Similarly, 22,25-epoxyholothurinogenin (peak 13) and its dehydrogenated analog (peak 9) ionized positively at <em>m/z</em> 485 and 483, respectively.</p>

</h3>
<p id="Par21">The saponin content in the body wall and visceral extracts of <em>H. impatiens</em> were studied in the negative and positive modes (Fig. 3S). LC-MS/MS analysis showed the presence of peaks 5, 8, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 27, 28, 30, 31, 32, and 33 (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>) which were earlier eluted in the chromatogram within the first 13 min. In this time frame, the intensities and peak areas of dehydroholosta-7,22,24-triene-3,17-diol (peak 12), 22,25-epoxyholothurinogenin (peak 13), and holothurinoside A1 (peak 33) were higher in the visceral extract than in the wall (Figs. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>, 11S, and 12S). Triterpenes like arguside F (peak 14), holothurinogenin B (peak 28), and holothurinosides X (peak 30) were exclusively detected in the viscera whereas holosta-7,22,24-triene-3,17-diol (peak 15), holothurinoside C (peak 16), cousteside I (peak 17), and holothurin A2 (peak 23) were only detected in the walls. Comparable abundance of desulfated holothurin A (peak 20), impatienside A (peak 21), bivittoside D (peak 24), holothurinoside B (peak 25), and holothurin B (peak 31) were observed in the viscera and walls. Our results agreed with the LC-MS previously reported on the extract of <em>H. impatiens</em> wall (collected from the Philippines) in the presence of impatienside A, bivittosides C and D, and holothurin A, yet the wall of the Red sea species studied here was devoid of holothurinosides C and H, holothurin A<sub>3</sub>, and pervicoside C<sup><a href="#CR50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR50">50</a></sup>. Another study<sup><a href="#CR51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR51">51</a></sup> reported on the isolation of holothurin A, fuscocineroside B, and pervicoside C from the Vietnamese <em>H. impatiens</em> crude methanol extract however the study has not specified whether the reported triterpenes were present in the wall or the viscera of the organism. A scatter plot showing the distribution of parent mass ions of saponins in the wall and visceral extracts of <em>H. impatiens</em> is presented in Fig. 12S where highly abundant ions are clustered in darker color.</p>

<p id="Par29">The effects of the body wall <em>versus</em> visceral extracts of the three sea cucumber samples on the viability of the colorectal adenocarcinoma cell lines DLD-1 and LoVo showed superiority of the visceral extract of <em>H. impatiens</em> in reducing the viability of both cancer cells (Table <a href="#Tab2" class="usa-link">2</a>). No cytotoxicity was observed for the remaining extracts on LoVo cells. The cytotoxic activities of different species of sea cucumbers and their isolated compounds were reported on several cancer cell lines<sup><a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>,<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a>,<a href="#CR53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR53">53</a>–<a href="#CR57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR57">57</a></sup>. The methanol extract of <em>H. atra</em> obtained from the Indian Ocean showed anti-proliferative activity against HeLa (90% cell inhibition), MCF-7 (75% inhibition), Hep2, and T47D cell lines<sup><a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>,<a href="#CR58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR58">58</a>–<a href="#CR60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR60">60</a></sup>. Another study<sup><a href="#CR61" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR61">61</a></sup> reported on the cytotoxic activity of <em>H. atra</em> on four cell lines at concentrations ranging between 9.6 and 14.3 µg/ml through induction of apoptosis. The lanostane-type triterpenes, impatienside A and bivittoside D, isolated from <em>H. impatiens</em> showed cytotoxic activities comparable to the standard drug etoposide when tested on seven cancer cell lines with IC<sub>50</sub> values between 0.37 and 2.75 µg/ml<sup><a href="#CR62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR62">62</a></sup>.</p>

<p id="Par35">A bioactive molecular network was performed to have an overview on the main class of components that could be responsible for the cytotoxicity observed for the dichloromethane fraction. LC-qTOF-MS/MS analysis was done on the fraction (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>) to figure out its metabolome content. After processing the mass data with MZmine-2, the relative intensities of all obtained ions and the observed cell viability results were uploaded to the workflow of the bioactive molecular network (<a href="https://github.com/DorresteinLaboratory/Bioactive_Molecular_Networks" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://github.com/DorresteinLaboratory/Bioactive_Molecular_Networks</a>) where the bioactivity score of each ion was calculated. These scores were introduced to the FBMN (feature-based molecular network) generated by GNPS where nodes with high bioactivity score (<em>r</em> &gt; 0.7, <em>p</em>-value &lt; 0.05) could be considered as viable cytotoxic candidates. As displayed in the bioactive molecular network (Fig. <a href="#Fig7" class="usa-link">7</a>), the cluster of saponins is the one in which almost all its nodes have bigger size suggesting that triterpenes might contribute to the cytotoxicity of the dichloromethane fraction. By comparing the <em>m/z</em> features in the dichloromethane fraction compared to other fractions, triterpenes such as bivittosides D (<em>m/z</em> 1425 as [M-H]<sup>−</sup> or <em>m/z</em> 1471 as [M+HCOO]<sup>−</sup>) and C (<em>m/z</em> 1409 as [M-H]<sup>−</sup> or <em>m/z</em> 1455 as [M+HCOO]<sup>−</sup>) seem to contribute to the cytotoxicity of the dichloromethane, ethylacetate, and aqueous ethanolic fractions since bivittosides C and D were more abundant in these fractions compared to the relatively less active butanol fraction. Bivittosides C and D were totally absent in the hexane fraction which was inactive. Glycerolipids with <em>m/z</em> values at 526, 554, and 552 might not contribute to the cytotoxic effect of the fractions since they displayed low relative abundance in the aqueous ethanolic fraction which displayed comparable cytotoxic activity to that of the ethyl acetate fraction enriched in glycerolipids.</p>

<p id="Par38">Network pharmacological investigations were carried out on bivittosides C and D present in the bioactive dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, and aqueous ethanolic fractions of <em>H. impatiens</em> viscera. A total of 104 targets were obtained and a network was created between the screened compounds and their targets (Fig. 19S) to visualize the inter-relationship between them. Cancer-related targets were intersected with the compounds targets to yield 99 common potential therapeutic (Fig. 20S) targets which were used as an input to construct protein-protein interaction (PPI) network (Fig. 21S) having 98 nodes (representing the common target proteins identified by STRING), 509 edges (representing the total interactions between these proteins), 10.4 as average node degree (representing the average number of proteins interacting with each node), minimum required interaction score at 0.4 (representing a medium confidence level - a measure of how likely STRING judges an interaction between two proteins to be true given the available evidence), an average local clustering coefficient of 0.522 (the higher the values, the higher the connectivity of the nodes to each other), and the PPI enrichment p-value was &lt; 1.0e<sup>− 16</sup>. The network density was 0.119 (representing how densely the network is populated with edges. Density values range between 0 and 1) and a characteristic path length (average shortest path length connecting two nodes) of 2.42. The edge thickness correlated with the confidence degree and the strength of data support. Central hub proteins were extracted from the common targets using Cytoscape plugins such as CytoNCA and cytoHubba where the genes are ranked based on their degree. The top 15 hub proteins (calssified based on their degree) are displayed in Fig. 22S. Functional annotation analysis revealed that the main biological processes (BP) in which hub genes are involved include the regulation of cellular proliferation as well as the response of cells to chemical and/or external stimuli (Fig. 23S). These hub genes were found to be involved in major molecular functions (MF) including the binding of identical proteins, signaling receptors, integrins, and enzymes. KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 24S) showed the involvement of the identified hub genes in several pathways of cancer, in atherosclerosis, interleukin and PI<sub>3</sub>K-Akt signaling. Reactome pathway analysis showed the involvement of hub genes in signaling related to interleukins, cytokines, and receptor tyrosine kinase.</p>
<p id="Par39">PI3K-Akt is one of the crucial pathways for cell proliferation, migration, cell cycle, and apoptosis. It is most frequently upregulated in several types of cancers (probably due to mutations) contributing to the hallmarks of tumor microenvironment such as angiogenesis, autophagy, apoptosis, resistance to drugs, inflammation, and epithelial to mesenchymal transition<sup><a href="#CR63" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR63">63</a></sup>. The ErbB receptors family are receptor tyrosine kinases and include EGFR (ErbB1/HER1), ErbB2 (neu, HER2), ErbB3 (HER3) and ErbB4 (HER4). They have been likewise linked to cancer pathogenesis as they are often amplified, mutated, or overexpressed in cancer<sup><a href="#CR64" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR64">64</a>,<a href="#CR65" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR65">65</a></sup>. These results were in accordance with those previously reported about triterpenes such as holothurin A and echinoside A to downregulate PI3K/Akt/β-catenin pathway<sup><a href="#CR66" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR66">66</a>,<a href="#CR67" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR67">67</a></sup>. The interference with these pathways was found to induce apoptosis and inhibit angiogenesis and metastasis, hence could be of potential use in the design of new anticancer agents.</p></section><section id="Sec21"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Molecular docking studies</h3>
<p id="Par40">To further validate the assumptions of network pharmacology results, a molecular docking study (Table 1S) was conducted with bivittosides C and D on STAT3 (the first hub gene based on its degree) and the co-crystallized ligand was used to identify the binding pocket and as a control. Bivittosides C and D showed binding energies at -8.6 and − 8.3 kcal/mol, respectively, comparable to the control which displayed ΔG at -8.7 kcal/mol (Table <a href="#Tab5" class="usa-link">5</a>). Docking was likewise performed on Akt1 and EGFR (as one of receptor tyrosine kinases) where bivittosides C and D showed ΔG of -10.6 and − 10.1 kcal/mol (for Akt1) and ΔG of -10.4 and − 10.2 kcal/mol (for EGFR), respectively. The docking results further validated the cytotoxicity assays and highlighted the role of triterpenes as possible key players for the observed cytotoxic activity. The interactions between the ligands and proteins are displayed in Fig. <a href="#Fig8" class="usa-link">8</a>. Binding affinities and RMSD values of the controls are presented in Table 2S.</p>

<p id="Par43">Biochemical investigations on three underexplored Red Sea Holothurian species revealed different distributions of triterpenes in their body walls and internal organs with higher relative abundance of saponins in the muscular walls of <em>H. atra</em> and <em>B. vitiensis</em> yet the viscera of <em>H. impatiens</em> showed relatively high abundance of triterpenes. In vitro cytotoxicity studies using CCK-8 and SRB assays revealed superiority of the visceral extract of <em>H. impatiens</em> on DLD-1, LoVo, PC3, HFB-4, and H-460 cell lines. Bioassay-guided fractionation revealed high cytotoxicity for the dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, and aqueous ethanolic fractions of <em>H. impatiens</em> viscera. The butanol fraction was less active while the hexane fraction was almost inactive. Bioactive molecular networking suggested triterpenes to be the class most probably contributing to the observed activity. LC-qTOF-MS analysis of the dichloromethane fraction showed the presence of bivittosides C and D as common triterpenes in the bioactive fractions however they had lower abundance in the butanol fraction while totally absent in the hexane fraction. Network pharmacology predicted the top 15 hub targets of bivittosides C and D among them STAT3, IL1B, Jun, and PTGS2 which are involved in different cancer signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt and receptor tyrosine kinases. Docking investigations of bivittosides C and D showed good binding energies on STAT3, Akt, and EGFR, hence supporting the network pharmacology results. The study highlights the therapeutic and nutritional values of the Red Sea cucumbers.</p></section><section id="Sec23"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h2>
