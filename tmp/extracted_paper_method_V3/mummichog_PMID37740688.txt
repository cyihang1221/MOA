>Materials and methods   Study participants   Participants were part of the Environmental and Reproductive Health (EARTH) study ( Messerlian  etal. , 2018 ). Briefly, the EARTH study was a prospective cohort study that examined how dietary and environmental factors influence fertility. Women attending the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) Fertility Center (2005–2019) for infertility evaluation or treatment were invited to participate. At enrollment, participants completed a questionnaire about their diet, environmental exposures, and reproductive health. Participants also had their height and weight measured by trained study staff to calculate BMI (kg/m 2 ). The Institutional Review Boards at MGH and the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health approved this study. All participants provided written informed consent.   Two hundred women undergoing a fresh autologous ART cycle between 2005 and 2016 were randomly selected for a serum metabolomics sub-study, as previously described ( Gaskins  etal. , 2021 ). Women randomly selected for this sub-study were similar in terms of demographic and ART cycle characteristics to all the eligible women ( Gaskins  etal. , 2021 ). Of these 200 participants, 125 women also had a follicular fluid sample taken from the first follicle that was clear (uncontaminated with blood), which was analyzed for metabolomics. Follicles were considered dominant if they were >16 mm on the last ultrasound performed the day of hCG trigger. During retrieval, fluid was aspirated in individual tubes for study purposes from three of the largest dominant follicles, in no particular order. Outcomes of controlled ovarian stimulation   Women in the study underwent one of three controlled ovarian stimulation protocols on Day 3 of induced menses after completing a cycle of oral contraceptives: (i) luteal phase GnRH-agonist protocol, (ii) follicular phase GnRH-agonist/Flare protocol, or (iii)GnRH-antagonist protocol. Protocol selection was based on clinical indication with luteal-phased GnRH agonist being used for normal/high responders, follicular phase GnRH agonist/Flare for low responders, and GnRH-antagonists for normal/low responders. Among patients pre-treated with oral contraceptive pills, the GnRH antagonist protocol was started when a dominant follicle reached ≥14 mm or estradiol was >1000 pg/ml. Among patients without pre-treatment with oral contraceptive pills, GnRH antagonist protocol was started when a dominant follicle reached ≥12 mm or estradiol >400 pg/ml. In patients utilizing a GnRH agonist protocol, Lupron (AbbVie Inc., North Chicago, IL, USA) was started in the luteal phase of the preceding cycle and was continued until hCG trigger day. In the Flare protocol, GnRH agonist was administered at a dose of 1 mg between Days 2 and 4 of the cycle and subsequently decreased to 0.25 mg. Stimulation was started on Day 3 per routine. During gonadotropin treatment, women were monitored for serum estradiol levels (Elecsys Estradiol II reagent kit, Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA), follicle size measurements and counts, and endometrial thickness and pattern. hCG was administered intramuscularly ∼35–36 h before the scheduled egg retrieval procedure to induce oocyte maturity. All visible follicles that were accessible by the needle were aspirated. Per study protocols, the first three dominant follicles were chosen for follicular fluid collection. We investigated two markers of response to controlled ovarian stimulation: peak estradiol levels (E 2 ) and number of mature oocytes collected. The measurement of these outcomes has been described elsewhere ( Gaskins  etal. , 2019 ). In brief, peak serum E 2  concentration was defined as the highest level of E 2  preceding the oocyte retrieval and obtained on the day of hCG administration. Following oocyte retrieval, a trained embryologist classified the retrieved oocytes as either germinal vesicle, metaphase I, metaphase II, or degenerated. The number of mature oocytes was the sum of all metaphase II oocytes. Blood and follicular fluid collection   During gonadotropin treatment, most commonly (70%) on Day 3 of the IVF cycle protocol, ∼6 ml of blood was collected at a routine appointment between 7 and 10 a.m. At blood draw, women were not required to be fasting. Samples allowed to clot for 20–60 min were centrifuged at 1040 g  for 20 min at room temperature and stored at −20°C at MGH until being transferred to Harvard School of Public Health to be stored at −80°C. During oocyte retrieval, a follicular fluid sample was taken from a woman’s first three follicles with a 16-G needle. Each sample was collected in a separate tube prepared with 1 ml of flushing media. Once the oocytes were removed, the follicular fluid was centrifuged at 1040 g  for 10 min at room temperature to separate the supernatant and pellet. The resulting aliquots were then stored at −80°C. To reduce variability, we used follicular fluid taken from the first large follicle collected. Metabolomic analysis   Serum and follicular fluid supernatant samples were shipped in separate batches, on dry ice, to Emory University for metabolomics analysis. We utilized established protocols for feature extraction that has been previously used for non-fasting samples and has been described elsewhere ( Go  etal. , 2015 ;  Liang  etal. , 2018 ,  2019 ;  Li  etal. , 2021 ;  Hood  etal. , 2022b ;  Hwang  etal. , 2022 ). Briefly, samples were treated with two volumes of acetonitrile and were analyzed in triplicate. Samples were randomized prior to analysis to minimize batch effects. Serum and follicular fluid samples were analyzed separately using liquid chromatography with high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS; Dionex Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano; Thermo Orbitrap Fusion). To facilitate greater feature detection and separation, we used two chromatography columns, the C18 hydrophobic chromatography column (Higgins Targa C18; 2.1 × 50 mm 2 ; 3-µm particle size) with negative electrospray ionization (ESI) (-30kV) and the hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) column (Waters XBridge BEH Amide XP HILIC; 2.1 × 50 mm 2 ; 2.6-µm particle size) with positive electrospray ionization (ESI) (3.5 kV). The sheath and auxiliary gases were set at 30 (arbitrary units) and 5 (arbitrary units) for the C18 column and 45 (arbitrary units) and 25 (arbitrary units) for the HILIC column, respectively. With each batch, we used two quality control samples, NIST 1950 ( Simón-Manso  etal. , 2013 ) and pooled human plasma (Equitech Bio), which has been described elsewhere ( Go  etal. , 2015 ;  Liu  etal. , 2020 ). Briefly, pooled human plasma assisted with batch correction while NIST 1950 samples, across batches, assisted with signal intensity normalization relative to a calibrated reference. Furthermore, inclusion of NIST 1950 samples was used to improve intra- and inter-laboratory comparison. We used ProteoWizard to convert raw data files to .mzML files using apLCMS and xMSanalyzer ( Yu etal. , 2009 ;  Chambers  etal. , 2012 ;  Uppal  etal. , 2013 ). Unique features were characterized based on the mass-to-charge ratio ( m / z ), retention time, and ion intensity. Features that appeared in <10% of samples were removed. We also removed features with a median coefficient of variation among the triplicates >30% and a Pearson correlation <0.7. Average intensities of the remaining features were natural log-transformed to normalize these data and allow for analysis with conventional statistical methods. Statistical analysis   We utilized generalized linear models to identify features associated with each of the two outcomes of controlled ovarian stimulation,   Y j i = α   +   β 1 j Oocyte i   +   β 2 j BMI i   +   β 3 j Age i   +   β 4 j Female i +   β 5 j Unexplained i   +   β 6 j Protocol i   +   e i j   In these models,  Y ji  was the natural log of the intensity for feature  j  and participant  i . Oocyte ik  was woman  i ’s controlled ovarian stimulation outcome (e.g. estradiol level or mature oocytes). These models also included the woman’s BMI (BMI i ), age (Age i ), initial infertility diagnosis (Female i ; Unexplained i  vs Male), and ovarian stimulation protocol (Protocol i ; luteal phase GnRH agonist vs other).  E ij  denotes the residual normal error. Covariates were selected based on  a priori  knowledge and biological relevance. Separate models were used for the C18-negative and HILIC-positive columns. We identified significant features at  P -value <0.05. Given the high number of statistical tests, we also corrected these raw  P -values for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure at two thresholds ( q -value: <0.20 and <0.05). We used volcano plots todescribe the magnitude and statistical significance of the associationsbetween the metabolic features and each controlled ovarian stimulation outcome. Pathway analysis   Pathway analysis was completed using mummichog (v. 1.0.10) which has been described and validated elsewhere ( Li  etal. , 2013 ). Briefly, mummichog computes biological pathways from a feature list using mass-to-charge ratio and retention time without prior metabolite identification. An adjusted  P -value for each pathway is calculated by resampling the reference input file using a gamma distribution ( Li  etal. , 2013 ). We utilized a reference file for each chromatography column (C18 and HILIC) with the file consisting of features with a raw  P -value <0.05. We examined pathways with  P -values <0.05 for any of the two outcomes and compared the significance and number of matched metabolites in each pathway. In this analysis, pathways could have the same number of overlapping features but different pathway  P -values and pathway sizes because of the different number of significant underlying features in the reference file and the differing number of features detected initially. Heat maps were used to visually compare the pathways across each outcome and each biological matrix. Feature annotation and confirmation   For chemical annotation of significant metabolic features ( P  < 0.05), we tentatively matched these features to authentic standards analyzed in the same manner and with the same chromatography columns in our lab ( Sumner  etal. , 2007 ). Significant features were matched with authentic standards (level-1 evidence) ( Sumner  etal. , 2007 ) by comparing their  m / z . We further screened annotated features by their retention times and those with retention times that differed by more than 30 s with the authentic standards were excluded. If features matched to multiple metabolites based on m/z and retention time, we selected those with the closest retention times. We further screened these matches on their retention time, isotope patterns, and peak quality by examining the extracted ion chromatograph to further minimize false positive matches. Only those with high-quality peaks were considered high-quality matches. We then used the Human Metabolome Database to identify the superclass and class of each matched metabolite.   Response to ovarian stimulation   Since poor response and hyper response to ovarian stimulation are not an optimal outcome either, we further evaluated whether there was any evidence of a non-monotonic association between the level-1 metabolites identified and ovarian response categories defined as poor (<4 mature oocytes, n = 9), normal (4–18 mature oocytes, n = 110), and high (>18 mature oocytes, n = 6). We used ANOVA testing to compare the difference in mean concentrations for each metabolite by ovarian response category (α = 0.05). Results   Sample characteristics   Women included in this sample had a mean age of 35 years (SD: 3.7) and a mean BMI of 24.0 (SD: 4.6) kg/m 2  ( Table 1 ). Most of the cycles occurred between 2009 and 2012 (62%; n = 77) and the most common initial infertility diagnosis was unexplained (43%; n = 54). The median peak estradiol level was 1968 pmol/l (range: 591–4051 pmol/l) and the median number of mature oocytes retrieved was 9 (range: 1–27). The Spearman correlation between peak estradiol and number of mature oocytes was 0.63.   Table 1. Significant features   In the C18 column, 10 803 unique features were observed in the serum metabolome after data quality assurance, of which 480 and 437 were significantly associated ( P  < 0.05) with peak estradiol levels and number of mature oocytes, respectively ( Fig. 1 ). In the same column, 14 394 unique features were observed in the follicular fluid metabolome after data quality assurance, of which 752 and 993 were significantly associated ( P  < 0.05) with peak estradiol levels and the number of mature oocytes, respectively. In the HILIC column, 12 968 unique features were observed in the serum metabolome, of which 850 and 538 weresignificantly associated ( P  < 0.05) were peak estradiol levels and number of mature oocytes, respectively ( Fig. 2 ). In thesame column, 17 161 unique features were found in the follicularfluid metabolome, of which 929 and 986 were significantly associated ( P  < 0.05) with peak estradiol levels and thenumber of mature oocytes, respectively. Pathway analysis   Peak estradiol levels were associated with eight pathways and ten pathways in the C18 column and the HILIC column, respectively. In the C18 column, two pathways occurred in the serum metabolome and six occurred in the follicular fluid metabolome ( Table 2 ). The two serum metabolome pathways were all fatty acid pathways (fatty acid metabolism and polyunsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis). In the follicular fluid, alanine and aspartate metabolism, androgen and estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism, C21-steroid hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, bile acid biosynthesis, prostaglandin formation from dihomo gama-linoleic acid, and vitamin B3 metabolism were associated with peak estradiol levels. In the HILIC column, six pathways occurred in the serum metabolome and six pathways occurred in the follicular fluid metabolome ( Table 3 ). In the serum metabolome, alkaloid biosynthesis II, phosphatidylinositol phosphate metabolism, biopterin metabolism, and vitamin E metabolism were associated with the peak estradiol levels. In the follicular fluid metabolome, butanoate metabolism, androgen and estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism, C21-steroid hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, and vitamin D3 metabolism were associated with the peak estradiol levels. Between the two columns, androgen and estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism, C21-steroid hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, and bile acid biosynthesis were associated with peak estradiol levels in the follicular fluid metabolome while no pathways associated with peak estradiol levels overlapped in the serum metabolome. Across the two matrices, carnitine shuttle and bile acid biosynthesis were associated with peak estradiol levels in the HILIC column. No overlap was observed between the two matrices in the C18 column.   Table 2.
