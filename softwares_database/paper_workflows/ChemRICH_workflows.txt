========== paper_workflow entry 1 ==========
related_tool: ChemRICH
sample_description: The study used venous blood samples collected from 69 pairs of type 2 diabetic patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR) and without DR (DM), matched via propensity score matching (PSM) based on age, gender, BMI, and HbA1c. All participants fasted for over 8 hours prior to blood collection. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 2000 rpm, 4°C, for 10 minutes, and 1.5 mL aliquots were stored in sterile tubes at −86°C for metabolomic analysis. A total of 613 metabolites were detected using UPLC-MS/MS (318 in positive ion mode, 295 in negative ion mode); after preprocessing—including removal of metabolites with CV >30% in QC samples, missing ratio >20% in either group, and imputation of missing values (<20% missing) by half the lowest detected peak area—461 metabolites remained for downstream analysis. Log transformation and Pareto scaling were applied to improve data normality and comparability.
complete_workflow_description: Metabolomic data processing and analysis were jointly performed using RStudio version 1.2.5042 and MetaboAnalyst version 5.0. Raw UPLC-MS/MS data were acquired using Analyst Software V.1.6.3 and processed in a widely targeted manner using MultiQuant Software for conversion, peak detection, retention time correction, and peak alignment. Statistical analysis included paired t-test with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction for differential metabolite identification; PLS-DA with 1000-permutation testing was conducted to compute VIP scores. DEMs were defined by FDR-adjusted q-value < 0.05, |fold change| > 1.2 or < 0.8, and VIP > 1. Hierarchical clustering heatmap of top 25 DEMs used Euclidean distance metric and Ward’s clustering method. Metabolic pathway analysis (MetPA) employed hypergeometric test (over-representation analysis) and out-degree centrality (pathway topology analysis) against the KEGG database (ko01100 reference pathway). Integrated pathway analysis used MetaMapp to combine biochemical pathways (KEGG) and chemical relationships (PubChem), visualized in Cytoscape (Saito et al., 2012; Fan et al., 2020). ChemRICH analysis followed Barupal and Fiehn (2017) methodology, with parameters including PubChem as background, FDR < 0.01 for cluster significance, and RCS regression with adjustment for clinical covariates.

========== paper_workflow entry 2 ==========
related_tool: ChemRICH
sample_description: The study recruited 211 cirrhotic outpatients prospectively from GI and Hepatology Clinics at Virginia Commonwealth University and McGuire VA Medical Centers between October 2015 and November 2016. Cirrhosis diagnosis was confirmed via liver biopsy, cirrhotic liver on imaging, frank decompensation (ascites, hepatic encephalopathy [HE], prior variceal bleeding), or evidence of varices in patients with chronic liver disease. Exclusion criteria included unclear cirrhosis diagnosis, prior organ transplant, HIV infection, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), inability to consent, inability to provide biological samples, or unwillingness to allow follow-up reviews. Fasting morning serum samples were collected from all 211 participants; urine samples were obtained from 164 participants (35 non-urine donors were on dialysis, others declined). All participants provided written informed consent following IRB approval (approval numbers BAJAJ004, BAJAJ015, HM13191, HM13466). Samples were used for multi-variate metabolomics analysis targeting outcomes over a 90-day follow-up: non-elective hospitalizations, overt HE (defined as West-Haven grade ≥2), liver transplantation, and death.
complete_workflow_description: Metabolomics analysis was conducted at the NIH West Coast Metabolomics Center using published gas chromatography–time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC-TOF MS) techniques. Raw data underwent generalized log10 transformation and auto-scaling prior to statistical modeling. Multivariate logistic regressions were performed via the web-based platform Metabox, with each single metabolite modeled against each clinical outcome while adjusting for covariates: age, gender, diabetes, alcoholic etiology, prior HE, PPI use, SBP prophylaxis, and rifaximin use. Significance was defined as p<0.05, with regression coefficients indicating effect size; Benjamini-Hochberg correction controlled false discovery rate (FDR). Two primary analytical workflows were implemented: (1) Chemical similarity enrichment using ChemRICH (as described above); and (2) Predictive modeling using partial least squares–discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) implemented in Metabox or compatible software, where adjusted urine/serum metabolite profiles and MELD scores were separately used to build outcome-specific classifiers. Model performance was evaluated via leave-one-out cross-validation, and predictive power was quantified by area under the ROC curve (AUC), with statistical comparison of AUCs across models. Additionally, PCA (principal component analysis) and permutation testing were performed to validate PLS-DA model robustness and assess separation between outcome groups.

========== paper_workflow entry ==========
related_tool: ChemRICH
sample_description: The study analyzed plasma samples from 220 mice, including 30 unique gene knockout strains (each with three male and three female mice) and 40 strain-matched wild-type C57BL/6NCrl controls. Additional quality control samples included 20 human pooled plasma samples (from BioIVT) and up to 10 method blanks per assay. All mouse plasma samples were obtained from the International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium (IMPC), shipped on dry ice, and stored at −80 °C until analysis at the West Coast Metabolomics Center. Samples were processed across seven analytical platforms: GC-TOFMS, HILIC-POS, HILIC-NEG, CSH-POS, CSH-NEG, targeted oxylipin LC-MS/MS, and targeted bile acid/steroid LC-MS/MS.
complete_workflow_description: The end-to-end metabolomics workflow consisted of: (1) sample acquisition and metadata curation (IMPC-sourced mouse plasma, human pool QC, blanks); (2) multi-platform analytical acquisition — GC-TOFMS for primary metabolites, five LC-MS methods (HILIC-POS, HILIC-NEG, CSH-POS, CSH-NEG, and two targeted MRM assays for oxylipins and bile acids/steroids); (3) raw data conversion to standardized formats (mzML via ProteoWizard MSConvert; ChromaTOF .peg/.txt/.cdf); (4) platform-specific preprocessing — ChromaTOF v4.0 for GC-TOFMS (no smoothing, 3 s peak width, baseline subtraction, S/N ≥ 5:1 deconvolution); BinBase (rtx5) for GC-TOFMS spectral filtering and RI-based identification; manual peak integration in MultiQuant v3.0.2 for targeted assays (Gaussian smooth width = 1.0, retention half window = 10–15 s, min peak width = 8 points, min peak height = 750, noise = 40%, baseline subwindow = 1.7 min, peak splitting = 3 points); (5) signal extraction — apex-based peak height from extracted ion chromatograms (m/z tolerance ±0.005 Da, RT tolerance ±0.10 min); (6) quality filtering — removal of features with >50% missing values or >50% RSD across technical replicates; (7) normalization — SERRF (random forest-based) for GC-TOFMS; median-batch normalization for HILIC-NEG; no normalization for HILIC-POS, CSH-POS, and CSH-NEG due to absence of batch effects; (8) annotation using curated databases and MoNA spectral library; (9) metabolite-level quantification (peak height or molar concentration via calibration curves); (10) structural classification using ChemRICH; and (11) data deposition — raw files (NIH Metabolomics Workbench ST001154), processed matrices (Supplemental Tables S11–S15), annotation files (S3–S9), and R scripts (GitHub: https://github.com/barupal).
source_paper: K et al. (2019), Metabolites, DOI: 10.3390/metabo9050101, PMID: 31121816

========== paper_workflow entry ==========
related_tool: ChemRICH
sample_description: Metabolomics data from 251 breast tumor samples (192 estrogen receptor positive [ERpos] and 59 estrogen receptor negative [ERneg]) in the MetaCancer cohort, measured using gas chromatography/time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC-TOFMS); the dataset included 470 detected metabolites (161 identified, 309 unidentified), of which 164 were significantly different between ERpos and ERneg groups (raw p-value < 0.05).
complete_workflow_description: The study employed an integrated multi-omics bioinformatics workflow (Figure 1) consisting of the following sequential steps: (1) Univariate statistical testing (Mann-Whitney U test for metabolomics and proteomics; GEO2R for transcriptomics) to identify significantly different features (metabolites, proteins, transcripts) between ERpos and ERneg tumors in the MetaCancer cohort and eight additional GEO datasets; (2) Meta-analysis across nine transcriptomics datasets using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test on raw p-value distributions to identify consistently dysregulated metabolic genes; (3) Annotation of human genes with enzyme commission (EC) numbers using Expasy and NCBI to define a set of 1549 metabolic enzymes; (4) Reactome pathway over-representation analysis on significantly dysregulated molecular features (542 genes, 104 proteins, 56 metabolites) to identify enriched metabolic pathways; (5) Biochemical network mapping using MetaMapp to integrate metabolites, proteins, and enzymes via KEGG and Expasy databases, visualized in Cytoscape; (6) Text mining via PubMed searches (using NCBI eutils) to count abstracts linking each metabolic gene to 'breast cancer', enabling ranking of under-studied yet significant genes. All statistical analyses were performed in R, and the workflow explicitly prioritized poorly studied metabolic genes with strong multi-omics evidence of association with ERneg phenotype.
source_paper: Kumar et al. (2019), Oncotarget, DOI: 10.18632/oncotarget.26995, PMID: 31231467

related_tool: ChemRICH
sample_description: Breast tissue samples from breast cancer (BC) patients and healthy controls, collected through the Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (CSMC) Biobank under IRB approval (Pro00044997). The study used untargeted metabolomic profiling via GC-TOF-MS and HILIC-MS platforms; however, the specific number of BC vs. control samples, tissue processing details (e.g., snap-freezing), and clinical metadata (e.g., tumor subtype, stage, grade) are not explicitly stated in the provided text.
complete_workflow_description: The end-to-end workflow begins with human breast tissue samples from BC patients and healthy controls, extracted using a solvent mixture of acetonitrile/isopropanol/water (3:3:2), followed by centrifugation, drying, and reconstitution. Metabolite profiling was performed using two platforms: GC-TOF-MS (with Restek Rtx-5Sil MS column, Leco Pegasus IV MS, 80–500 Da range, −70 eV ionization) and HILIC-MS (platform mentioned but no technical details provided). Raw GC-TOF data were preprocessed in ChromaTOF v2.32 to extract apex masses, then processed via the BinBase algorithm (rtx5) with strict filters: chromatogram validity (<10 peaks >10^7 counts/sec), retention index window ±2000 units, 5% base peak abundance cutoff, MS similarity >800 for marker detection, 5th-order polynomial RI regression, and isomer filtering. Failed spectra were curated into new database entries if S/N >25, purity <1.0, and presence >80% in study class. Peak detection was also performed untargetedly using MZmine 2.0 (up to 300 chromatograms), with targeted quantification and identification using Agilent MassHunter (with MS/MS, NIST14/Metlin/MassBank libraries) and manual validation against the Fiehn library. Metabolites were annotated to MSI Level 1 using authentic standards. Data were log2-transformed, mean-centered, and autoscaled; statistical analysis included Student’s t-test with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction, volcano plot filtering (fold-change >2 or <0.5, p<0.1), PCA, hierarchical clustering (Euclidean distance, Ward’s method), PLS-DA with permutation testing and Q2 evaluation, and SVM classification in MATLAB R2014a with 10-fold cross-validation and AUC-ROC assessment. Pathway and chemical similarity analyses employed ChemRICH, MetaMapp, and MSEA — though configuration details for these tools are omitted.
source_paper: Sili et al. (2020), Metabolites, DOI: 10.3390/metabo10040170, PMID: 32344578

========== paper_workflow entry ==========
related_tool: ChemRICH
sample_description: Tissue samples from 31 healthy pigs fed either a control grower diet or the same diet supplemented with three or six servings of freeze-dried white button mushroom (WBM) for six weeks. Tissues analyzed included pre-frontal cortex, hippocampus, and liver — each representing distinct neuroanatomical and metabolic compartments. Metabolite extraction and quantification were performed using targeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), yielding detection of 154–167 metabolites per tissue (out of 408 in the kit), above the limit of detection (LOD defined as three times background noise).
complete_workflow_description: Raw LC-MS metabolomics data were first preprocessed by replacing missing values with the minimum detectable value for each compound and removing metabolites with constant values within an organ. Metabolites below the limit of detection (LOD, defined as three times background noise) were excluded. Data were then subjected to multivariate statistical analysis: principal component analysis (PCA) was used for outlier detection; partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed per tissue to assess group separation, with variable importance in projection (VIP) scores calculated and metabolites with VIP > 1.0 considered influential; area under the ROC curve (AUC) was computed to evaluate classification performance. Differential metabolite analysis employed the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test to compare mean ranks between treatment and control groups, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction (thresholds: FDR < 0.05 for primary significance, FDR < 0.1 for exploratory findings). Fold change (FC) was calculated as the ratio of median metabolite abundance in treatment versus control groups. Heatmaps visualized FC patterns across tissues and diets. Finally, Chemical Similarity Enrichment analysis (ChemRICH) was applied to identify structurally related metabolite clusters showing coordinated up- or down-regulation, using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (p < 0.05) and bubble plot visualization. All statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.0.2; DESeq2 v1.28.1 was used for parallel differential gene expression analysis (though not part of the metabolomics workflow per se).
source_paper: I et al. (2021), Metabolites, DOI: 10.3390/metabo11110779, PMID: 34822437

========== paper_workflow entry ==========
related_tool: ChemRICH
sample_description: Two human prostate cancer cell lines were analyzed: LNCAP (prostate adenocarcinoma) and LASCPC-01 (prostate small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma), with three biological replicates per cell line. Cells were cultured under defined conditions—LNCAP in RPMI1640 + 10% FBS, and LASCPC-01 in HITES medium + 10% FBS—and subjected to untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics profiling using LC-MS. Metabolite and lipid features were extracted from LC-MS data, and only features present in at least 50% of samples within each cell line were retained for ChemRICH analysis.
complete_workflow_description: The study employed a multi-omics workflow beginning with culture and harvesting of three biological replicates each of LNCAP (adenocarcinoma) and LASCPC-01 (neuroendocrine carcinoma) cells. For transcriptomics: total RNA was extracted using Trizol/CHCl3 followed by RNeasy cleanup; strand-specific mRNA libraries were prepared using the Agilent SureSelect protocol (poly-A selection, fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation, 14-cycle PCR amplification); libraries were quality-checked on Bioanalyzer and sequenced on Illumina HiSeq (single-end 50 bp). Reads were aligned to GRCh37/hg19 using TopHat v2.0.9, transcript assembly and quantification performed with Cufflinks v2.1.1, normalization and differential expression analysis conducted via Cuffnorm and Cuffdiff (v2.1.1), reporting expression in FPKM. For metabolomics/lipidomics: LC-MS raw files were converted to ABF format using ABF Converter; peak picking, alignment, deconvolution, and identification were performed using MS-DIAL v2.94 with specified parameters (smoothing: linear-weighted moving average, smoothing level: 3 scans, min peak width: 5 scans, min peak height: 50,000, mass slice width: 0.1 Da, sigma window: 0.01); ion adducts, duplicates, and isotopes were resolved using MS-FLO; compound identification leveraged retention time–m/z and MS/MS spectral libraries from MassBank of North America; only features detected in ≥50% of samples per cell line were retained. Finally, ChemRICH was applied to the filtered metabolite/lipid feature tables to identify enriched metabolite clusters, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed on transcriptomic results.
source_paper: Bei et al. (2019), Metabolites, DOI: 10.3390/metabo9050082, PMID: 31035489
