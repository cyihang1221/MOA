</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="Par1">Brucellosis is a zoonosis prevalent worldwide and very recurrent in less developed or developing regions. This zoonosis affects livestock, generating high financial losses to producers, in addition to transmitting diseases to humans through meat consumption or handling contaminated products and animals. In this study, five extraction methods for <em>Brucella abortus</em> intracellular metabolites, using different solvent compositions and cell membrane disruption procedures, were evaluated. Derivatized extracts were analyzed by GC-HRMS. Raw data were processed in XCMS Online and the results were evaluated through multivariate statistical analysis using the MetaboAnalyst platform. The identification of the extracted metabolites was performed by the Unknowns software using the NIST 17.L library. The extraction performance of each method was evaluated for thirteen representative metabolites, comprising four different chemical classes. Most of these compounds are reported in the cell membrane composition of Gram-negative bacteria. The method based on extraction with methanol/chloroform/water presented the best performance in the evaluation of the extracted compounds and in the statistical results. Therefore, this method was selected for extracting intracellular metabolites from cultures of <em>Brucella abortus</em> for untargeted metabolomics analysis.</p>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h3>
<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s42770-023-01001-6.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> <em>Brucella abortus</em>, Extraction method, Intracellular metabolites, Untargeted metabolomics, Gas chromatography, High resolution mass spectrometry</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par2">Brucellosis is a zoonosis caused by bacteria of the genus <em>Brucella</em>, which is listed as a notifiable disease in the World Organization for Animal Health (OIE) [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>], and as a bioterrorism agent, category B, in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>]. According to the CDC, pathogens from this category are moderately easy to spread, cause moderate morbidity, and have low mortality rates. <em>Brucella</em> bacteria have environmental resistance and difficult therapeutic treatment. As an easily spread infectious disease, brucellosis is rapidly transmitted between herds (epizootic disease) and from herds to humans, affecting public health and the economy [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>–<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>]. A review of the current evidence on the existing links between metabolism and virulence of <em>Brucella</em> was published by researchers [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>]. The authors reported that <em>Brucella</em> virulence depends on its ability to modulate the immune response and physiology of host cells; therefore, investigating the metabolism of <em>Brucella</em> in the host is crucial to decipher the infectious strategies of this bacteria [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>]. In this context, “omics” sciences have begun to be explored in order to investigate changes in the metabolism and biochemical composition of <em>Brucella</em> cells. For example, Tang et al. [<a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>] have used metabolite analysis and proteomics to differentiate biofilm and planktonic <em>Brucella abortus</em> cultured in different media. Bacterial cells, such as those of <em>Brucella abortus</em>, are capable of living in a biofilm or planktonic state [<a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>]. The authors [<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>] analyzed serum metabolites from mice infected with <em>Brucella abortus</em> and uninfected, identifying 21 metabolites that contributed to the separation between the study groups. The authors verified that the level of lysophosphatidylcholines, which are lysophospholipids that make up the plasmatic membrane, could be an indication of the occurrence of infection with <em>Brucella abortus</em> [<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>].</p>
<p id="Par3">Microbial metabolomics studies can help to improve understanding of the metabolic processes affected by a disease or treatment, degree of virulence, pathogen growth, interaction with the host, and environmental exposure or genetic modifications in biological systems. The identification of these metabolic response pathways allows researchers to discover novel biomarkers and understand biochemical mechanisms, which can help to improve the tools available for early diagnosis and monitoring of diseases, as well as to develop new therapeutical targets [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>, <a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>].</p>
<p id="Par4">The bacterial metabolome is composed of several classes of metabolites, which have widely different physicochemical properties and concentrations. Therefore, the complexity of the system makes sample preparation a critical and important step in the metabolomics workflow, given that it determines the coverage of intracellular metabolites extracted from microorganisms. Given the diversity of extraction procedures described in the literature for different biological matrices, including cells and microorganisms, it is important to optimize the procedure for extracting intracellular metabolites in bacterial metabolomics studies, as inefficient extraction may compromise an overall biological interpretation [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>, <a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>].</p>
<p id="Par5">Therefore, developing a specific protocol for extracting intracellular metabolites from a given microorganism is an important step in order to obtain a more comprehensive and reliable metabolomics dataset [<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>, <a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>]. Within this context, in the present work, a procedure for the extraction of intracellular metabolites from bacterial cultures of <em>Brucella abortu</em>s was optimized for untargeted metabolomics analysis. Gas chromatography coupled to high resolution mass spectrometry (GC-HRMS) was used to detect extracted metabolites in derivatized extracts.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods
</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Chemicals and reagents</h3>
<p id="Par6">HPLC-grade acetonitrile, methanol and isopropanol were purchased, respectively, from Tedia (Fairfield, USA), J. T. Baker (Allentown, USA), and Honeywell (Charlotte, USA). <em>O</em>-methoxyamine was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (Buchs, Switzerland), whereas sodium chloride was obtained from Synth (Diadema, Brazil). Hydrochloric acid (37%), pyridine (≥ 99%), and triphenylmethane (99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, USA). Anhydrous chloroform (≥ 99%), methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE, 99.8%), activated<em> N</em>-Methyl-<em>N</em>-(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide I (MSTFA I), and tryptose agar culture medium (Difco) containing 5 to 10% bovine serum were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (São Paulo, Brazil).</p></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Bacterial strains</h3>
<p id="Par7">Pure cultures of the bacterium <em>Brucella abortus</em>, biovar 1, obtained from the bacteria bank of the <em>Laboratório Federal de Defesa Agropecuária of Pedro Leopoldo</em> in Minas Gerais (Brazil), were cultivated in Petri dishes containing 20 mL of enriched culture medium, composed of tryptose agar (Difco) containing 5 to 10% bovine serum. The plates were incubated under a 5% CO<sub>2</sub> atmosphere at a controlled temperature (37 °C) for 48 h. Three biological replicates were prepared for each extraction method. More replicates were not considered to be necessary as the bacteria cultivation occurs in a well-controlled environment, where little variability is expected.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Quenching and bacterial samples harvesting</h3>
<p id="Par8">Bacterial concentration was measured at 540 nm in a spectrophotometer Genesys 10 UV model from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, USA). Approximately 6 × 10<sup>9</sup> colony forming units (CFU) were collected from the surface of the plates into cryogenic tubes containing 1 mL of 70% (v/v) methanol solution at -20 °C for bacterial inactivation and quenching. Petri dishes containing the culture and the extraction solvent were kept on ice throughout the procedure.</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Extraction methods</h3>
<p id="Par9">Five methods for extracting intracellular metabolites from <em>Brucella abortus</em> were evaluated in this study, using three biological replicates. For all extraction, the solvents were prepared on the day of extraction and kept refrigerated at -20 °C before being added to the samples. All the methods used 1 mL of bacterial suspension of concentration 6 × 10<sup>9</sup> CFU mL<sup>−1</sup> and different cold extraction solutions (&lt; 0 °C), as described below (items 2.4.1 to 2.4.5).</p>
<section id="Sec7"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Extraction method 1 (M1)</h4>
<p id="Par10">The procedure was adapted from the Fiehn protocol [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>] and comprised the following steps: (a) the cell pellet was decanted by centrifugation (15,200 × g, -10 °C, 10 min) and the supernatant was discarded; (b) 1 mL of ice-cold acetonitrile:isopropanol:water 3:3:2 (v/v/v) was added to the pellet, which was then vortexed (10 s) for cell disruption and metabolite extraction; (c) the mixture was sonicated on an ice bath (sweet mode, 37 kHz, 1 min); and (d) after cooling in an ultrafreezer (-80 °C, 1 min), the mixture was homogenized in vortex (5 min), centrifuged (15,200 × g, -10 °C, 10 min), and the supernatant was collected.</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Extraction method 2 (M2)</h4>
<p id="Par11">The procedure was adapted from the Standard Operating Procedures (SOP-6) described by Goodacre et al. [<a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>], which involved the following steps: (a) the cell pellet was decanted by centrifugation (15,200 × g, -10 °C, 10 min) and the supernatant was discarded; (b) 700 μL of chilled methanol:water 4:1 (v/v) was added to the pellet, followed by vortexing (10 s); (c) the mixture was frozen in liquid nitrogen (1 min); (d) thawed on an ice bath (5 min) and then vortexed (30 s); (e) three freeze/thaw/vortex cycles were performed, as described in steps (c) and (d), for complete cell disruption; (f) the mixture was then centrifuged (15,200 × g, -10 °C, 10 min) and the supernatant was collected; and (g) 300 μL of methanol:water 4:1 (v/v) was added to the pellet, steps (c) to (f) were repeated, and the supernatant collected into the same flask containing the supernatant from the first extraction.</p></section><section id="Sec9"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Extraction method 3 (M3)</h4>
<p id="Par12">The same extraction solution described by t'Kind et al. [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>] was used in this procedure, which was performed according to the following steps: (a) the cell pellet was decanted by centrifugation (15,200 × g, -10 °C, 10 min) and the supernatant was discarded; (b) 1 mL of methanol:chloroform:water 3:1: 1 (v/v/v) was added to the pellet and the mixture was sonicated on an ice bath (sweet mode, 37 kHz, 1 min); (c) frozen on liquid nitrogen (1 min); (d) and thawed while vortexing (15 min); (e) four freeze/thaw cycles were performed, as described in steps (c) and (d), for complete cell disruption; and (f) the extract was centrifuged (15,200 × g, -10 °C, 10 min) and the supernatant was collected.</p></section><section id="Sec10"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Extraction method 4 (M4)</h4>
<p id="Par13">This procedure with phase separation was adapted from Matyash et al. [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>] and comprised of the following steps: (a) the cell pellet was decanted by centrifugation (15,200 × g, -10 °C, 10 min) and the supernatant was discarded; (b) 300 μL of methanol and 1 mL of MTBE were added to the pellet; (c) the mixture was vortexed (1 h); (d) 250 μL of ultrapure water was added and vortexed (10 min) again, followed by centrifugation (1000 × g, -10 °C, 10 min) for phase separation and collection of the upper layer (organic phase); (e) 400 μL of MTBE:methanol:water 10:3:2.5 (v/v/v) was added to the mixture for re-extraction of the aqueous phase; (f) the mixture was centrifuged (1000 × g, -10 °C, 10 min) and the organic phase (upper layer) was collected into the same flask containing the phase collected in the first extraction. The aqueous phase was collected into another flask. This extraction method involves a two-phase separation: an aqueous and an organic phase, which were named M4a and M4o, respectively.</p></section><section id="Sec11"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Extraction method 5 (M5)</h4>
<p id="Par14">This procedure consisted of the same steps described for M3, except that chloroform was substituted by MTBE in the extraction solution (i.e., the extraction solution was composed of methanol:MTBE:water 3:1:1 (v/v/v).</p></section></section><section id="Sec12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Extraction blanks</h3>
<p id="Par15">The extraction blanks of each method were prepared by replicating all extraction steps without the bacteria sample.</p></section><section id="Sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Derivatization</h3>
<p id="Par16">For derivatization, 200 μL of extract was collected in amber vials and concentrated to complete dryness on a SpeedVac operated at room temperature. In the methoximation step, 20 μL of <em>O</em>-methoxyamine in pyridine (10.18 mg mL<sup>−1</sup>) were added to the dry extracts. Then, the vials were vortexed for 10 s and transferred to a thermostatic bath (30 °C) protected from light. Every 15 min, the vials were vortexed simultaneously for 30 s and returned to the bath. This cycle was performed 6 times until completing 90 min. The vials were subsequently cooled to room temperature to avoid loss of volatile metabolites during the silylation step. For silylation, 70 μL of activated MSTFA I and 30 μL of pyridine were added to the vials, which was then vortexed for 10 s. Subsequently, the vials were kept warm in a thermostatic bath at 40 °C for 30 min [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>, <a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>].</p></section><section id="Sec14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">GC-HRMS analysis</h3>
<p id="Par17">Analysis of the derivatized extracts was performed on a gas chromatograph coupled to a quadrupole hyphenated to a time-of-flight mass spectrometer (GC/MS Q-TOF), model 7200B from Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, USA). As samples prepared for untargeted metabolomics analysis have several classes of compounds, the GC system was configured with two capillary columns. Separation was performed using a Rtx-OPPesticides2 capillary column of 30 m in length, 0.25 mm in internal diameter, and 0.25 μm in film thickness, with a film composition of 5% diphenyl/ 95% dimethylpolysiloxane (Restek, Bellefonte, USA). In addition to the separation column, located at the outlet of the injector, a hollow auxiliary column (0.8 m × 0.10 mm, Restek) was positioned in sequence and used for backflushing at the end of the separation. The Ultimate Purged Union, connected to an independent Electronic Pneumatic Control (EPC), made the connection between the two columns. Helium (99.99%) was used as carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL min<sup>−1</sup>. A solution of 0.60 μg mL<sup>−1</sup> triphenylmethane was prepared in pyridine to be used as an internal standard (IS) to evaluate the system performance throughout the sample injections. In addition, internal calibration of the MS system was performed after every three injections using perfluorotributylamine (PFTBA) standard in order to adjust the mass readings. An aliquot of 5 μL of each sample was injected with Injection Dispense Speed ​​of 25 μL min<sup>−1</sup>, using the autosampler in Solvent Vent mode with Split Vent purge flow of 60 mL min<sup>−1</sup>. The injection was performed in the sandwich mode with 1 μL of IS (5 μL of sample, 1 μL of air gap and 1 μL of IS solution). The injector temperature program was 45 ºC (kept for 1.37 min) and 45 to 325 ºC at 600 ºC min<sup>− 1</sup>, maintained until the end of the separation. The settings in Solvent Vent mode were: initial Vent Flow of 250 mL min<sup>−1</sup> and 5 psi of pressure until 1.35 min, Purge flow to Split Vent of 60 mL min<sup>−1</sup> at 2.61 min, and 20 mL min<sup>−1</sup> of flow after 4 min. The oven temperature program was 40 °C (1 min), 40–60 °C (70 °C min<sup>−1</sup>, held for 0.5 min), 60–170 °C (40 °C min<sup>−1</sup>) and 170–290 °C (10 °C min<sup>−1</sup>, held for 10 min), with a total separation time of 26.54 min. The electron ionization energy was 70 eV and the mass spectral acquisitions were in Scan mode from 45 to 1000 Da. The transfer line, filament source and quadrupole temperatures were 280 °C, 250 °C, and 180 °C, respectively.</p></section><section id="Sec15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data treatment and statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="Par18">Initially, raw data files (*.D) were converted to *.mzXML format by MSConvert GUI program using the Scan Time filter (analysis time range from 120 to 1500 s) to eliminate the solvent delay [<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>]. The converted data were processed on XCMS Online (<a href="https://xcmsonline.scripps.edu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://xcmsonline.scripps.edu/</a>), using the parameters recommended for GC-TOF [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>], and the bandwidth (bw) values of 1 and 5 were evaluated. After processing the data, the results obtained were treated on Excel to eliminate molecular features that presented relative standard deviation (RSD) greater than 30% among the triplicates. The generated data matrix was loaded into MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (<a href="https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/</a>) to perform data normalization as pre-processing and subsequent statistical treatment. The data pre-processing step included filtering by relative standard deviation (RSD), normalization by median, log transformation (base10) and Pareto scaling [<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>–<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>]. Metabolite annotations were performed on the software Unknowns (Agilent, Version B.10.1/Build 10.1.733.0) for deconvolution of MS spectra using NIST library (version 17.L). The Bioinformatics &amp; Evolutionary Genomics platform (<a href="http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/</a>) was used to create the Venn diagram as a tool for comparing the molecular features extracted by each method.</p></section></section><section id="Sec16"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="Sec17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data quality assessment</h3>
<p id="Par19">The quality of the data acquired on the GC-HRMS system was evaluated throughout the analytical sequence using the peak area of the internal standard triphenylmethane within each injection. A Shewhart chart (control chart), depicted in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>, revealed a random distribution of the internal standard peak areas around the mean value, with all points staying within the acceptable limits recommended by Gika et al. [<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>], which are based on the Westgard rules [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>]. It is observed that only two non-consecutive points exceeded the upper alert limit (<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IEq1"><math id="M2"><mrow><mover><mi>x</mi><mo>¯</mo></mover><mo>+</mo><mn>2</mn><mi>s</mi></mrow></math></span>), while none of the points reached the  <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IEq2"><math id="M4"><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mover><mi>x</mi><mo>¯</mo></mover><mo>±</mo><mn>3</mn><mi>s</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math></span> limit. This result indicates that the analysis was under control and the data acquired was not adversely affected by possible analytical variations along the injections.</p>

<p id="Par20">An initial data processing was performed on XCMS Online using recommended parameter for GC-TOF, while evaluating two algorithms for retention time correction (<em>obiwarp</em> and <em>peakgroups</em>) and two <em>bw</em> values (1 and 5). Both methods (M3 and M5) used the same physical processes for cell membrane disruption, differing only in the composition of the extraction solvent. Therefore, the nature of the extraction solvent is the factor that explains the difference in metabolic coverage between these two methods. Chloroform, used in method M3, has a higher polarity index (4.1) than MTBE (2.5), which was used in M5 [<a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>]. In addition, chloroform also presents approximately twice the value for partition ratio (log P = 1.97) of MTBE (log P = 0.94) [<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a>]. Therefore, these physicochemical properties likely confer the differences in selectivity between the extraction methods, guaranteeing chloroform greater extraction power when compared to MTBE [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>, <a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>].</p>

<figcaption><p>Venn diagram comparing features with RSD less than 30% by the different extraction methods evaluated</p></figcaption></figure></section><section id="Sec21"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Principal component analysis and hierarchical cluster analysis</h4>
<p id="Par23">For the features extracted by each method, two statistical treatments, using unsupervised multivariate analysis, were performed: principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA). These treatments were performed on MetaboAnalyst, where the pre-processing steps were also conducted, including data filtering, normalization, transformation, and dimensioning.</p>
<p id="Par24">The PCA score plot (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>A) shows that principal components one and two (PC1 and PC2) in combination explained 56.9% of the variation. M3 was clearly separated from the other extraction methods by PC2, showing a considerably different profile. Methods M1, M2 and M4a showed similarity in the metabolic profile that can be observed by the distribution of groups in the PCA graph (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>A). M4o showed the greatest variability within the triplicates. This variability can be explained by the difficulty in collecting a five times smaller volume of extractor solvent in a two-phase system. The volume reduction was an adaptation made to the original method [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>] to match the volumes used in the other evaluated methods.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="Fig3"><h5 class="obj_head">Fig. 3.</h5>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=10484873_42770_2023_1001_Fig3_HTML.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/84ac/10484873/f0eabe49598d/42770_2023_1001_Fig3_HTML.jpg" loading="lazy" id="MO3" height="333" width="676" alt="Fig. 3"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/Fig3/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p><strong>A</strong> PCA score plot of the extraction methods evaluated, where ellipses represent the 95% confidence interval (<em>n</em> = 3). <strong>B</strong> Dendrogram generated by HCA based on Euclidean distance and ward.D clustering algorithm of the study samples</p></figcaption></figure><p id="Par25">The dendrogram was based on the Euclidean distance, which measures the similarity between the samples and the <em>ward.D</em> clustering algorithm, so that groups (clusters) were formed according to the degree of similarity between the samples [<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>]. The result of the clustering (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>B), at the Euclidean distance of 60, shows a subdivision of the dataset into four major clusters. Group <strong>a</strong> comprised replicates 01 and 03 from M4o; group <strong>b</strong> contained the samples from M3; group <strong>c</strong> was formed by samples from M5 and replicate 02 from M4o; and group <strong>d</strong> included all the replicates of methods 1, 2 and 4a.</p>
<p id="Par26">Therefore, the separation between the methods observed in the PCA score plot was consistent with the distribution of the samples in the HCA. When looking to the clustering within the three biological replicates of each extraction method, it is observed that only M3, M4a and M5 had their triplicates grouped together, whereas M1 and M2 were intercalated with each other, demonstrating high similarity in the extracted set of metabolites, and M4o was quite dispersed in the dendrogram. The extraction method M3 was the only one that grouped all its triplicates separately from other methods (group <strong>b</strong>), which highlights a relatively differential extraction as well as a good repeatability within the triplicates.</p></section><section id="Sec22"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Classes of extracted metabolites</h4>
<p id="Par27">The features with RSD of peak areas under 30% were treated in the Unknowns Analysis software (Agilent Technologies), for feature identification with a minimum spectral matching score of 70% when using the NIST library (version 17.L). The spectral search revealed the presence of some features with known biological relevance, which can potentially help to better understand the infectious cycle involved in brucellosis. Thirteen representative metabolites from four classes (fatty acids, glycerides, carbohydrates, and aromatic compounds) are presented in Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>, which shows the peak areas of some identified metabolite and the respective standard deviation obtained for the biological triplicate, after applying each extraction method.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="Fig4"><h5 class="obj_head">Fig. 4.</h5>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=10484873_42770_2023_1001_Fig4_HTML.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/84ac/10484873/cbbecb1b99f5/42770_2023_1001_Fig4_HTML.jpg" loading="lazy" id="MO4" height="401" width="677" alt="Fig. 4"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/Fig4/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>Comparison of the peak areas obtained for the extracted metabolites for the four classes: fatty acids, glycerides, carbohydrates and aromatic compounds. Error bars indicate the standard error of the area mean from biological triplicates</p></figcaption></figure><p id="Par28">Within the four classes of compounds identified, long-chain fatty acids (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>) had the highest number of compounds extracted, among which (Z)-10-nonadecenoic acid, and vaccenic acid where the metabolites with the most intense peak areas. All methods were able to extract fatty acids, but M3 had the best extraction response for the six representative metabolites identified in this class. It is worth noting that vaccenic acid (C18:1, 11-octadecenoic acid, C<sub>18</sub>H<sub>34</sub>O<sub>2</sub>) has already been reported to be present in <em>Brucella abortus</em> and <em>Brucella melitensis</em> cultures [<a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>], whereas (Z)-10-nonadecenoic acid (C19:1, C<sub>19</sub>H<sub>36</sub>O<sub>2</sub>), although not previously reported in <em>Brucella</em>, has been found in other gram-negative bacteria, like <em>Salmonella</em> [<a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a>]. Myristic, palmitic, and stearic acids are other long-chain fatty acids that are present in several organisms, including <em>Brucella</em> and/or other bacteria [<a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>, <a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>, <a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a>]. The glycerides, more specifically monoacylglycerols, were the second class with the highest number of extracted metabolites (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>). According to Knoll et al. [<a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>], triacylglycerols in the bacterial cell wall can be metabolized during the stress-induced transition to the non-replicated phase and during infection. Thus, changes in the levels of monoacylglycerols, such as 1-monomyristin and 2-monopalmitin, may indicate alterations in the cell envelope through the glycerolipid and triacylglycerol pathways. Again, method M3 showed the best extraction results, with 1-monopalmitin being the metabolite that had the highest peak areas among the other extracted glycerides. Sucrose and glycerol were better extracted by M4a and M3 (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>). Glycerol is an important substrate for phospholipid biosynthesis in microorganisms, including some gram-negative bacteria [<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a>]. Sucrose, although until recently related only to phototrophic organism, has already been reported as part of the metabolism of several types of bacteria, including some gram-negative Proteobacteria, the phylum to which <em>Brucella</em> also belongs [<a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a>]. For benzoic acid, a representative metabolite from the class of aromatic compounds (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>), M3 and M4 showed better extraction. Benzoic acid has been reported as a metabolite found in ruminal bacteria, as part of the benzoate degradation pathway [<a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a>], as well as a byproduct of phenylalanine metabolism in the sediment-derived bacterium <em>Streptomyces maritimus</em> [<a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">39</a>].</p>
<p id="Par29">Therefore, considering these four classes of metabolites, M3 provided the most efficient condition for extraction, resulting in higher peak areas and good repeatability for the biological triplicate. Information on the pKa and logP of the representative identified metabolites is presented in Table <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S1</a> in the Supplementary material.</p></section></section></section><section id="Sec23"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p id="Par30">The identification of intracellular metabolites that may help in early detection of <em>Brucella abortus</em> infection in animals and humans is directly related to the quality of all steps involved in the metabolomics workflow, including the extraction method, which is essential in discovery studies for determining promising biomarker candidates. Due to the current lack of stablished extraction protocols for <em>Brucella</em> cells metabolomics, the present study evaluated five extraction procedures, which have already been reported for other types of cells/parasites, in order to determine the most recommended approach for studies involving <em>Brucella</em>. M1 was adapted from the Fiehn protocol, used for cellular cultures at the concentration of 10<sup>7</sup> cells/mL [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. An acetonitrile:isopropanol:water mixture (3:3:2, v/v/v) was used as extraction solvent, with cell membrane disruption by sonication followed by vortexing. M2 was based on the Standard Operating Procedures (SOP-6) reported by Goodacre et al. [<a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>] for suspended cells at 2 × 10<sup>6</sup> cells/mL. An 80% (v/v) methanolic solution at -20 °C was the extraction solvent and the procedure included two sequences of three freeze–thaw cycles in a liquid nitrogen bath. M3 was based on the work by t’Kind et al. [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>] for the extraction of 4 × 10<sup>7</sup> parasites. The extraction solvent was methanol:water:chloroform (3:1:1, v/v/v) and the cell membrane rupture was accomplished by four freeze–thaw cycles, interspaced with vortex agitation. M4 was adapted from the procedure described by Matyash et al. [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>] for metabolite extraction from 5.4 × 10<sup>10</sup> cells, which in fact generates two extracts: one composed of an aqueous phase (M4a) and another one composed of an organic layer (M4o). Different compositions of extraction solution were tested by the authors, including mixtures of MTBE, methanol, and water. Lastly, M5 was very similar to M3, except that chloroform was substituted by MTBE.</p>
<p id="Par31">Based on our results, the most effective cell membrane rupture and intracellular metabolite extraction were obtained when using M3, which provided the greatest number of extracted molecular features with the lowest overall RSD of peak areas for the biological triplicate. The solvent mixture composed of metanol:water:chloroform was able to extract polar (carbohydrates) and non-polar (fatty acids and glycerides) molecules from the cells. The higher extraction efficiency obtained for the solvent containing chloroform is probably due to its higher polarity and partition coefficient, when compared to MTBE.</p>
<p id="Par32"><em>Brucella</em> is a gram-negative bacterium, which present an outer membrane with a phospholipid bilayer containing both polar (phosphates and glycerol) and non-polar (fatty acids and lipids) parts, lipoproteins and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) that determine the bacterial antigenicity and toxigenicity [<a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">40</a>, <a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">41</a>]. The LPS is composed of three parts: (a) a lipidic region present in the upper portion composed by lipid A; (b) a polysaccharide core containing different sugar types; and (c) a polysaccharide O elongation. Lipid A is fixed on the wall of the cell membrane, being released in case of cellular death, what characterizes it as an endotoxin of gram-negative bacteria. This compound present in the LPS is responsible for the inflammatory response during the infection period, being able to lead to septic shock and severe infections [<a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">41</a>, <a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">42</a>]. As already reported by Vu et al. [<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>], alterations in the levels of compounds originated from the cell membrane may indicate infection with <em>Brucella abortus</em>.</p>
<p id="Par33">To our knowledge, this was the first study of <em>Brucella abortus</em> that searched for a suitable extraction procedure of intracellular metabolites for untargeted metabolomics applications by GC-HRMS. Some representative metabolites, including six fatty acids, four glycerides, two carbohydrates and one aromatic compound, were identified using the software Unknowns Analysis and may be relevant metabolites to be evaluated in future metabolomics studies involving <em>Brucella</em>. Forthcoming studies may, for instance, contribute to increase the knowledge about <em>Brucella abortus</em> metabolism and infection cycle, allowing a better understanding about the mechanisms of cellular infection and survival within the host cells. In turn, this may contribute to important clinical advances, not only when fighting the disease, but also in new strategies for prevention and eradication of brucellosis [<a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">40</a>].</p>
<p id="Par34">To conclude, we highlight that to carry out a good metabolomic project, it is important to have a well-defined experimental design, which includes a sample preparation step that is suitable for the sample of interest. In this context, the present pilot study provides a compilation of results that will allow researchers in metabolomics, microbiology, and other related areas to make more informed decisions about the sample preparation options when performing metabolomics studies to advance the knowledge about <em>Brucella</em> and brucellosis.
