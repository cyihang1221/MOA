<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=11740895_ac4c05018_0001.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/e1df/11740895/36218095114d/ac4c05018_0001.jpg" loading="lazy" id="ab-tgr1" height="156" width="791" alt="graphic file with name ac4c05018_0001.jpg"></a></p>
<p>Many analytical methods
have been developed for performing targeted
metabolomics. By combining multiple analytical techniques, comprehensive
coverage of the metabolome can be achieved. We combined multiple analytical
techniques to comprehensively and quantitatively characterize the
widely studied NIST human plasma reference material, SRM 1950. Our
goal was to provide a large, well-validated list of confident metabolite
concentration values (i.e., benchmarks) to assist the metabolomics
community in its calibration and comparison efforts. We used four
analytical platforms: high-resolution NMR spectroscopy, direct injection
tandem MS (DI-MS/MS), liquid chromatography tandem MS (LC-MS/MS),
and inductively coupled plasma MS (ICP-MS). Eight validated analytical
assays were run, yielding accurate quantitative measurements for 728
unique metabolites or metabolite species. Through computer-aided literature
mining, we identified another 330 unique metabolites previously quantified
in SRM 1950. We compared NIST-certified values along with literature-derived
concentrations/ranges to the metabolite concentrations measured by
our four platforms and eight assays. From these assays/platforms,
we generated a list of high-confidence concentration values of 1058
metabolites or metabolite species in SRM 1950 including data for 60
amino acids/related compounds, 48 bile acids, 72 amines/sugars/alcohols,
21 metals, 8 catecholamines, 11 vitamins, 92 organic acids, 40 fatty
acids/steroids/nucleobases/indole derivatives, 5 polyfluorinated compounds,
7 carotenoids, 39 acylcarnitines, 76 oxylipins, 13 sterols, and 566
lipids/lipid species. This data set represents the most complete quantitative
characterization of SRM 1950. An online database (SRM1950-DB) containing
1058 plasma metabolites/metabolite species in SRM 1950, their structures,
HMDB IDs, mass, chemical class, concentrations, references, and reliability
is freely available at <a href="https://srm1950-data.wishartlab.com" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://srm1950-data.wishartlab.com</a>.</p></section><hr class="headless">
<p id="sec1">Metabolomics is a field of ‘omics science that involves
the comprehensive characterization of the metabolome–the collection
of metabolites and other small molecules found in cells, tissues or
biofluids.<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a></sup> These chemicals may include
endogenous metabolites (encoded by the host genome) as well as exogenous
or xenobiotic compounds that are ingested, inhaled or absorbed by
the host. Because the metabolome sits at the interface between the
genome and the environment, metabolomics has found many widespread
applications in life sciences. These include mechanistic studies in
health and disease,<sup><a href="#ref2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref2">2</a>−<a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup> biomarker discovery,<sup><a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a>,<a href="#ref6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref6">6</a></sup> drug target
identification,<sup><a href="#ref7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref7">7</a>,<a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> agricultural science,<sup><a href="#ref9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref9">9</a>,<a href="#ref10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref10">10</a></sup> food analysis,<sup><a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a>,<a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a></sup> environmental monitoring,<sup><a href="#ref13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref13">13</a>,<a href="#ref14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref14">14</a></sup> natural product discovery<sup><a href="#ref15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref15">15</a>,<a href="#ref16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref16">16</a></sup> and toxicology.<sup><a href="#ref17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref17">17</a>−<a href="#ref19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref19">19</a></sup></p>
<p>While using a single analytic platform is often the easiest
route
to perform metabolomics, using multiple analytical platforms, especially
for quantitative/targeted metabolomics, significantly broadens metabolite
coverage. For instance, we have shown that using quantitative nuclear
magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy combined with quantitative direct
infusion mass spectrometry (DI-MS), liquid chromatography (LC)-MS,
gas chromatography (GC)-MS and inductively coupled plasma ionization
(ICP)-MS can greatly expand metabolite coverage for a range of biofluids
including human cerebrospinal fluid,<sup><a href="#ref20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref20">20</a>,<a href="#ref21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref21">21</a></sup> serum,<sup><a href="#ref22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref22">22</a></sup> urine,<sup><a href="#ref23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref23">23</a></sup> and saliva<sup><a href="#ref24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref24">24</a></sup> over what can be obtained for any single platform.
Other kinds of quantitative multiplatform metabolomic studies performed
on ruminal fluid,<sup><a href="#ref25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref25">25</a></sup> bovine milk<sup><a href="#ref26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref26">26</a></sup> and various food products<sup><a href="#ref27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref27">27</a></sup> have yielded similarly impressive results.</p>
<p>Broad
metabolite coverage is especially important for metabolomics
standardization. As metabolomic platforms and assays continue to evolve,
it is becoming increasingly important to establish benchmarks, to
create well-characterized, standardized reference materials (RMs),
and to carefully evaluate cross-platform performance with these benchmarks
or RMs. This standardization and “ring-trial” evaluations
are essential so that when laboratories move to different platforms,
transition to newer platforms or create new metabolomic assays, they
are not impaired in their ability to combine or compare previous analyses.
In this regard, the metabolomics community (especially those working
on mammalian metabolomics) has increasingly turned to the use of a
standard reference material (SRM) to help compare, calibrate and combine
metabolomic data. Currently, the SRM most commonly used for human
(and many mammalian) metabolomics studies is the US National Institutes
of Standards and Technology (NIST) SRM 1950 human pooled plasma.<sup><a href="#ref28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref28">28</a></sup> This was the first RM created specifically for
metabolomics research. SRM 1950 was prepared by pooling plasma from
100 different individuals after overnight fasting, with an equal number
of men and women aged 40–50 years old and a racial distribution
representing the US population. Using carefully validated chemical
assays, NIST quantitatively measured 85 compounds and 10 nonmetabolite
markers in SRM 1950.<sup><a href="#ref29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref29">29</a></sup></p>
<p>Many metabolomics
laboratories use SRM 1950 as a standard. A detailed
Google scholar search for “SRM 1950” and “SRM1950”
yielded a total of 1344 mentions or citations, suggesting that there
is widespread use of this standard across the metabolomics community.
Given its popularity and potential importance as a reference standard,
several metabolomic studies have characterized the SRM 1950 metabolome.
These include two studies which quantitatively characterized the lipidome
of SRM 1950 in 2010 and in 2017,<sup><a href="#ref30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref30">30</a>,<a href="#ref31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref31">31</a></sup> another study
in 2013<sup><a href="#ref32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref32">32</a></sup> that identified (but not quantified)
353 metabolites or metabolite species in SRM 1950 using a combination
of NMR, GC-MS and LC-MS and a very recent study in 2020 that used
LC-MS to quantify 204 metabolites in SRM 1950.<sup><a href="#ref33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref33">33</a></sup> In another recent international lipidomics ring trial study,
consensus values of 685 lipid species in SRM 1950 plasma was reported
using the SCIEX Lipidyzer © platform.<sup><a href="#ref34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref34">34</a></sup> Collectively, these studies along with the NIST COA (certification
of analysis) have identified &gt;2000 unique metabolites or metabolite
species in SRM 1950 and have quantified 942 metabolites or metabolite
species - most of which are lipids. However, human plasma and serum
contain many more metabolites.<sup><a href="#ref22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref22">22</a></sup> This suggests
that hundreds to thousands of other metabolites certainly exist in
SRM 1950, and if more of these could be identified, quantified or
validated, it would greatly expand the utility of this metabolomics
standard.</p>
<p>To expand the coverage of quantitatively measured
metabolites for
SRM 1950, we conducted a multiplatform, fully targeted analysis of
SRM1950 at The Metabolomics Innovation Centre (TMIC), Canada’s
national metabolomics laboratory. Using four analytical platforms
and eight different quantitative assays, we quantified all the measurable
metabolites in SRM 1950 and assessed the consistency and accuracy
of these TMIC measurements against those reported by NIST and those
published by other laboratories. The TMIC platforms and assays provided
concentration values for 728 metabolites or metabolite species in
SRM 1950, of which 116 had not previously been reported. Computer-aided
literature mining allowed us to identify and quantify another 330
unique metabolites and metabolite species. This resulted in a high-confidence
reference list of 1058 plasma metabolite or metabolite species concentrations
in SRM 1950. We created a freely available online database (SRM1950-DB; <a href="https://srm1950-data.wishartlab.com" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://srm1950-data.wishartlab.com</a>) containing this complete, downloadable set of 1058 plasma metabolites/metabolite
species. This database should allow other research laboratories to
easily compare and calibrate their assays or their metabolite measurements.
This data set also provide a large reference set for plasma metabolite
values in healthy individuals.</p>
<section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Experimental Section</h2>
<section id="sec2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Reagents</h3>
<p>Two units
of the frozen SRM 1950 (10 vials,
each containing 1.0 mL) were purchased from NIST.</p></section><section id="sec2.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">NMR Spectroscopy</h3>
<p>The SRM 1950 sample was deproteinated
by passage through two water-prewashed 3 kDa MWCO (molecular weight
cutoff) filters, mixed with 5X NMR sample buffer (pH 7.0), and transferred
to 3 mm SampleJet NMR tubes. All <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectra were collected
on a 700 MHz Avance III (Bruker) spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm
HCN Z-gradient pulsed-field gradient (PFG) cryoprobe. All <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectra were processed and analyzed using in-house software
called MagMet.<sup><a href="#ref35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref35">35</a></sup> See the <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.4c05018/suppl_file/ac4c05018_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supporting Information (SI)</a> for details.</p></section><section id="sec2.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">DI+LC-MS/MS
Analysis Using the TMIC PRIME Assay</h3>
<p>This
in-house assay uses a combination of DI and reverse phase (RP) LC
(Agilent 1290 series UHPLC system, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto,
CA) for analyte separation coupled to a Sciex 4000 QTRAP (Applied
Biosystems/MDS Analytical Technologies, Foster City, CA) mass spectrometer.
The assay can identify and quantify up to 142 different endogenous
metabolites or metabolite species including amino acids, acylcarnitines
(ACs), biogenic amines and derivatives, organic acids, glycerophospholipids,
sphingolipids (SM) and sugars.<sup><a href="#ref36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref36">36</a>,<a href="#ref37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref37">37</a></sup> The method uses chemical
derivatization, analyte extraction and separation, combined with selective
mass-spectrometric detection using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM)
pairs to identify and quantify metabolites. Isotope-labeled internal
standards (ISTDs) along with other ISTDs are used for accurate metabolite
quantification. See SI for details.</p></section><section id="sec2.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">DI+LC-MS/MS Method Using
the TMIC MEGA assay</h3>
<p>This custom-made
assay uses a combination of DI-MS and LC-MS/MS (Agilent 1290 series
UHPLC system coupled to a Sciex 4000 QTRAP)<sup><a href="#ref38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref38">38</a></sup> and can identify and quantify up to 726 different endogenous metabolites
or metabolite species. The method uses chemical derivatizations (for
organic acids, amino acids, amino acid derivatives and biogenic amines),
analyte extraction and LC separation (or DI analysis for lipids and
ACs), combined with selective mass-spectrometric detection using MRM
pairs to identify and quantify metabolites. See SI for details.</p></section><section id="sec2.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">DI+LC-MS/MS Analysis Using the Biocrates MxP500 Kit Assay</h3>
<p>This commercially available kit (BIOCRATES Life Sciences AG, Innsbruk,
Austria), used with an Agilent 1290 series UHPLC system and a Sciex
5500 QTRAP mass spectrometer, can identify and quantify up to 630
different endogenous metabolites or metabolite species. The method
combines derivatization and extraction of analytes, and the selective
mass-spectrometric detection using MRM pairs. The MxP 500 kit contains
a 96 deep-well plate with a filter plate attached with sealing tape,
reagents and solvents. Isotope-labeled ISTDs and other ISTDs are integrated
in the kit plate filter for metabolite quantification. See SI for
details.</p></section><section id="sec2.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Analysis of Bile Acids Using LC-MS/MS</h3>
<p>This assay was
used to identify and quantify 48 bile acids.<sup><a href="#ref39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref39">39</a></sup> Plasma proteins were precipitated using acetonitrile-0.2% formic
acid. Bile acids were concentrated by phospholipid-depletion solid-phase
extraction (PD-SPE).<sup><a href="#ref39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref39">39</a></sup> All UPLC-MRM/MS
runs were performed using an Ultimate 3000 RSLC system (Dionex Inc.,
Amsterdam, The Netherlands) coupled to a 4000 QTRAP mass spectrometer
(Sciex, Concord, Canada). Concentrations of the 48 detected bile acids
were calculated with internal calibration.<sup><a href="#ref39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref39">39</a></sup> See SI for details.</p></section><section id="sec2.7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Analysis of Catecholamines Using LC-MS/MS</h3>
<p>This in-house
assay was used to identify and quantify up to eight catecholamines.<sup><a href="#ref40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref40">40</a></sup> The method combines derivatization with HPLC
separation (Agilent 1100 series) and QTRAP 4000 (Sciex Canada, Concord,
Canada) mass spectrometer analysis. See SI for details.</p></section><section id="sec2.8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Analysis of
Vitamins Using LC–MS/MS</h3>
<p>Both water-soluble
and fat-soluble plasma vitamins (13 vitamins in total) were analyzed
via a previously published method.<sup><a href="#ref26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref26">26</a></sup> Plasma
proteins were precipitated from SRM 1950 spiked with isotopically
labeled ISTD mixture using aqueous trichloroacetic acid solution (for
water-soluble vitamins) or methanol/ZnSO<sub>4</sub> (for fat-soluble
vitamins). The supernatant for water-soluble vitamin analysis was
injected for LC–MS/MS analysis. The supernatant from methanol/ZnSO<sub>4</sub> precipitation was treated with hexane to extract the fat-soluble
vitamins, dried and reconstituted with methanol before LC-MS analysis.
Different RP columns and gradients were used to separate the water-
or fat-soluble vitamins using an Agilent 1260 series UHPLC system
(Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA) coupled with a Sciex QTRAP 4000
mass spectrometer. See SI for details.</p></section><section id="sec2.9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metal Ion Analysis Using
ICP–MS</h3>
<p>All trace elemental
analysis was done using previously described methods<sup><a href="#ref26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref26">26</a>,<a href="#ref37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref37">37</a></sup> on a PerkinElmer NexION 350x ICP–MS (PerkinElmer, Woodbridge,
Canada). The SRM 1950 was diluted with nitric acid, hydrogen peroxide
and water containing indium as an ISTD before analysis. An external
calibration curve was used for the quantitation. See SI for details.</p></section><section id="sec2.10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Computer-aided Literature Mining</h3>
<p>A literature search
on PubMed was conducted using different combinations of the terms
“SRM 1950″, “metabolomics”, “metabolome”,
“chemical analysis”, “metabolite profile”,
“NMR”, “GC–MS” and “MS/MS’
to identify papers that characterized the metabolite content of SRM
1950.</p></section><section id="sec2.11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">SRM1950-DB</h3>
<p>This database contains the complete set
of 1058 plasma metabolites/metabolite species in SRM 1950, their structures,
HMDB IDs, mass (monoisotopic and average), chemical class, concentrations
- with standard deviations, references or notes (on workup methods)
and reliability. All data can be downloaded as a CSV file by clicking
on the “Download” tab located at the top of the homepage
and then clicking on “Download” under the CSV column.
Information about limits of detection, limits of quantification, accuracy,
precision and recoveries of metabolites for the assays is available
for download under the same tab.</p></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<p>Our primary
objective was to determine how many metabolites could
be detected and quantified in SRM 1950 using multiple quantitative
analytical assays run on NMR and multiple MS platforms. Our secondary
objective was to determine which assays generated the most accurate
or consistent measurements when compared to reported NIST values or
published reference ranges. The overall goal was to create an extended
reference list of metabolites and their concentrations for SRM 1950.
A total of 728 metabolites or metabolite species were identified and/or
quantified using the eight different assays and four different analytical
platforms (including 700 MHz NMR, LC-MS/MS, DI+LC-MS/MS and ICP-MS).
Metabolite species are a group of isobaric or isomeric metabolites,
typically lipids, where precise identification is not possible. In
addition, computer-aided literature mining also allowed us to identify
and quantify 330 unique metabolites or metabolite species in SRM 1950.
The complete list of metabolites or metabolite species along with
their reported concentrations and their human metabolome database
(HMDB) IDs are listed in <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.4c05018/suppl_file/ac4c05018_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Table S1</a> and on the web through the SRM1950-DB web page (<a href="https://srm1950-data.wishartlab.com" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://srm1950-data.wishartlab.com</a>). For metabolites or metabolite species measured by more than one
platform, averages, along with their standard deviations (SD) as reported
for all platforms, are provided. For metabolites measured by only
one platform, the concentration plus the standard deviations from
the triplicate measurements of that assay or platform are listed.</p>
<p>In the following sections, we provide more detail regarding the
number and classes of metabolites/metabolite species measured by the
different assays, their performance relative to NIST-reported values,
their performance relative to other reported SRM 1950 values<sup><a href="#ref30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref30">30</a>,<a href="#ref31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref31">31</a></sup> and their performance relative to literature or HMDB-reported values.<sup><a href="#ref41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref41">41</a></sup></p>
<section id="sec3.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolites Measured by NMR</h3>
<p>56 metabolites
in SRM 1950
were identified and quantified by NMR including five alcohols, 18
amino acids, seven amino acid-like compounds, 11 carboxylic acids,
six fatty acids, a ketone, a nucleobase or related compound, a vitamin
cofactor, a sulfone and urea, along with two ACs and two carbohydrates.
Most of the NIST certified (12) and referenced amino acids (4) as
well as the amino acid-like compounds were quantified by NMR (14/15)
except homocysteine (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.4c05018/suppl_file/ac4c05018_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S2</a>). Almost
all NMR-quantified amino acids (12/14) were within 10–15% of
the NIST measurements while one was slightly higher (lysine) than
the reported NIST values. Of the remaining amino acids and derivatives
not certified by NIST, 5/6 (except tryptophan) were quantified by
NMR. Their concentrations fell well within ranges reported in the
literature and in the HMDB. Three other important metabolites measured
by NMR and certified by NIST include creatinine, glucose and urea
(<a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>). Only urea
was underestimated by NMR (discussed later) while creatinine and glucose
were within the range of NIST values. The remaining 33 metabolites
were not certified by NIST and included alcohols, carboxylic acids,
short chain fatty acids, and others. All 33 metabolites concentrations
fell within ranges reported by the HMDB except formate which was 10%
less than the lowest reported value. Thus, 53/56 NMR measured metabolites
were within NIST and literature (i.e., HMDB) reported concentrations
(<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.4c05018/suppl_file/ac4c05018_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S1</a>). Of the metabolites listed in <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.4c05018/suppl_file/ac4c05018_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S1</a>, 10 compounds were uniquely identified
and quantified by NMR: acetoacetate, dimethylglycine, D-mannose, glycerol,
ethanol, isopropanol, acetone, methanol, propylene glycol and dimethyl
sulfone.</p>

<p>The human plasma SRM 1950 was the first metabolomic standard created
by NIST in 2006.<sup><a href="#ref32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref32">32</a></sup> A total of 22,000 aliquots
of SRM 1950 were prepared and stored at −80 °C. These
aliquots have been sold/distributed by NIST since then and will continue
to be distributed at least until 2026. SRM 1950 was prepared by pooling
the plasma of 100 different individuals after overnight fasting, using
an equal number of men and women aged 40–50 years old with
a racial distribution that represents the current US population. More
specifically, the blood from these subjects was collected into cold
lithium heparin, which was used as the anticoagulant. The samples
were immediately centrifuged at 4 °C at 8000<em>g</em> to generate plasma. To minimize losses of metabolites, the plasma
samples were frozen to −80 °C within 30 min of collection
and stored under nitrogen. A rapid glucose test was administered to
exclude individuals who did not adhere to the fasting requirement.<sup><a href="#ref32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref32">32</a></sup> Because of the high standards with which it
was prepared, SRM 1950 has become a very popular RM for metabolomics
laboratories.</p>
<section id="sec4.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Previous Studies of SRM 1950 Composition</h3>
<p>NIST is well-known
for its high standards, its extensive quality assurance and quality
management as well as for its care in both preparing and measuring
RMs. Therefore, the concentration values reported by NIST for their
SRMs should be considered as the “gold standard” or
“ground truth”. When SRM 1950 was first released in
2006, NIST quantified and certified 95 components (85 compounds and
10 nonmetabolite markers) which are indicated on its COA. These include
16 amino acids and related compounds, 19 vitamins, carotenoids and
vitamers, 2 cholesterol values, 27 fatty acids, 20 clinical markers
(including 6 metals and 6 proteins) and 6 perfluorinated compounds.
Most of the cholesterol, fatty acid, protein and perfluorinated compounds
are not typically measured by metabolomics researchers. Therefore,
∼85 NIST-reported concentration values in SRM 1950 by their
COA can be routinely used by metabolomics laboratories to calibrate
their instruments or compare their results with other plasma or serum
sample measurements.</p>
<p>As only 85 quantified compounds is actually
quite a modest number for a metabolomics reference standard, several
groups have studied SRM 1950 metabolomic to further expand the metabolome
coverage of this important RM. The first involved the characterization
of the SRM 1950 lipidome by the LIPIDMAPS consortium in 2010.<sup><a href="#ref30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref30">30</a></sup> This LIPIDMAPS study quantified 588 lipids or
lipid species in SRM 1950 using a combination of GC-MS, LC-MS (using
a QTRAP) and LC-MS/MS (using a triple quadrupole) techniques. In 2010,
a multiplatform metabolomics study was conducted on SRM 1950 using
GC-MS, LC-MS, and NMR.<sup><a href="#ref32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref32">32</a></sup> This study identified
(but not quantified) 353 different metabolites or metabolite species
along with ∼15 clinically relevant measures. In 2017, an updated
characterization of the SRM 1950 lipidome was conducted.<sup><a href="#ref31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref31">31</a></sup> This interlaboratory study involved 31 different
laboratories, which collectively measured a total of 1,527 unique
lipids or lipid species. Consensus concentration estimates and associated
uncertainties were determined for 339 of these lipids. Finally, in
2020, a quantitative study was conducted on SRM 1950 using LC-HRMS
methods that quantified 204 metabolites or metabolite species.<sup><a href="#ref33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref33">33</a></sup></p>
<p>In this study, we identified and quantified,
with high confidence,
728 metabolites or metabolite species in SRM 1950. Computer-aided
literature mining collected quantitative information on another 330
unique metabolites or metabolite species. Many of these were obtained
from the studies<sup><a href="#ref31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref31">31</a>−<a href="#ref33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref33">33</a></sup> just mentioned. In total, we generated a list of
high-confidence concentration values for 1058 metabolites or metabolite
species in SRM 1950. Out of these 1058 metabolites, NIST has officially
quantified 85 metabolites in SRM 1950 and these are considered “gold
standard” values. For the 34 compounds we measured that could
be “fairly” compared to the NIST values, we found excellent
concordance. Furthermore, as many of the compounds that we have added
to the SRM 1950 metabolite list are commonly measured metabolites,
these new concentration values and these newly identified/quantified
metabolites or metabolite species should be quite valuable for the
broader metabolomics community.</p></section><section id="sec4.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Potential Applications</h3>
<p>A significantly expanded set
of reference metabolites and metabolite concentrations in SRM 1950
has a number of potential applications. These include their use in
1) untargeted HRMS metabolite identification; 2) untargeted “reference-based”
HRMS metabolite quantification; 3) general instrument calibration;
and 4) as “healthy reference” or “healthy control”
plasma concentrations for biomedical studies. With regard to their
potential application in untargeted HRMS metabolite identification,
metabolomics laboratories may now use SRM 1950 as a reference standard
to establish lab-specific retention time + HRMS <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> pairs or lab-specific retention time + <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> + MS/MS spectral triplets corresponding
to the 1058 compounds in SRM 1950. Once these RT + <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> data are established for one reference biofluid
in a given lab or a given instrument, then it should be relatively
routine to apply this information to almost any other biologically
similar biofluid (mouse, rat, other mammalian serum or plasma samples)
analyzed in the same lab, with the same instrumentation. The expanded
collection of known metabolites in SRM 1950 could be used for untargeted
HRMS metabolomic studies to confidently identify 700–1000 metabolites
in mammalian serum or plasma. However, for HRMS analysis, it is also
important to remember that isomers can be present for many of these
metabolites.</p>
<p>For untargeted metabolite quantification, the expanded
set of quantified metabolites would make SRM 1950 an ideal standard
for reference-based metabolite quantification.<sup><a href="#ref33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref33">33</a></sup> Reference-based metabolite quantification, which is also
called reference standardization, offers a route to perform accurate
quantification in untargeted metabolomics studies.<sup><a href="#ref33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref33">33</a></sup> It also represents a practical solution for harmonizing
cross-platform and cross-laboratory data collected from untargeted
metabolomics studies performed on HRMS instruments. Reference standardization
corrects for systematic technical errors by normalizing metabolite
spectral peak intensities to metabolite concentrations relative to
a calibrated reference sample (such as SRM 1950) analyzed with the
study samples. Because ion counts detected by HRMS instruments are
generally proportional to metabolite concentrations, MS instrument
responses obtained for an identified metabolite with a stable, known
concentration in the SRM 1950 reference could be used as a calibrant
to estimate the concentrations of the same metabolite detected in
study samples. As shown by Liu et al., 2020,<sup><a href="#ref33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref33">33</a></sup> a well calibrated reference standard with a large number of known
concentration values could be used to accurately quantify hundreds
of known (and even unknown metabolites) without isotopic standards.
Our newly established reference values for SRM 1950 could extend the
coverage of reference standardization by a factor of 4.</p>
<p>With
many reliable metabolite concentrations now available for
SRM 1950, the information in <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.4c05018/suppl_file/ac4c05018_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S1</a> (and
the online database SRM 1950-DB, <a href="https://srm1950-data.wishartlab.com" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://srm1950-data.wishartlab.com</a>) can now serve as a very extensive list of “healthy reference”
or “control” plasma concentrations for biomedical studies.
As obtaining healthy control samples for metabolomic studies or comparing
against healthy control values with commonly collected metabolomic
data can be difficult, having &gt;1000 healthy human metabolite concentrations
for plasma certainly should make these comparisons much easier. Furthermore,
since the plasma metabolome and the serum metabolome are similar,
the SRM 1950 plasma metabolite concentration values could also be
used as healthy reference values for human serum.</p></section><section id="sec4.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Platform Comparisons</h3>
<p>While the primary objective of
this work was to increase the breadth of quantitative metabolite coverage
in SRM 1950, we also sought to explore which metabolomics platforms
were most accurate in terms of their concentration measurements or
most comprehensive in terms of their coverage. While ICP-MS proved
to be the most accurate platform, it clearly had the lowest breadth
of coverage (only 21 metal ions quantified in plasma). Likewise, NMR
also proved to be among the most accurate metabolomic platforms for
quantification, but like ICP-MS, it also has rather modest coverage
(56 compounds) compared to the LC-MS platforms. Overall, when combined,
the generalized LC-MS platforms measured the largest number of metabolites
or metabolite species (∼700 quantified metabolites) in SRM
1950. However, the accuracy of the metabolite measurements was not
generally as good for LC-MS as it was with NMR or ICP-MS. In this
regard, we can conclude that LC-MS is best for maximizing metabolite
coverage while NMR and/or ICP-MS is best for maximizing quantitative
accuracy.</p>
<p>Our analysis of SRM 1950 also revealed that there
were some clear differences in the metabolite concentrations reported
for different platforms. Some of these differences were very likely
due to differences in sample preparation methods. For instance, for
the NMR assay, the sample must be filtered through a 3 kDa MWCO ultrafilter
prior to NMR spectral collection. This was done to remove proteins
(particularly serum albumin) whose broad and abundant proton signals
tend to mask the protons of metabolites of interest. However, ultrafiltration
can lead to the loss or reduction of certain metabolites, especially
those known to bind serum albumin, such as urea and tryptophan. Nagana
Gowda and Raftery<sup><a href="#ref45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref45">45</a></sup> demonstrated that ultrafiltered
serum had 10–74% lower concentrations of tryptophan compared
to precipitated serum. We observed this for tryptophan with SRM 1950,
as it was not detectable by NMR (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.4c05018/suppl_file/ac4c05018_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S2</a>) but was detected and correctly quantified by LC-MS/MS. A similar
rationale can be applied to the very low urea concentration measured
by NMR, which was correctly quantified by the MEGA LC-MS/MS assay.
This difference likely reflects the free value (measured by NMR) rather
than the total (free + protein-bound) urea value (measured by LC-MS).</p>
<p>While ultrafiltration is commonly used to remove proteins from
plasma for NMR analysis, for MS-analyses, protein is more commonly
removed from plasma via precipitation using alcohols (methanol or
ethanol) or acids. This protein precipitation step prevents proteins
from clogging the HPLC or UPLC columns used in many LC-MS assays.
Precipitation also releases those metabolites that are tightly bound
to plasma proteins (such as tryptophan, urea and other uremic toxins).
Likewise, different lipid extraction techniques (MeOH-based protein
precipitation,<sup><a href="#ref46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref46">46</a></sup> Folch,<sup><a href="#ref47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref47">47</a></sup> methyl <em>tert</em>-butyl ether or MBTE + chloroform<sup><a href="#ref48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref48">48</a></sup>) also can lead to different reported abundances
of lipids. Therefore, when these compound concentrations are reported,
the method being used for sample preparation must be clearly indicated.
Our overall finding, which is in line with those reported by many
others,<sup><a href="#ref20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref20">20</a>−<a href="#ref26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref26">26</a></sup> is that when the same sample preparation and workup methods are
used, the same concentrations are usually reported.</p>
<p>In addition
to these sample workup differences, different platforms
use different approaches to quantification. NMR uses peak integration
relative to a spiked ISTD (of known concentration) while LC-MS uses
MRM methods that employ internal isotopic ISTDs and various calibration
curves. Each has their strengths and weaknesses. The most reliable
metabolite quantification is achieved by NMR with CVs less than 5%.
The next most reliable metabolite quantification is achieved by LC-MS
methods employing MRM analysis of authentic isotopic standards with
CVs of 10–20%. Semiquantification via a representative single
isotopic standard for a class of compounds (as is commonly done for
lipids and ACs with DI-MS/MS methods) is generally less reliable with
CVs of 20–40%.</p>
<p>We also noted that there were some minor
differences between the
metabolite levels reported by our platforms and those reported by
NIST (or the literature). Some of these differences could be due to
storage conditions. It is now known that certain plasma metabolites
may be quite unstable when stored at −20 °C or even −80
°C over very long periods of time. Given that the SRM 1950 aliquots<sup><a href="#ref49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref49">49</a></sup> have already been stored for more than 18 years
(from 2006 to 2024), it is possible that the values reported by NIST
may differ from those reported here because of differences in sample
composition due to storage time or uncontrolled shipping conditions.
NIST is also aware that concentrations of metabolites may change over
long periods of time and is monitoring SRM 1950 continuously. Indeed,
in April 2016, reference values for glutamic acid, ornithine, retinyl
palmitate, and retinyl stearate were removed as these values had become
quite different than what was originally measured. NIST will continue
to inform registered users of any changes to this standard that may
occur over the storage period and caution users to acquire the current
data sheet for their specific lot of SRM 1950.</p></section></section><section id="sec5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p>This study systematically explored the breadth of quantitative
metabolite coverage achievable for different quantitative metabolomics
platforms/assays for the NIST plasma standard SRM 1950. This work
led to several novel findings or innovations including: 1) the quantification
of 116 metabolites and metabolite species that had not been previously
quantified in SRM 1950; 2) The use of multiple orthogonal, quantitative
methods to measure SRM 1950; and 3) the consolidation of many independent
quantitative data SRM 1950 data sets into a single, easily accessible
web resource (SRM1950-DB) that contains 1058 “consensus”
metabolite concentrations, each with an attached standard deviation
value. Based on an extensive review of the literature, we believe
this work represents the most complete quantitative characterization
of the world’s most commonly used metabolomic reference standard
(SRM 1950). In conducting this study, we found a number of interesting
results. First, the reported concentrations of metabolites are highly
dependent on the workup protocols, especially for lipids and serum-albumin-bound
metabolites (urea, tryptophan). Second, we found that different assays
and platforms exhibited profoundly different coverage. The assays
exhibiting the best breadth of coverage were the general LC-MS/MS
assays (∼700 metabolites or metabolite species detected and
quantified), while the assays exhibiting the lowest breadth of coverage
were the NMR (∼56 metabolites), specialized LC-MS/MS (8–50
metabolites) and ICP-MS (21 metabolites) assays. Third, by combining
multiple assays, it was possible to get even broader, complementary
coverage of the metabolome. Specifically, by using eight different
assays and four different analytical platforms, 728 unique compounds
could be both identified and quantified. By combining these values
with literature data, we generated a list of 1058 “consensus”
metabolite concentrations. This work should provide the metabolomics
community with high-confidence reference values for SRM 1950 that
should improve metabolomics assay calibration, comparison and standardization.</p></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
