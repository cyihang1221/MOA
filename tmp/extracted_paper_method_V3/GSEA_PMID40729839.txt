>Method   Age-stratified murine model under SPF conditions for comparative aging studies   SPF-grade male C57BL/6J mice were used at 2 months (young) and 20 months (aged)​​ (n = 10 per group). All mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Shanghai Jiao Tong University. Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xinhua Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (XHEC-NSFC-2019-240) and followed the ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (22-26°C, 40-60% humidity). After intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (150 mg/kg)​and euthanasia, periodontal tissues were collected. Four randomly selected gingival tissue samples from each group were used for protein sequencing. Cell culture   Human periodontal ligament fibroblasts (hPDLFs) were cultured in a special medium (CM-H136, Pricella) and placed in a constant temperature cell culture box containing 5 % CO2 at a set temperature of 37°C. H&E staining   The tissue samples were fixed, embedded in paraffin and sectioned using H&E staining kit (C0105S, Beyotime ), followed by xylene (534056, Sigma) dewaxing for 10 minutes, gradient ethanol hydration; the nucleus was stained with hematoxylin for 5 minutes, the cytoplasm was stained with eosin for 1 minute, dehydrated with gradient ethanol, and transparentized with xylene for 2 times, once every 5 minutes. Finally, neutral gum was sealed and observed under a microscope. Immunohistochemical staining   The collected mouse gingival tissue specimens were made into paraffin sections, and immunohistochemical staining was performed by dewaxing and hydration, rinsing, antigen repair (citric acid antigen method), antibody reaction (Anti-TNF alpha antibody, abcam, 5 µg/mL), staining and dehydration. Neutral gum was mounted, observed under a microscope, photographed and scored. Protein extraction   SDT buffer was added to the periodontium sample, and transferred to 2 ml tubes with amount quartz sand. The lysate was homogenized by MP Fastprep-24 Automated Homogenizer (6.0m/s, 30s, twice). The homogenate was ultrasonically treated. The collected hPDLFs were added to the lysate ( P0013B, Beyotime ) for 30 min. The treated cells and tissue samples were centrifuged at 14000 g for 15 min, and the supernatant was filtered with a 0.22 μm filter. The filtrate was quantified with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012, Beyotime). Then the samples were boiled for 10 minutes and stored at -80 °C. SDS-PAGE electrophoresis   20 µg of proteins for each sample were mixed with 6X loading buffer respectively and boiled for 5 min. The proteins were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gel. Protein bands were visualized by Coomassie Blue R-250 staining. Western blot   Protein samples and protein Marker were added for gel electrophoresis for 1-2 h. The membrane was transferred for 1.5 h under ice bath conditions and blocked with 5% Blotting Grade (P0216-300g, Beyotime) for 2 h. EGFR antibody (2232S, Cell Signaling Technology , 1 : 1000) and GAPDH antibody (2118T, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000) were added respectively, and placed overnight at 4 °C. HRP-labeled secondary antibody (1: 10000) was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. After washing the membrane, the development photograph was taken, and the target band was selected by ImageJ software to measure the gray value for statistics. Total SOD activity detection   Using the SOD detection kit (S0101S, Beyotime), an appropriate amount of sod sample preparation solution was added to the cells or tissues, centrifuged at about 12,000 g at 4° C for 3-5 min, and the supernatant was retained. The protein concentration was measured by BCA method and the protein was diluted with diluent. An appropriate amount of WST-8 working solution and reaction starting working solution were prepared according to the volume of 160μl per reaction, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. FASP enzyme   A total of 100 μg protein from each sample was initially reduced in 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 100 °C for 5 minutes. To eliminate detergents, DTT, and other small molecules, the samples underwent repeated ultrafiltration using UA buffer (8 M urea, 150 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5) with 30 kDa centrifugal filter units (Sartorius). Subsequently, alkylation was performed by adding 100 μL of 100 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) prepared in UA buffer, followed by incubation in the dark for 30 minutes. The filters were then sequentially rinsed 3 times with UA buffer and twice with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (NH 4 HCO 3 ) to remove excess reagents. Enzymatic digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C by adding 4 μg of sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega) in 40 μL of 50 mM NH 4 HCO 3 . The resulting peptides were recovered by centrifugation and desalted using a C18 reverse-phase column. Peptide concentration was estimated by measuring UV absorbance at 280 nm, applying an extinction coefficient of 1.1 for a 0.1% (g/L) solution, calculated based on the average content of tryptophan and tyrosine residues in invertebrate proteins. NanoElute chromatography   Peptide samples were analyzed using a nanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) interfaced with a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker) equipped with a CaptiveSpray ion source. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a 25 cm × 75 μm fused silica column packed with 1.6 μm C18 particles and an integrated emitter (IonOpticks, Australia). The column was maintained at 50 °C using a thermostatically controlled oven (Sonation GmbH, Germany). Prior to sample loading, the column was equilibrated with 4 column volumes of buffer A (0.1% formic acid in Milli-Q water) at a pressure of 800 bar. Samples were loaded in 100% buffer A and eluted at a constant flow rate of 300 nL/min using a linear gradient. Mass spectrometry identification   The timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) was operated in parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode. Data were acquired over a mass-to-charge (m/z) range of 100-1700. Ion mobility separation was performed with a 1/K₀range from 0.75 to 1.40 V·s/cm² and a ramp time of 100 ms. The duty cycle was locked at 100%. Source parameters included a capillary voltage of 1500 V, a dry gas flow of 3 L/min, and a dry temperature of 180 °C. PASEF acquisition comprised 10 MS/MS scans per cycle, with a total cycle time of 1.16 s. Precursors with charge states from 0 to 5 were selected for fragmentation, with dynamic exclusion applied for 0.5 minutes. Target intensity was set at 10,000 with a threshold of 2500, and collision-induced dissociation (CID) energy ranged from 20 to 59 eV. Parameters for qualitative and quantitative protein analysis   The raw MS data were processed with MaxQuant (v1.6.17.0). Spectra were searched against a project-specific protein database. The precursor ion mass tolerance was set at 6 ppm for the initial search. Trypsin/P was specified for enzymatic digestion, allowing up to 2 missed cleavages and a 20 ppm tolerance for fragment ions. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification, while N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation were included as variable modifications. A global false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% was applied at both peptide and protein levels. Label-free quantification (LFQ) was based on normalized spectral intensities. Proteins with a fold change above 2 or below 0.5 and  P < . 05 (Student’s t-test) were identified as differentially expressed. Clustering analysis   Quantitative values for target proteins were normalized prior to clustering. The Python package matplotlib was utilized to conduct hierarchical clustering of both samples and protein expression levels (distance metric: Euclidean; linkage: average). The resulting relationships were visualized through a hierarchical heatmap. GO functional annotation   Gene Ontology (GO) annotation was performed using Blast2GO (v1.4.4) in 4 main steps: sequence alignment (BLAST), GO term mapping, annotation, and refinement via ANNEX.   Initially, protein sequences were aligned to a designated database using NCBI BLAST+ (ncbi-blast-2.3.0+) on a Linux platform, retaining the top 10 hits with E-values ≤ 1e-3. GO terms corresponding to the highest scoring matches were extracted using Blast2GO Command Line (database: go_20190701.obo). During annotation, GO terms were assigned based on sequence homology, term reliability, and GO hierarchy structure. InterProScan (interproscan-5.30-69.0) was applied to detect conserved domains, which were incorporated to enhance functional interpretation. Finally, ANNEX refinement was conducted to improve annotation specificity and link related terms. KEGG pathway annotation   Functional classification of proteins based on KEGG Orthology (KO) was conducted using KOALA (v3.0). Target protein sequences were aligned against the KEGG GENES database (version: KO_INFO_END.txt, 2023.10.17), and KO identifiers were assigned accordingly. Associated pathway data were subsequently retrieved for functional mapping. Enrichment analysis for GO and KEGG annotations   GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were carried out using a unified method. Fisher’s Exact Test was applied to evaluate the overrepresentation of specific GO terms or pathways in the target set compared to the background, enabling statistical assessment of enrichment significance. Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction   Total RNA was extracted from gingival tissues of young and aged mice by TRIzol method (15596018CN, Invitrogen). Reverse transcription kit ( R323-01, Vazyme company ) was used to convert it into c DNA, and a reverse transcription reaction system ( 20 μL system ) was configured. Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR amplification was performed, and gene expression was calculated using 2 ^ ( -△△Ct ). The primer sequence is as follows: Egfr: F- GCCATCTGGGCCAAAGATACC, R-GTCTTCGCATGAATAGGCCAAT;  F2: F- CCGAAAGGGCAACCTAGAGC, R-GGCCCAGAACACGTCTGTG;  Col1a1: F- GCTCCTCTTAGGGGCCACT, R-ATTGGGGACCCTTAGGCCAT;  Hspa1b: F- GAGATCGACTCTCTGTTCGAGG, R-GCCCGTTGAAGAAGTCCTG;  Vtn: F- AGGCCCTTTTTCATACTAGCCC, R-AAGCTCGTCACACTGACACTT;  Itgb4:F-  ACTCCATGTCTGACGATCTGG  ,  R-GGGACGCTGACTTTGTCCAC. DCFH-DA staining   The hPDLFs were treated with H 2 O 2 ( 150μM)for 16 hours, and the ABT-treated (HY-103389, MCE, 20μM) hPDLFs were added with a final concentration of 10μM DCFH-DA dilution staining solution (S0033S, Beyotime ) and incubated in a 37℃ cell culture box for 20 minutes. The excess probe liquid was washed away with PBS and observed under a fluorescence microscope. Network-based GO term enrichment visualization   The bioinformatic environment ShinyGO 0.76 (available at:  http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go76/ ) was used to create networks summarizing overlapping GO terms. GSEA analysis   Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted for all identified genes using the GSEA software. Mouse dataset were obtained from the msigdbr package in R (V7.5.1). Protein-protein interactions   First, we found the direct or indirect interaction network between the target protein IDs in the String database, and then, we used the Cytoscape software (V8.11.0.1934) to generate interaction Network Diagram. Statistical analysis   Data is expressed as SD ± mean, and analyzed using Graphpad Prism 8 software. Unpaired Student's t-test was used for evaluation. A p-value of ≤ .05 is considered statistically significant, and these values are indicated in the figure with an asterisk (* P ≤ . 05;  ⁎⁎ P ≤ . 01;  ⁎⁎⁎ P ≤ . 001). Result   The level of ROS in the aging gingival tissue increases significantly   H&E staining and TNF-α immunohistochemical staining showed that the gingival tissue of the aged group exhibited epithelial atrophy, a reduction in connective tissue fibrosis, and a state of chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, with upregulated expression of TNF-α ( Figure 1 A). Meanwhile, Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis revealed a marked increase in the distance from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar bone crest (ABC) in aged mice compared to young controls, indicating age-related alveolar bone loss ( Figure 1 B). Furthermore, MitoSOX staining demonstrated a significant elevation in mitochondrial ROS levels in the gingival tissues of aged mice. Nuclear staining with Hoechst was used to confirm the localization and structural integrity of nuclei, which further validated the site of ROS accumulation ( Figure 1 C). An acute senescence model was induced in mouse gingival fibroblasts by hydrogen peroxide (H 2 O 2 ) treatment. Subsequent quantification of superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity showed a significant reduction in the aged group compared to controls ( P < . 001) ( Figure 1 D).   Fig. 1. There are significant differences in the proteomic characteristics of normal mice and aged mice   To investigate the mechanisms underlying gingival tissue aging and identify potential therapeutic targets, we conducted a proteomic analysis on gingival tissues from young and aged mice. Our study identified 2678 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between the Aged and Control groups using the 4D label-free platform and mass spectrometry. 224 proteins were further filtered based on a fold change > 1.2 and a p-value < .05, comprising 97 upregulated and 227 downregulated proteins ( Figure 2 A). A heat map of the 8 samples revealed consistent expression patterns within each group and distinct differences between the groups ( Figure 2 B).   Fig. 2. The most representative biological processes and molecular functions of senescent histones are mainly related to the proliferation ability of gingival cells and oxidative stress status   Bioinformatics enrichment analysis using the Gene Ontology (GO) classification was performed on both upregulated and downregulated proteins in the gingival tissue of old mice. In the Biological Process (BP) category, downregulated proteins were primarily enriched in pathways related to supramolecular fiber organization, cell adhesion, cell migration regulation, cytoskeleton organization, and cell motility ( Figure 3 A). These results suggested that aging gingiva exhibits reduced fiber stability, impaired wound repair, and a weakened immune response, consistent with the known histological changes in aging gingiva. Conversely, the upregulated proteins in the gingival tissue of old mice were enriched in processes such as the negative regulation of blood coagulation, hemostasis, and wound healing, as well as cellular detoxification and responses to toxic substances ( Figure 3 B), indicating compromised coagulation and healing functions, along with the presence of cytotoxic substances in the tissue. In the Molecular Function (MF) category, downregulated proteins were enriched in insulin-like growth factor I binding, growth factor binding, protein kinase binding, and kinase binding ( Figure 3 E). This suggests that key proliferative signaling pathways are downregulated in aging gingiva, negatively affecting cell survival and proliferation. In contrast, the upregulated proteins were mainly associated with activities such as glutathione disulfide oxidoreductase, carbonyl reductase (NADPH), disulfide oxidoreductase, oxidoreductase (acting on CH-OH groups with NAD or NADP as acceptors), and antioxidant activity ( Figure 3 F). These functions are closely linked to ROS clearance and resistance to oxidative stress, indicating a significant oxidative stress state in aging gingiva.   Hierarchical clustering analysis summarized the correlations between biological processes and molecular functions for the differentially expressed proteins. Deeper nodes indicated more pronounced gene set enrichment, while larger nodes reflected lower p-valuesand thicker edges signified a greater overlap in genes. After a thorough literature review and analysis, we identified 4 primary functional interaction networks. In the gingival tissue of old mice, decreased protein expression was mainly associated with the biological processes related to gingival tissue integrity and the molecular functions governing cell growth and proliferation signaling pathways ( Figure3 C, G). Increased protein expression in the gingival tissue of old mice was primarily linked to biological processes involved in counteracting cytotoxic substances and molecular functions related to oxidative stress resistance ( Figure 3 D, H). KEGG and GESA enrichment analysis showed that the relevant proliferation signaling pathways were downregulated in aging gingiva   To investigate alterations in cell signaling pathways within aging gingiva, we conducted KEGG enrichment analysis on all differentially expressed proteins. The analysis revealed that key pathways involved in aging gingiva, particularly the PI3K-AKT pathway, were affected ( Figure 4 A). Mapping these differentially expressed proteins onto the KEGG PI3K-AKT pathway diagram highlighted the central role of EGFR in this signaling cascade ( Figure 4 B).   Fig. 4. It has not yet been confirmed whether this result is related to the oxidative stress state of aging gums. To investigate this, we used DCFH-DA staining to detect the ROS content in the gingival fibroblasts of mice after different treatments. The results confirmed that the ROS content in gingival fibroblasts decreased after H 2 O 2  induction of aging, while this trend was reversed after using ABT to inhibit CYP450 ( Figure 5 B). Superoxide dismutases (SODs) have been shown to play an important role in ROS damage and in regulating ROS signaling. 15  We further measured the total SOD content in gingival fibroblasts from 3 groups of mice. Consistent with the previous results, ABT treatment alleviated the reduction in SOD content caused by aging gingival fibroblasts ( Figure 5 C).   Fig. 5.
