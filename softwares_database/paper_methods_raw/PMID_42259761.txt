ABSTRACT
The objective was to evaluate the potential of machine learning
algorithms utilizing thermal imaging and/or milk-based parameters
for the identification of subclinical mastitis(SCM) in dairy cows.
Holstein dairy cows (n = 194) were enrolled, and milk samples
were collected from each quarter separately (n = 776 quarter-level
samples) and analyzed for milk-based parameters. Four images per
cow, representing all four quarters, were captured using a handheld
infrared camera. Skin temperatures were extracted using Fluke
Connect software, yielding 776 images for analysis. SCM was
defined at the quarter level as SCC > 200,000 cells/mL, identifying
139 infected and 637 healthy quarters. The data were analyzed to
test Random Forest Classifier(RFC), Logistic Regression Classifier,
AdaBoost Classifier(ABC), and Naïve Bayes Classifier algorithms.
The RFC models using milk features and temperature features
achieved accuracy and precision scores of 0.88 and 0.88,
respectively, yielding 0.90 AUC values, with high feature
importance for milk lactose and SNF. Using only thermal imaging
features, the ABC model yielded accuracy and precision of 0.82 and
0.67, respectively, with 0.56 AUC. We conclude that SCM
prediction using machine learning algorithms is most promising
when combined with thermal images and milk-based parameters.
Future studies with larger datasets and refined methods for thermal
image capture and analysis are warranted to validate these findings.
Introduction
Mastitis remains one of the most economically significant diseases
in the dairy industry that impacts farm profitability (Hogeveen et
al. 2011). It is characterized by inflammation of the mammary
gland, primarily caused by bacterial infections, and can manifest as
either clinical or subclinical mastitis (Bradley 2002; Jamali et al.
2018). While clinical mastitis is identifiable by visible signs such as
redness, pain, swollen udders, and abnormal milk, subclinical
mastitis (SCM) is more challenging to detect because of its lack of
overt symptoms (Seegers et al. 2003). SCM is defined by an
elevated somatic cell count (SCC) in milk, leading to reduced milk
quality, decreased production, and increased treatment costs
(Halasa et al. 2007). The on-farm prevalence of SCM varies widely,
with studies reporting rates ranging from 20% to over 50%
depending on region and management factors (Ruegg 2003; Abebe
et al. 2016).
The economic impact of SCM is substantial, with estimates
suggesting that it accounts for a greater portion of the total mastitis-
related losses (Halasa et al. 2007; Krishnamoorthy et al. 2021). In
the United States, the annual cost of mastitis to the dairy industry is
estimated to be in the billions of dollars (Ruegg 2017). Therefore,
early and accurate detection of SCM is critical for effective
management and control. Traditional diagnostic methods, such as
somatic cell counts, electrical conductivity, and the California
Mastitis Test (CMT), have been widely used but have intrinsic
limitations. These methods often require collecting milk samples
from cows, which is time-consuming, and may not provide real-time
results suitable for on-farm decision-making (Schukken et al. 2003;
Ruegg 2017). Additionally, the variables measured in these
techniques can be influenced by non-mastitis factors such as the
stage of lactation, milking frequency, and stress, potentially leading
to misdiagnosis (Schepers et al. 1997; Krishnamoorthy et al. 2021).
In recent years, interest in non-invasive technologies, and cow-level
features for mastitis detection has increased (Pakrashi et al. 2023).
Infrared thermography (IRT), or thermal imaging, has emerged as a
promising tool for identifying inflammation in dairy cattle (Polat et
al. 2010; Schaefer et al. 2004). The principle of IRT is based on
detecting heat patterns; inflammation associated with mastitis
increases local temperature due to enhanced blood flow and
metabolic activity, which can be captured by thermal cameras
(Stewart et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2022). Several studies have
investigated the efficacy of IRT in detecting mastitis. As a high level
of inflammation, which contributes to increased temperature, is
inherent in clinical mastitis cases, the utility of temperature
variables in SCM is of concern, as the inherent clinical signs are not
present. Hovinen et al. (2008) demonstrated that IRT could detect
clinical mastitis with high accuracy, indicating that temperature
increases in affected udder quarters. With the recent rise of
advanced analytics and machine learning tools, their utilization for
mastitis detection is suggested. In addition to other advanced
analytics tools, machine learning algorithms have been identified to
detect mastitis from thermal images (Wang et al. 2022; Chu et al.
2023). These advancements highlight the potential of machine
learning to process complex cow- and quarter-level data, identify
subtle patterns of inflammation, and integrate multiple variables for
more robust SCM detection, paving the way for automated, on-farm
diagnostic systems. However, the use of milk-derived variables, IRT,
and their combination in SCM prediction has not been extensively
explored. The main goal of this project is to identify a strategy to
use advanced analytic tools using IRT, milk-based variables and
their combination for mastitis detection in dairy farms. This study
aims to evaluate the potential of using infra-red imaging alone as
well as in combination with milk-derived features (fat%, protein%,
SNF%, lactose, and MUN) using machine learning algorithms to
identify SCM in dairy cattle. By comparing and combining thermal
imaging with traditional diagnostic methods and developing
standardized algorithms, we seek to provide dairy farmers with an
effective, non-invasive method for the early detection and
management of SCM.
Materials and methods
Study population
This study was conducted at a commercial dairy farm in central
Texas during February 2023, milking a rolling average of 2200
cows. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal
Care and Use Committee at Texas A&M AgriLife Research (IACUC
#2022-026A). A total of 194 Holstein dairy cows were enrolled,
with days in milk (DIM) ranging from 25 to 245 and parity 1–7. The
sample size used was based on available resources, including funds
and logistics, during the study period. Cows were purposively
selected based on their lactation stage [Early lactation (25–70 DIM 
= 63; Mid (70–150) = 72; Late lactation (151–245) = 59], parity [
1st lactation = 52; 2nd lactation = 45; > 2 = 97], and historical
udder level somatic cell count (SCC) records [SCC > 200 thousands]
to ensure a representation of healthy and potentially affected
animals of different cow groups for the robust model development
based on previous monthly herd tests. Cows with dead quarters
were not used in this study. The farm was operated under standard
farm management practices, with cows housed in a free-stall barn
and milked twice daily in a double 24 parallel milking parlor. Cows
were fed a total mixed ratio (TMR) two times a day, formulated to
meet or exceed the nutritional requirements for lactating Holstein
cow producing 30 kg of milk/d with 3.5% fat and 3.1% true
protein.
Mastitis management on the farm
Cows on the farm were milked two times per day. Cow monitoring
involved checking for clinical mastitis signs during the milking
process. Cows identified with clinical mastitis were treated
according to the farm protocol. All cows were monitored monthly
for their milk production, milk components, and somatic cell counts
(SCC) through DHIA testing. Cows with high SCC values in these
tests were closely monitored for their probability of developing
clinical signs of mastitis.
Milk sampling and analysis
Milk samples were collected from each udder quarter (n = 776
quarter-level samples) during the morning milking session. A 10 mL
sample was obtained from each quarter using sterile techniques and
stored at 4 °C until analysis. After cleaning the teat ends thoroughly
using a cotton pad soaked in alcohol (70%), milk samples were
collected from all quarters on the day of the dairy visit. Milk
samples were submitted immediately to the Texas DHIA laboratory
(Stephenville, TX). The samples were evaluated for multiple
parameters, including SCC (reported as n × 1000 cells/mL), fat (%),
protein (%), lactose (%), solids-not-fat (SNF, %), beta-
hydroxybutyrate (BHBA, mM/L), acetone (mM/L), and milk urea
nitrogen (MUN, mg/dL). The SCC was measured using a Fossomatic
analyzer (Foss, Hillerød, Denmark), while other milk components
were analyzed using a Bentley FTS/FCM 400 analyzer (Bentley
Instruments, Chaska, MN, USA). Subclinical mastitis (SCM) was
defined at the quarter level as SCC > 200,000 cells/mL, which is
consistent with established thresholds in dairy research (Schukken
et al. 2003).
