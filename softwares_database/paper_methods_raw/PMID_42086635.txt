
Materials and methods
Research subjects and areas
The study was conducted in 2023 in Shandong Province, China – an
important region for tomato production. It included both open
fields and greenhouses and utilized two popular cultivars:
Dongfeng-1, a high-yielding, weather-resistant hybrid, and Gypsy, a
mid-ripening variety with good commercial potential but
susceptible to fungal infections in high humidity. The experiment
involved 10 greenhouse plots (2,000 m² total) and 10 open-field
plots (5,000 m² total), each divided into equal areas. All plots had
the same sandy loam soil. Greenhouse conditions were stable (24–
28 °C, 60–70% humidity), while open-field plots experienced
natural variability (20–30 °C, 50–65% humidity). Permission to
conduct experiments was obtained from participating farmers.
Data collection and preparation
Images and data from 20 study plots were used to analyze fungal
disease occurrence. The plots were equally assigned across five ML
algorithms, using the same data to ensure consistent comparison.
Data collection tools included:
High-resolution cameras. In open fields, cameras were mounted on
drones flying at an altitude of 3–5 m above the plants; in
greenhouses, they were installed on mobile trolleys moving along
the rows at a distance of 0.5–1 m to capture close-up images.
Multispectral sensors. The sensors were mounted on both drones
(open fields) and trolleys (greenhouses) at a height of 1 m above
the plants to record reflectance data for detailed spectral analysis.
Microclimate sensors: In open fields, soil moisture sensors were
installed at a depth of 10 cm, and air temperature sensors at a
height of 1.5 m. In greenhouses, the setup was denser – one soil
moisture sensor per 5 m² and air sensors positioned at a height of
1.2 m – to more precisely track conditions near the leaves.
Data update frequency
High-resolution cameras and multispectral sensors operated daily
from 8:00 to 10:00 AM to reduce variations in lighting and
temperature. Drones conducted biweekly flights to collect images
and spectral data from all plots. Soil moisture and air temperature
sensors provided real-time data every 15 min. These methods
ensured the acquisition of accurate data for machine learning
algorithms. Agronomists annotated approximately 20,000 images,
labeling affected areas and classifying diseases. To ensure a fair
comparison of the machine learning models and avoid data leakage,
the dataset was divided into three independent subsets at the image
level: a training set (70%), a validation set (10%) and a test set
(20%). All models were trained using the training set, while the
validation set was used exclusively for hyperparameter tuning and
model selection. The test set was not used during model training,
but for the final evaluation of model performance. Additionally, a 5-
fold cross-validation procedure was applied to the training set to
enhance the models’ generalizability and mitigate the risk of
overfitting.
