</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ABS1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="S1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objective</h3>
<p id="P2">To develop a computational approach to link clinical outcomes with environmental exposures and molecular variations measured in DOD serum-repository samples.</p></section><section id="S2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p id="P3">ICD-9 codes which corresponded to cardiopulmonary symptoms for service personnel were selected to test for associations with deployment-related inhalation hazards and metabolomics, micro-RNA, cytokine, plasma markers, and environmental exposure analyses for corresponding samples. xMWAS and Mummichog were used for integrative network and pathway analysis.</p></section><section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p id="P4">Comparison between 41 personnel exhibiting new cardio-pulmonary diagnoses after deployment start-date to 25 personnel exhibiting no symptoms identified biomarkers associated with cardiopulmonary conditions. Integrative network and pathway analysis showed communities of clinical, molecular, and environmental markers associated with fatty acid, lipid, nucleotide, and amino acid metabolism pathways.</p></section><section id="S4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h3>
<p id="P5">The current proof of principle study establishes a computational framework for integrative analysis of deployment-related exposures, molecular responses, and health outcomes.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Health surveillance, systems biology, hazardous exposures, exposome, DoD serum repository</p></section></section><section id="S5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p id="P6">Health effects that occur as a consequence of adverse exposures related to military deployment, such as those due to the Vietnam and Gulf wars (<a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">1</a>, <a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">2</a>), can be impacted by demographic factors and other exposures and occur years after exposure (<a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a>). This complexity requires long-term studies and improved tools to address deployment-associated health concerns (<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a>–<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a>). Within the evolving framework of exposome research and novel technological advances, the scientific field is rapidly improving capabilities to test for interactions of a host of occupational exposures, demographic variables and clinical outcomes.</p>
<p id="P7">This is particularly important because complex diseases, such as coronary artery disease and asthma, often occur with a combination of molecular, environmental, and lifestyle risk factors (<a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a>, <a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>). Application of multi-scale markers and network analyses have potential to improve disease prediction and enhance our understanding of such complex disease processes and may also help extend ability to elucidate biological pathways for therapeutic interventions. Recent review articles have highlighted the importance of using machine learning for aggregating multi-scale data from clinical and omics platforms for improved disease prediction, understanding of disease mechanisms, and precision medicine (<a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a>, <a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a>, <a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a>).</p>
<p id="P8">The Department of Defense Serum Repository (DoDSR) was initially established as a biobank for serum of service members which had been tested for HIV infection (<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a>–<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a>) and has extensive clinical and molecular information available for the service members. Using high-resolution metabolomics (HRM), we previously showed the feasibility of using samples stored in the (DoDSR) for bio-monitoring the presence of environmental chemicals (<a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a>–<a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">18</a>), microRNA’s (<a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">19</a>, <a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a>), cytokines (<a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a>), health behavior (<a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a>), and nutritional status (<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a>), as well as defining the association of these with the metabolome (Go <em>et al</em>.; Smith <em>et al</em>; <em>JOEM</em> Current Issue) for 200 individuals deployed to high risk areas, <em>i.e</em>., Balad, Iraq, and Bagram, Afghanistan, compared to 200 individuals stationed domestically. Thus, the DoDSR provides an opportunity to test the feasibility of integrative network analysis methods to link clinical characteristics and multi-omic responses with health outcomes. Such capability could be very useful because occupational exposures in high-risk areas are challenging to quantify due to lack of capabilities for adequate environmental monitoring. Additionally, lack of validated exposure bio-markers have proven challenging for clinical diagnoses for deployment related environmental exposures. Therefore, many of the mechanisms for the environmental chemicals to induce clinical outcomes are unknown, especially given the variability of duration and concentration of exposures as well as the chemical properties of these agents.</p>
<p id="P9">Importantly, in addition to multi-dimensional molecular data, clinical outcomes information is available from ICD-9 disease classification coding for the 400 service members for whom the extensive environmental chemical, miRNA, cytokine and metabolomics data are available (<a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a>). Although the ICD9 codes are relatively limiting, we felt that there were enough codes to justify a pilot study to test integrative network analysis to link clinical outcomes with environmental exposures and molecular variations in service personnel. Accordingly, the main objectives of the current pilot study were to: 1) determine whether exposure-related changes to the metabolome, microRNAs, cytokines, and plasma protein markers (<a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a>–<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a>);(Go <em>et al</em>.; Smith <em>et al</em>; 2019 <em>JOEM</em> Current Issue) collected from data within DoDSR serum are correlated with cardiopulmonary outcomes as measured by ICD-9 diagnoses in deployment cohort; 2) develop a computational framework to identify multi-scale associations between clinical, molecular markers, environmental exposures, and disease outcomes.</p></section><section id="S6"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">METHODS</h2>
<section id="S7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Ethics statement</h3>
<p id="P10">The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Uniformed Services University, Emory University, and the University of Rochester. Samples and ICD-9 records were assigned code numbers, and the key linking the samples and ICD-9 codes was retained at the Armed Forces Health Surveillance Center and not shared with the research groups conducting this study.</p></section><section id="S8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample Characteristics</h3>
<p id="P11">Pre- and post-deployment serum samples for 200 active-duty service personnel who were deployed to Iraq or Afghanistan (the Case group) were retrieved from the DoD Serum Repository as previously described (<a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">17</a>) Go <em>et al</em>; 2019 <em>JOEM</em> Current Issue). A group of 200, non-deployed Service Members (Controls) without similar deployment exposures but with specimens in the DoDSR were selected as controls for this study. Additional selection criteria for Case and Controls included time in service between collections, and were not matched based on gender or age. We also obtained a database of medical records using ICD-9 codes and linked to the serum samples with code numbers. All data on DoD Service members contained in the Defense Medical Surveillance System (DMSS) are validated against DoD personnel data. Data elements (i.e., gender, age, grade, race/ethnic, and marital status) for a medical event are derived from personnel data considered to be current on the date of a medical event. Overall population statistics (i.e., person year denominators) are calculated based on longitudinal personnel data. Unknown (or missing) data are excluded from the calculations. We included persons where the ICD9 code was recorded at only one visit. Records of healthcare encounters from military treatment facilities and purchased care hospitalizations as well as ambulatory medical care were included in this study. First time encounters with an ICD-9 code of interest, within the surveillance period were captured. An individual censored for one encounter remained in the study to identify other encounters of interest.</p>
<p id="P12">Based on these criteria, we identified a subset of 49 ICD-9 codes representing cardiopulmonary symptoms and diagnoses that we hypothesized could be reasonably related to deployment-related inhalational exposures (See <a href="#T1" class="usa-link">Table I</a>). Prior to analysis, subjects were filtered based on missing data and manual curation of clinical data to check for mismatch between deployment dates and medical appointment dates. We then classified subjects by the timing of medical encounters which referenced specified ICD-9 codes as either 1) Case-Onset, which includes personnel who developed new cardiopulmonary ICD-9 codes after the start of deployment (N=41); 2) Case-Never group that included service personnel who never exhibited cardiopulmonary symptoms before, during or after deployment (N=25) or 3) Case-Pre, which included the individuals that exhibited cardiopulmonary symptoms prior to deployment (N=125). We performed the same analysis for the non-deployed Control group. We identified 18 subjects with onset of at least one cardiopulmonary ICD-9 code during their military service (Control-Onset), 33 subjects who never exhibited any of the listed symptoms or diagnoses (Control-Never), and 133 subjects who exhibited cardiopulmonary symptoms before blood collection period (Control-Pre). Visualization of the different sample populations for both Cases and Controls can be found in the tree diagram illustrating the breakdown of the DoDSR serum samples (see <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig 1</a>).</p>

<p id="P13">The serum samples were processed for several classes of biomarkers (<a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a>), miRNAs (<a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">19</a>, <a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a>), cytokines (<a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a>), cotinine (<a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a>) environmental chemicals like PAHs, dioxins and furans (<a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">16</a>), and the metabolome (Go <em>et al;</em> 2019 <em>JOEM</em> Current Issue). The methods and initial results for each of these measurements are described in the respective articles.</p></section><section id="S10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis between deployment and serum biomarkers</h3>
<p id="P14">To identify possible associations between symptom onset and serum biomarkers, we performed non-parametric Wilcoxon sign rank test to compare biomarkers levels between Case-Onset x Case-Never and Control-Onset x Control-Never, using the absolute values of the biomarkers from the post-deployment serum sample. A false discovery correction was not applied for this exploratory study due to small sample sizes and exploratory nature of the analyses. Control-Onset x Control-Never was used as a control and only markers significant in Case-Onset comparisons were investigated.</p></section><section id="S11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) for ICD-9 data</h3>
<p id="P15">Multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) is a dimensionality reduction technique for categorical data that allows detection and exploration of structural relationships between variables and samples (<a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">24</a>). The MCA() function in R package FactoMineR was used to perform MCA to select the top three dimensions corresponding to 10% or more variation (Total Variance 33.2%). The explain_code() function in R package icd was used for mapping ICD-9 codes with their corresponding description. The contributions of ICD-9 codes to each dimension were visualized using the corrplot() function in R package corrplot.</p></section><section id="S12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Integrative network analysis: identifying associations between clinical data, biomolecules, and environmental chemicals</h3>
<p id="P16">High-resolution metabolomics, microRNA, plasma protein markers and cytokines, benzo(a)pyrene diol epoxide (BPDE) and dioxin/furan measurements, and cotinine levels from a subset of 66 Case-Onset (N=41) and Case-Never (N=25) groups were integrated using xMWAS (<a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">25</a>). The input data was categorized into: 1) the molecular layer, which included 9,092 metabolic features (<em>m/z</em> and time), 32 plasma proteins and immune markers, 144 microRNAs; 2) the environmental exposures layer, which included cotinine, benzo(a)pyrene diol epoxide (BPDE), and 3 Principal Components (PC) for the dioxins as previously described in (Khan et al 2019 JOEM Current Issue) (PC1, PC2, and PC3); and 3) the clinical layer, which included the first three principal dimensions from the MCA analysis corresponding to cardiopulmonary disease sub-category. Integrative network analysis was performed using sparse partial least squares regression analysis, a multivariate approach for data integration, to only include associations with |r|&gt;0.3 and p&lt;0.05. The multilevel community detection method (<a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a>) in xMWAS was used for identifying communities of tightly connected clinical outcomes, biomolecules, and environmental exposures. Pathway analysis was performed using Mummichog pathway analysis tool in MetaboAnalyst v4.0.</p></section></section><section id="S13"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS</h2>

</div></div></section></section><section id="S15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Association between molecular markers and onset of cardiopulmonary symptoms</h3>
<p id="P18">Out of 246 biomarkers assessed, 26 biomarkers had significant associations only in comparisons with deployment for cardiopulmonary conditions (see <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Table 1</a>). Some biomarkers, including IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA), platelet factor 4 (PF4), and glutamate were elevated in both deployment and non-deployment focused comparisons. Others biomarkers found to be different include cholesterol, which was significant between the Control-Onset and Control-Never, and serum amyloid A which was significantly different Case-Onset x Case-Never. Several of the biomarkers that were identified in this analysis were also found to be associated with deployment in another independent analysis (Thatcher et al., 2019 <em>JOEM</em> Current Issue); miR-101, miR-151, miR-30a and miR-27b. We also repeated this analysis using a subset of ICD-9 codes associated with asthma, bronchitis, and other obstructive lung diseases (not shown) and found that miR-101 and miR-151 were associated with onset of obstructive lung disease symptoms more specifically as well as with the onset of symptom in the broader category of all cardiopulmonary ICD-9 codes. miR-30e, although not identified as a classifier of deployment in an independent analysis (Thatcher et al., 2019 <em>JOEM</em> Current Issue) was also associated with both onset of obstructive lung disease and overall cardiopulmonary disease symptoms.</p></section><section id="S16"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">ICD-9 data processing</h3>
<p id="P19">The ICD-9 indicator matrix (coded 0 or 1) was processed using multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) to identify and explore relationships between different ICD-9 codes. The first three principal dimensions that explained 33% of the total variation were selected for downstream analysis. Other than allergic rhinitis, Dimension 1 was primarily associated with cardiovascular symptoms (chest pains, palpitations, abnormal ECG) (12.1% explained variation). Dimension 2 was most closely associated with chronic upper respiratory inflammation—rhinitis and sinusitis—with or without allergy (11% variation). Dimension 3 contained symptoms that have airway obstruction as a common factor. Both non-specific cough in an otherwise healthy person, and exercise induced bronchospasm, can be indicators of asthma-like conditions, and are sometimes coded as asthma (9.8% variation) (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig 2</a>).</p>

<p id="P20">Integrative network analysis identified seven communities of tightly connected (|r|&gt;0.3 and <em>p</em>&lt;0.05) clinical dimensions, molecular markers, and environmental exposures (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig 3</a>). Three of the communities included respective associations with the three ICD9 dimensions, three included associations with the dioxin Principal Components and one community included molecular markers associated with smoking. Among the most apparent correlations, Community 2, containing ICD9 Dim2 (sinusitis/rhinitis), was positively correlated with Community 4 (cotinine) and Community 3, containing ICD9 Dim1 (cardiovascular conditions), was correlated with Community 6 (BPDE).</p>

<p id="P22">A complete list of all significant molecular markers and annotated metabolites in the clusters can be found in <a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Supplementary Table 2</a>. Metabolic pathway analysis showed association between ICD-9 dimensions and cotinine, BPDE, and dioxins and metabolites involved in amino acid, nucleotide, urea cycle, lipid, fatty acid, and glycan metabolism (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig 4</a>). Several pathways such as pyrimidine metabolism, linoleate, and TCA cycle, showed associations with both ICD-9 dimensions and environmental chemicals, suggesting a link between and the exposure metabolome and onset of cardiopulmonary disease outcomes. miR-208 was strongly associated with ICD9-Dim1 (cardiovascular conditions) at r=0.739 (<em>p</em>&lt;0.5). ICD9-Dim1 was also moderately associated with miR-101 (r=0.282; <em>p</em>&lt;0.05) and IgE (r=0.428; <em>p</em>&lt;0.05). Moderate associations were found between ICD9-Dim2 (sinusitis/rhinitis) and miR-101 (r=−0.30;<em>p</em>&lt;0.05), miR-339 (r=0.351;<em>p</em>&lt;0.05), and FMS-like tyrosine kinase ligand (FLT; r=0.416; <em>p</em>&lt;0.05). A moderate positive association was found between BDPE and ICD9 Dim3 (respiratory conditions) at r=0.33 (<em>p</em>&lt;0.05).</p>

<p id="P23">Our laboratory team has previously observed correlations with molecular changes associated with deployment of service personnel to high-risk areas (<a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a>, <a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">12</a>, <a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a>–<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a>, <a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a>–<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a>); (Go, Smith, Woeller, Khan, Thatcher, Krahl, et al 2019 Current Issue). These studies did not include clinical outcomes, however, so association of molecular signals with clinical outcomes remained elusive. The statistical comparison of biomarkers between deployed personnel and controls suggest effects are not related to deployment but are more closely linked to cardiopulmonary symptoms. Though a false discovery correction was not applied for this exploratory study the comparison Control-Onset x Control-Never was used as a control and only markers significant in Case-Onset comparisons were investigated.</p>
<p id="P24">Further in the present study, we took a systems biology approach to develop a computational framework to identify multi-scale associations between clinical, molecular markers, environmental exposures associated with cardiopulmonary outcomes. We observed that non-specific cough and exercise-induced bronchospasm, were within ICD-9 Dim3, and can be indicators of asthma-like conditions as well as coded as asthma (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig 2</a>). These results are what would be expected if the ICD-9 codes identify people with similar symptoms or diseases and therefore adds confidence to the identification of associations between ICD-9 clusters and metabolomic clusters. In addition, the results showed that ICD-9-Dim3 including respiratory codes was most broadly linked to environmental chemicals while ICD-9-Dim2 including sinusitis and rhinitis was negatively linked to ICD-9-Dim3 and positively linked to cotinine, an indicator of tobacco use. ICD-9-Dim1 including cardiovascular conditions showed associations with Community 6 (BPDE; PAHs). These results indicate that cigarette smoking may have an important contribution to sinusitis/rhinitis while environmental exposures to PAHs may have an important contribution to cardiovascular conditions.</p>
<p id="P25">Although preliminary in nature, these findings support the conclusion that integrative analysis has the potential to identify molecular links connecting exposures, biologic responses and disease outcomes.</p>
<p id="P26">Of key importance is whether deployment to an environment containing a known, but poorly documented environmental hazard (burn pits) causes acute or chronic disease, with or without a long latency. We can’t fully address that question in this study, as the sample size is low and only a few service personnel developed symptoms. However, we used this opportunity to develop an approach that can be adapted for future studies. We investigated whether there was any association between onset of cardiopulmonary diseases or symptoms and biomarkers that changed with deployment, using complimentary methods. We identified several miRNA’s (miR-101, miR-151, miR-208, miR-30a and miR-27b) as well other clinically used biomarkers (cholesterol and serum amyloid A) that were found to be associated with deployment and cardiopulmonary disease (<a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">32</a>, <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">33</a>). miRNA’s associate with both exposure (<a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">19</a>, <a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34">34</a>) (Woeller et al 2019 JOEM Current Issue) as well as diseases (<a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35">35</a>–<a href="#R38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R38">38</a>). These miRNA could prove to be useful biomarkers for environmental chemical exposures, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, polychlorinated dibenzodioxins, and polychlorinated dibenzofurans, some of which have relatively rapid biologic elimination after transient exposures.</p>
<p id="P27">Additionally, we identified three principal components which were defined by ICD-9 codes, which represent either a symptom or a diagnosis depending on the designation (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig 2</a>). Once integrated with the metabolome and other molecular data, we found 7 communities which distinguished the 3 clinical components and associated molecular signatures (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig 3</a>). Pathway analysis revealed several key pathways including pyrimidine, linoleate, and TCA cycle; however, many of the significantly associated pathways were central metabolic pathways (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig 4</a>). Some pathways, like the TCA cycle as an example, were also associated with dioxins, but not BPDE or cotinine. These results indicate that multi-scale integrative network analysis could be utilized to tease apart mixture-related effects. This is likely to depend on the information provided and must be considered speculative until further tested and verified in additional studies.</p>
<p id="P28">Despite the exploratory nature of this study, the results show that that data from biomonitoring of environmental chemical exposures can be used with associated metabolome and serum biomarkers to identify molecular links of exposure to onset of symptoms related to deployment. This pilot study is limited by power because the study population was small, and confirmation of the clinical diagnosis was uncertain. Additionally, the Case and Control subjects were selected to match time in service between sample collections and were not age or sex matched. Another limitation is utilization of the ICD codes. Some of the limitations of using ICD codes to link clinical outcomes include potential for misclassification of diagnoses when utilizing administrative medical encounter data. The ICD-9 coded encounters also provide little to no information on the severity of the condition and may underestimate the true health impact. Future studies with this novel systems-level approach are warranted to address these issues by increasing the population of deployed service personnel that have developed specific diseases.</p></section><section id="S19"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CONCLUSIONS</h2>
<p id="P29">Our study presents a novel computational framework for multi-scale integrative network analysis of clinical, molecular, and environmental exposures data. This is a first proof-of-concept study which demonstrates the feasibility to identify novel associations across different layers and link deployment related exposures and molecular responses with health outcomes. Further examination of the proposed framework among a larger population group with confirmation of diagnoses, and validation of the biomarker associations are required.</p></section><section id="SM1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
