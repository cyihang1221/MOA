Abstract


Feature-Based Molecular
Networking (FBMN) on the Global Natural
Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) platform offers a transformative
framework for visualizing drug–drug interaction (DDI)-driven
metabolic reprogramming that conventional analytical approaches fail
to capture. Here, we demonstrate its application as a novel strategy
to resolve complex metabolic alterations arising from DDI-induced
toxicity. Using FBMN-integrated MS
2
 mapping, we established
a proof-of-concept workflow to elucidate large-scale metabolic transitions
and pathway rewiring. As a model system, co-administration of methamphetamine
(MA) and ethanol (EtOH)a combination known to potentiate toxicitywas
analyzed through liquid chromatography–quadrupole time-of-flight
mass spectrometry. Merging MS
2
 spectra across multiple
collision energies revealed diagnostic fragment ions that enabled
network connectivity and comprehensive annotation of 21 MA-related
features, including glucuronide conjugates and in-source fragments.
FBMN visualization uncovered an EtOH-driven metabolic shift, characterized
by suppression of benzene ring hydroxylation and the emergence of 
N
-acetylamphetamine and novel glucuronidated metabolites.
Collectively, these findings establish GNPS-FBMN as a powerful and
generalizable analytical strategy for delineating DDI-associated metabolic
reprogramming, providing mechanistic insight into the metabolic basis
of toxicity arising from drug combinations.




Introduction


Drug–drug interactions (DDIs) remain
a critical challenge
in pharmacology and toxicology, often leading to unexpected adverse
effects and increased clinical risks. In
contrast to therapeutic drugs, for which DDIs are carefully evaluated
during the preclinical phase of development,

,

 the
DDIs of illicit drugs remain largely uncharted despite the pervasive
occurrence of polydrug use. The toxicity of a drug is generally associated
with its concentration at the site of action, which is determined
by its overall pharmacokinetic behavior. Conventional analytical approaches,
such as targeted liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry
(LC–MS/MS) or pharmacokinetic measurements, provide valuable
quantitative information but are limited in their ability to capture
the global metabolic landscape underlying DDIs. DDIs may redirect
the metabolic fate of drugs, thereby elevating the importance of metabolites
that were previously disregarded or considered negligible. This implies that the evaluation of drug combinations
should not focus solely on the major metabolites but rather extend
to the broader spectrum of metabolic changes, highlighting the need
for complementary tools that can systematically visualize and interpret
the complexity of DDI-associated metabolic reprogramming.


Untargeted
MS/MS data-based Global Natural Products Social (GNPS)
feature-based molecular networking (FBMN) has emerged as a versatile
tool, with successful applications in natural products discovery,
metabolomics, and exposomics, where it clusters structurally related
metabolites and accelerates annotation.

−


 Despite these advances,
the application of FBMN to pharmacology and toxicologyparticularly
in the context of DDIs and metabolic toxicityremains limited.
By organizing large-scale metabolic maps according to similarities
in MS/MS fragmentation patterns, FBMN enables intuitive visualization
of metabolite families, annotation of novel derivatives, and prioritization
of biologically relevant features in complex biological matrices without
prior standards or reference libraries. This capacity is particularly
valuable for toxicology, where metabolic reprogramming can generate
low-abundance or previously unrecognized metabolites that may drive
adverse outcomes. Thus, GNPS-FBMN represents an emerging and underutilized
approach with strong potential to uncover the hidden metabolic signatures
underlying DDIs and their toxicological consequences.


In this
study, we demonstrate the utility of FBMN as a tool to
visualize metabolic toxicity arising from DDIs. As a proof-of-concept
case, we examined the interaction between methamphetamine (MA) and
ethanol (EtOH), a drug combination known to exacerbate toxicity and
adverse clinical outcomes. By integrating
FBMN with the metabolic pathway of MA, we highlight EtOH-mediated
alterations that may contribute to toxicity and propose a generalizable
framework for applying FBMN to DDI studies.

As shown in

the number of network clusters (from 63 to 60) compared with the single-CE
data set, whereas the number of connected nodes within the network
increased from 407 to 423. This indicates that each cluster formed
within the molecular network comprises a greater number of interconnected
nodes. In addition, manual inspection of the MS
2
 spectra
of MA and AM revealed cosine similarity scores of 0.86 and 0.93 for
the single-CE and merged multi-CE data sets, respectively, whereas
the number of matched peaks increased from 3 to 8 in the merged multi-CE

These findings demonstrate that extending MS
2
 spectral
information by merging data obtained across multiple CEs generates
additional diagnostic fragment ions, thereby providing richer reference
points for network construction. Furthermore, this observation demonstrates
that CE optimizationemphasized as a critical factor for enhancing
data-dependent acquisition performanceis equally essential
for reliable cluster formation in FBMN.

,

 Without such
optimization, meaningful spectral relationships may remain undetected,
leading to an underestimation of metabolic diversity and substantial
alterations in network topology. Importantly, our results highlight
the necessity of tailoring CE conditions to the structural features
of precursor ions by demonstrating that the limited network connectivitycommonly
encountered in the analysis of small-amine-containing drugs due to
their simple fragmentation patternscan be effectively addressed
through multi-CE spectral merging. This approach not only improves
the robustness of network connectivity by capturing otherwise inaccessible
diagnostic ions but also provides a broadly applicable strategy for
advancing FBMN-based metabolite characterization in toxicological
studies.

Feature-Based Molecular
Networking Revealing Metabolic Toxicity
Associated with MA–EtOH Coadministration


Comprehensive
assessment of drug metabolism is often restricted to the parent compound
and a few primary metabolites due to the limited availability of authentic
standards, a challenge that is particularly evident in studies of
illicit or emerging substances. Because metabolic transformations
frequently follow conserved structural backbones and yield related
fragmentation patterns, the GNPS-FBMN approach provides a robust framework
to visualize global metabolic pathways through network-based clustering. 
Figure 

 presents the networks
constructed from rat urine collected after MA/saline (M/S) or MA/EtOH
(M/E) administration. Two major clusters were identified: one associated
with M0 (MA) (
Figure 

A) and the other with its glucuronide conjugates (
Figure 

B). Their relative abundances
across groups were visualized using semiquantitative information.
In total, 21 MA-related ion features were annotated, including eight
glucuronide conjugates (
Table 

), with corresponding MS
2
 spectra provided in 
Supporting Information
 Figure S1. To evaluate
the validity of the metabolite annotations identified through manual
inspection of the MS
2
 spectra of MA-related metabolites,
we compared the experimental MS
2
 spectra with 
in
silico
 spectra predicted by the NIST-based ICEBERGG model. Metabolites excluding the four confirmed using
authentic standards showed a mean similarity score of 0.57 and a mean
cosine similarity of 0.60 when comparing 
in silico
 and experimental MS
2
 spectra. Although differences in
CE between the two spectra precluded comparison of relative intensity
of each fragment, the observed mean cosine similarity fell within
the accuracy range (0.41–0.73) reported for existing spectral models, indicating that the proposed
annotations in this study are sufficiently reliable (
Figure S2
). M1a (4-HMA) and M1b were identified as structural
isomers and classified as benzyl-ring-hydroxylated metabolites derived
from M0 (MA) (
Figure S1A–C
). Although
M1b could be interpreted as a β-hydroxylated metabolite of MA,
such as ephedrine, its fragmentation pattern did not correspond to
that of the authentic compound. Instead,
consistent with a previous report, M1b
is more likely to represent a benzyl-ring-hydroxylated isomer of MA.
M3a (4-HAM) and M3b, hydroxylated metabolites of M2 (AM), were detected
at retention times of 2.91 and 8.54 min, respectively (
Figure S1D–F
). Whereas M3a (4-HAM) displayed
a fragmentation pattern consistent with benzyl ring hydroxylation,
M3b generated fragment ions at 
m
/
z
 91.0547 and 119.0847, indicating that hydroxylation did not occur
on the benzyl group. Based on a previous study, M3b is proposed to be formed through the 
N
-hydroxylation of AM and its elevated abundance in the M/E group
suggests that EtOH co-administration alters AM hydroxylation pathways.
This EtOH-induced suppression of MA benzyl ring hydroxylation was
further supported by the quantitative results from our previous study. According to previous studies using pig liver
microsomes or post-mortem rabbit tissues, M3b, presumed to be 
N
-hydroxyAM, may mediate the formation of AM through a reduced
nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH)-dependent reduction reaction.

,

 The elevated NADH levels induced by EtOH are likely to promote this
complementary metabolic pathway, leading
to increased abundances of both AM and M3b. The [M + H]
+
 ion of M4 was detected at 
m
/
z
 178.1212,
consistent with the 
N
-acetylated metabolite of M2
(AM) (
Figure S1G
). In this study, M4 was
exclusively detected in the M/E group. Although a previous animal
study reported the presence of 
N
-acetylAM in rat
urine and plasma following administration of 5 mg/kg AM, it was not observed in the M/S group under comparable
MA dosing conditions. Furthermore, in a previous mice behavioral study,
intraperitoneal administration of 
N
-acetylAM induced
biphasic locomotor responsesinitial suppression followed by
hyperactivitydemonstrating its activity as a centrally acting
compound. These findings suggest that
EtOH induces distinct alterations in MA metabolism that may contribute
to the DDI effects between the two drugs, underscoring the importance
of capturing the global metabolic landscape. M5a–c (
Figure S1H
) and M6a–c (
Figure S1I
) exhibited protonated molecular ions at 
m
/
z
 135.0798 and 119.0854, respectively,
corresponding to fragment ions derived from M3a (4-HMA) and M0 (MA).
These signals are interpreted as products of the in-source fragmentation
of precursor ions, which subsequently underwent further collision-cell
fragmentation. Such in-source fragmentation can introduce potential
interpretative errors, thereby emphasizing the necessity of careful
annotation.

The network comprising
eight nodes in 
Figure 

B exhibited the characteristic neutral loss
of glucuronides (−176 Da), revealing product ions derived from
glucuronidation. M7a,b (
Figure S1J,K
) and
M8 (
Figure S1M
) were identified as glucuronides
of benzene-ring-hydroxylated metabolites of MA and AM. As downstream
metabolites of M1a (4-HMA)/M1b or M3a (4-HAM), their reduced relative
abundance in the M/E group supports an EtOH-mediated suppression of
benzene ring hydroxylation. M9 (
Figure S1N
) displayed a protonated molecular ion at 
m
/
z
 372.1634, accompanied by a product ion at 
m
/
z
 196.1314 (−176 Da neutral loss) and characteristic
fragments at 
m
/
z
 165.0895, 137.0593,
and 133.0628. Compared with M0 (MA), this mass shift of +46 Da suggests
that M9 is a glucuronide conjugate of 4-hydroxy-3-methoxy MA (HMMA),
formed through sequential dihydroxylation and methylation. Although
HMMA is primarily recognized as a Phase I metabolite of 3,4-methylenedioxy-MA,
previous study of MA metabolism in rat biofluids and human liver microsomes
supports the plausibility of this annotation. M10 (
Figure S1O
) was identified as a
glucuronidated metabolite of 
N
-acetylAM (M4), which
is generated via benzene ring hydroxylation. Interestingly, while
M4 was more abundant in the M/E group, its glucuronidated derivative
M10 appeared at higher levels in the M/S group, suggesting differential
regulation of downstream conjugation pathways. A distinct cluster
comprising M11, M12, and M13 (
Figure S1P,R
) showed the opposite trend, being predominantly observed in the
M/E group. MS
2
 fragmentation supported their annotation
as glucuronidated metabolites following hydroxylation at 
m
/
z
 150.1266, 166.1206, and 196.1329, respectively.
For M11, the presence of fragment ions at 
m
/
z
 119.0847 and 91.0535 suggested that hydroxylation and
subsequent glucuronidation likely originated from the 
N
-methylated propan-2-amine moiety of MA. M12 and M13 were inferred
to involve additional structural modifications: M12 displayed product
ions at 
m
/
z
 135.0803 and 107.0476,
consistent with benzene ring hydroxylation, while M13 yielded fragment
ions at 
m
/
z
 165.0908, 137.0585,
and 133.0607, indicative of benzene ring dihydroxylation and methylation.


The EtOH-induced suppression of aromatic hydroxylation and the
appearance of 
N
-acetylAM indicate a shift in MA metabolic
routing. Because both 
N
-demethylation and para-hydroxylation
are primarily mediated by cytochrome p450 (CYP) 2D6, the divergent
effects observed under EtOH co-administration are particularly notable.
Although the present data do not establish a definitive mechanism,
previous reports showing EtOH-induced upregulation of CYP3A4

,

an enzyme also involved in MA 
N
-demethylationsuggest
that enzyme-specific induction may partially account for the preferential
enhancement of 
N
-demethylation over aromatic hydroxylation.
Additionally, the exclusive formation of 
N
-acetylAM
in the M/E group is consistent with elevated levels of acetyl-CoA
production during EtOH metabolism, which may promote 
N
-acetyl-transferase-mediated acetylation. Overall, these results
imply that EtOH modulates multiple enzyme systems governing MA biotransformation,
although further mechanistic studies will be required to delineate
these pathways.




Figure 

 depicts
the FBMN-derived metabolic pathway map of MA under the EtOH influence.
Although the detailed sequence leading to the formation of M12 and
M13 remains unresolved, the reduction of M1a (4-HMA)/M1b and M7a,b
in the M/E group indicates that EtOH co-administration appears to
redirect MA metabolism from preferential benzene ring hydroxylation
toward hydroxylation occurring at the 
N
-methylated
propan-2-amine moiety of the molecular scaffold. While the major metabolic
routes of MA have been previously described, our FBMN analysis expanded the scope beyond parent compounds and
a few primary metabolites, enabling the discovery of hidden features
associated with DDIs. While FBMN provides a powerful framework for
visualizing metabolite families and DDI-induced metabolic shifts,
several methodological limitations should be considered. FBMN relies
on MS
2
 spectral similarity for annotation, which does not
guarantee definitive structural identification; closely related isomers
or compounds with shared substructures may yield similar fragmentation
patterns, necessitating confirmation with authentic standards when
available. In addition, FBMN offers semiquantitative information based
on relative MS
1
 feature abundances rather than absolute
concentrations, requiring caution when interpreting biological significance.
Despite these inherent limitations, FBMN remains a valuable screening
tool for rapidly assessing the complex metabolic transitions associated
with DDIs. Despite these limitations, the present results demonstrate
a distinct reprogramming of MA metabolism following EtOH co-administration
and underscore the utility of FBMN in toxicological assessments that
account for such interactions.

Conclusions


This
study demonstrates that GNPS-FBMN provides a powerful framework
to uncover large-scale metabolic reprogramming induced by DDIs. Applied
to MA–EtOH co-administration, FBMN revealed EtOH-driven shifts
in metabolic fateincluding suppression of benzene ring hydroxylation,
emergence of 
N
-acetylAM, and formation of novel glucuronidated
derivativesthat are not captured by conventional analyses.
These findings provide mechanistic evidence that EtOH reshapes MA
metabolism in ways that may contribute to its enhanced toxicity while
establishing FBMN as a complementary strategy to systematically resolve
complex metabolic transitions underlying DDIs and to inform toxicological
risk assessment in the context of polydrug use.
