</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/c982/7346274/d05d0f61831d/ao0c01610_0008.jpg" loading="lazy" id="ab-tgr1" height="374" width="641" alt="graphic file with name ao0c01610_0008.jpg"></p>
<p>Separation
techniques hyphenated to high-resolution mass spectrometry
are essential in untargeted metabolomic analyses. Due to the complexity
and size of the resulting data, analysts rely on computer-assisted
tools to mine for features that may represent a chromatographic signal.
However, this step remains problematic, and a high number of false
positives are often obtained. This work reports a novel approach where
each step is carefully controlled to decrease the likelihood of errors.
Datasets are first corrected for baseline drift and background noise
before the MS scans are converted from profile to centroid. A new
alignment strategy that includes purity control is introduced, and
features are quantified using the original data with scans recorded
as profile, not the extracted features. All the algorithms used in
this work are part of the Finnee Matlab toolbox that is freely available.
The approach was validated using metabolites in exhaled breath condensates
to differentiate individuals diagnosed with asthma from patients with
chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. With this new pipeline, twice
as many markers were found with Finnee in comparison to XCMS-online,
and nearly 50% more than with MS-Dial, two of the most popular freeware
for untargeted metabolomics analysis.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Liquid chromatography
hyphenated to high-resolution mass spectrometry
(LC-HRMS) is a fast-developing technique for untargeted proteomic
and metabolomic analyses.<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a>−<a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> Modern instruments provide unique separation power, high mass resolution,
and accurate mass measurements, allowing the separation, detection,
and quantification of hundreds of compounds, with concentrations spanning
many orders of magnitude, in a single run.<sup><a href="#ref6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref6">6</a></sup> The datasets resulting from a single LC-HRMS run are a collection
of MS spectra gathered along the time and can range from hundreds
of MB to a few GB. Due to the wealth of information that is contained
in the dataset, computer-assisted MS analysis tools are nowadays central
to the analytical scheme.<sup><a href="#ref7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref7">7</a></sup> The main tasks
of these tools are to separate chromatographic-like information from
the noise (mining or feature extraction), to recognize features from
the same compound in the different datasets (peak alignment), and
to analyze all the gathered information (chemometrics or machine learning).<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a>,<a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a>,<a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a>−<a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a></sup> The underlying assumption is that the variations
observed in the data are related to chemical variations in the samples
and thus to the metabolomic activities. However, this assumption is
not always correct, and many observed variations are the result of
processing errors, false positives, and misalignment.<sup><a href="#ref13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref13">13</a>,<a href="#ref14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref14">14</a></sup> For example, Myers and co-workers have recently demonstrated that
30 to 60% false positives,<sup><a href="#ref15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref15">15</a></sup> depending
on the experimental conditions, were detected by two of the most used
packages (XCMS and mzMine).<sup><a href="#ref16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref16">16</a></sup> Such a high
number of false positives are worrying as these will be carried over
throughout the whole analytical pipeline, potentially resulting in
false compound identification.<sup><a href="#ref17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref17">17</a></sup> This issue
is not specific to free software; indeed, Li and co-workers compared
two commercial software (MarkerView and Compound Discoverer) with
three free alternatives (MS-Dial, MZmine 2, XCMS), and similar performances
in the detection of correct features derived from compounds in the
mixtures were observed.<sup><a href="#ref18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref18">18</a></sup></p>
<p>Different
controls have been proposed to improve the reliability
of the features mined in metabolomics.<sup><a href="#ref13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref13">13</a>,<a href="#ref19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref19">19</a></sup> One of the
most relevant concepts introduced in untargeted analyses was the quality
control sample (QC).<sup><a href="#ref13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref13">13</a>,<a href="#ref19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref19">19</a>−<a href="#ref21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref21">21</a></sup> QCs should
be representative of the composition and matrix of all samples. They
are generally made of small aliquots of all target samples, and they
are injected between samples at regular intervals. Features are mined
and aligned between all samples and QC. Features present in all QC
samples with a low quantitative coefficient of variation are likely
to be well processed; only these features should be used for the sample.
With complex samples, more than 50% features are often discarded after
QC evaluation.<sup><a href="#ref22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref22">22</a></sup> This number will increase
if minor peaks are considered. While QCs allow removal of many false-positive
features (noise or baseline recognized as a peak), correct features
can also be discarded, and this is principally due to errors during
the different computerized steps. Maximizing extracted aligned features
while decreasing false positives is one of the main challenges in
untargeted metabolomics,<sup><a href="#ref7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref7">7</a></sup> and improving
and validating the algorithms used during untargeted data mining will
contribute to this goal.</p>
<p>Finnee is a Matlab toolbox designed
for analysis in hyphenated
datasets. Previous works describe a data cleaning approach, with algorithms
to correct baseline drift and background noise. The corrections were
made with MS scans recorded as profile, and it has been demonstrated
that this step allows reduction in the number of false positives,
even with peaks with low intensities.<sup><a href="#ref23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref23">23</a>,<a href="#ref24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref24">24</a></sup> This manuscript
presents the next steps with feature extraction, QC alignment methodologies
using a constrained clustering approach, and targeted reanalysis using
the original data. Algorithms are described and validated by comparing
the metabolomic signatures of exhaled breath condensates (EBCs) of
asthmatic individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
patients.</p>
<p>EBCs are obtained by condensation of gases and droplets
released
during exhalation.<sup><a href="#ref25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref25">25</a>−<a href="#ref28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref28">28</a></sup> This sampling procedure is suitable for patients of all age groups,
irrespective of the disease severity, as it is safe, noninvasive,
easy, and straightforward. Traditionally, pulmonologists focus their
attention on nonspecific characteristics of the EBC, such as the pH
that correlates with airway inflammation,<sup><a href="#ref29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref29">29</a></sup> but it has also been demonstrated that EBCs are a source of biomarkers
and can elucidate the inflammation process of the lungs, such as asthma
or COPD.<sup><a href="#ref30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref30">30</a>−<a href="#ref34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref34">34</a></sup> However, these biomarkers are often present at low concentrations,
which complicates the analysis.<sup><a href="#ref35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref35">35</a></sup> Results
obtained with Finnee are compared with XCMS-online<sup><a href="#ref36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref36">36</a>,<a href="#ref37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref37">37</a></sup> and MS-Dial.<sup><a href="#ref38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref38">38</a></sup></p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results and Discussion</h2>
<section id="sec2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Workflow
of the Computerized Approach for Finnee</h3>
<p>The
workflow developed in this work is separated into three steps; steps
1 and 2 are illustrated in <a href="#sch1" class="usa-link">Scheme <a href="#sch1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="sch1"><h4 class="obj_head">Scheme 1. Schematic Illustration of the Workflow of the Computerized
Finnee
Approach Used in This Study.</h4>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=7346274_ao0c01610_0005.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/c982/7346274/886abf2865ee/ao0c01610_0005.jpg" loading="lazy" id="gr5" height="663" width="750" alt="Scheme 1"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/sch1/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></figure><p>Step 1: Extracting features of interest – This step is similar
to classical pipelines and aims to find and align features along all
QCs. Briefly, after the acquisition, datasets resulting from each
QC sample are converted from their proprietary format to the open
mzML format.<sup><a href="#ref39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref39">39</a></sup> For this work, <em>msConvert</em>,<sup><a href="#ref40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref40">40</a></sup> supported by <em>ProteoWizard</em>,<sup><a href="#ref41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref41">41</a></sup> was used. MS scans should be obtained
as profile (or continuous) spectra rather than centroid (or discrete)
spectra.<sup><a href="#ref42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref42">42</a></sup> The QCs are cleaned from baseline
drift and then from background noise using Finnee<sup><a href="#ref23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref23">23</a>,<a href="#ref24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref24">24</a></sup> (see <a href="#sec2.2" class="usa-link">Data Cleaning</a>) before converting
the MS scans from profile to centroid spectra. QCs can now be mined
for chromatographic-like features (see <a href="#sec2.3" class="usa-link">Peak Mining</a>). Figures of merit for all peaks from each QC are summarized in
a peak list. In this work, a constrained clustering approach was developed
to align features present in the QC (see <a href="#sec2.4" class="usa-link">Peak List
Alignment</a>). At the end of this step, a series of features present
in one or more QCs are obtained. These are characterized by their
coordinate (accurate mass, <em>m</em><sub>ac</sub>, and centroid
time, <em>t</em><sub>c</sub>) and peak area. Only features
present in more than 50% QCs are retained.</p>
<p>Step 2: Optimization
and validation – Features, previously
selected, are reanalyzed from the original data using the region of
interest (ROI) (see <a href="#sec2.5" class="usa-link">Region of Interest (ROI) and
Quantification</a>). Quantification is performed in two steps.
First, the peak limits (times at peak start and peak end) are obtained
for each feature in every QC. Then, for each feature, consolidated
peak limits are obtained. All features are analyzed for a third time
using these common limits. Features are filtered to only retain the
ones common to all QCs with a relative standard deviation of their
area below a set threshold (in this work, 20%).</p>
<p>Step 3: Sample
analysis – Samples are analyzed sequentially
using the original data with the ROI and the consolidated limits.
Only features previously selected are mined for. This approach not
only allows analysis of the target features with high reliability
but also avoids the need to align hundreds of peak lists with the
corresponding computer limitation.</p></section><section id="sec2.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data Cleaning</h3>
<p>Data cleaning is used to remove needless
information in datasets. The procedure has been modified from previous
works.<sup><a href="#ref23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref23">23</a>,<a href="#ref24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref24">24</a></sup><a href="#sch2" class="usa-link">Scheme <a href="#sch2" class="usa-link">2</a></a> details the different steps used in the data cleaning
process.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="sch2"><h4 class="obj_head">Scheme 2. Schematic Illustration of the Data Cleaning Workflow Used in
This
Study.</h4>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/c982/7346274/d85cd4bc8b56/ao0c01610_0006.jpg" loading="lazy" id="gr6" height="2478" width="792" alt="Scheme 2"></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/sch2/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></figure><p>Briefly, for each run, the dataset, converted to mzML,
is opened
using the Finnee toolbox.<sup><a href="#ref43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref43">43</a></sup> The master
mzAxis (Mmz) is calculated using the most intense spectrum. All MS
spectra are then aligned to the Mmz using a linear interpolation algorithm.
This simple transformation allows use of a common <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> axis for all spectra. Data can easily be read
at a given time to obtain the MS spectrum or at a given <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value. The profile obtained at a specific <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value is named a single <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> profile (SMP). It should be emphasized that this
approach is different from the binning techniques,<sup><a href="#ref9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref9">9</a></sup>. Extracted ion profiles (EIPs) can be obtained summing
nearby SMPs.</p>
<p>The SMP that suffers from the influence of background
ions can
be detected due to the low number of nonzero intensities.<sup><a href="#ref24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref24">24</a></sup> These can be corrected for baseline drift using,
for example, the asymmetrically reweighted penalized least squares
(arPLS) algorithms.<sup><a href="#ref44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref44">44</a></sup> The noise at each <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> is estimated, and points, whose neighbor
intensities are lower than 10 times the value of the noise, are considered
as background noise, and their intensities are set to zero.<sup><a href="#ref24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref24">24</a></sup></p></section><section id="sec2.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Peak Mining</h3>
<p>MS spectra are converted
from profile to
centroid,<sup><a href="#ref23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref23">23</a></sup> and chromatographic-like features
are recognized and extracted as data points, in successive scans,
whose centroid <em>m</em>/z does not differ by more than a
set value.<sup><a href="#ref23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref23">23</a></sup> For each feature, figures
of merit (FOMs) are calculated (including the chromatographic statistical
moments<sup><a href="#ref23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref23">23</a></sup> and accurate masses<sup><a href="#ref42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref42">42</a></sup>) and are summarized in peak tables. Because
the datasets have already been corrected for baseline drift and background
noise, no deconvolution or baseline correction algorithms are used.
A detected feature may be a single ion, but it can also be multiple,
nonbaseline-separated, isobaric ions.</p></section><section id="sec2.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Peak List Alignment</h3>

<p>In this
work, following the determination of putative features, defined by
their accurate masses and centroid time, a targeted approach was implemented
to extract an ROI in the original data for each feature. The ROI is
an <em>m</em> × <em>n</em> matrix that contains
all points from the original dataset, whose time and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> are within a set interval. For this work, the
time interval corresponded to the centroid time of the targeted feature
±1 min, and the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> interval
spanned 11 increments in the Mmz centered around the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> closest to the accurate mass of the target features.
An example of an ROI with <em>t</em><sub>c</sub> = 1.95 min

<p>As final validation

with the optimized parameters: asthma vs COPD, asthma vs control,
and COPD vs control. After completion, results were exported, and
the features were aligned with the QC samples (see the Supporting
Information). Only features aligned with QC features with the previous
constraints were selected. Triplicates were pooled, features were
aligned, and those not present in all triplicates were discarded.
For each feature, the average area was calculated.</p>
<p>Welch’s <em>t</em>-test (<em>t</em>-test assuming unequal variance)
was used to select features in which the mean of the peak areas is
significantly different between two groups (<em>p</em> &lt;
0.05, <em>n</em> = 5 in each group). The same approach was
used for MS-Dial. The areas were not corrected for instrumental variation
with the QC samples. However, pooling the replicates that were injected
at random positions within the sample sequence allows the effect of
this bias to be diminished. For Finnee, the samples were analyzed
using the ROI with consolidated peak limits, optimized using the QC
samples. Previously, data in triplicate were averaged, and Welch’s <em>t</em>-test was used to retain features where the assumption
of equal means at the 5% significance level (<em>h</em> &gt;
0.05) was not verified.</p>

<p>The different tools developed in the Finnee toolbox aim to process
hyphenated MS datasets with thoroughness. In this work, the algorithms
have been used to recognize peaks, at low intensities, in EBC samples.
With this data, Finnee outperformed XCMS with 82% more markers discovered
and MS-Dial with 43% more markers. This result should not be extrapolated
to other studies; however, this demonstrated the validity of the pipeline.
It should be emphasized that the detected features should not be considered
yet as markers of asthma and COPD diseases. The samples analyzed were
few, and the markers should also be identified to determine if they
are endogenous metabolites or exogenous chemicals. The aim of this
work was to design tools that will allow extraction of markers at
low intensities. The pipeline will be used in a large-scale metabolomics
project aiming at obtaining reliable markers for COPD, asthma, and
asthma COPD overlap in EBC samples.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Experimental Section and
Computational Methods</h2>
<section id="sec4.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">EBC Samples and Clinical Assessment</h3>
<p>EBC samples were
collected from 15 individuals randomly selected (five controls, five
with asthma medical diagnosis, and five with COPD medical diagnosis,
as assessed by the OLDER Study – Obstructive Lung Diseases
in Elders). The Ethics Committee of Nova Medical School approved this
study.</p>
<p>Asthma was assigned when the patient reported respiratory
symptoms, was a nonsmoker, and presented a positive reversibility
test (FEV1 &gt; 12% and 200 mL). COPD disease was attributed to those
who also reported being current smokers, had a post-bronchodilator
FEV1/FVC &lt; 0.70, and had a negative reversibility test.</p></section><section id="sec4.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LC–MS
Analysis</h3>
<p>Samples were analyzed in triplicate
by LC–MS using an Orbitrap Q Exactive Focus (Thermo Scientific)
coupled to an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC (Thermo Scientific). A pooled quality
control (QC) sample was used to compensate for any possible time-dependent
batch effects. The QC samples were created using a small aliquot from
each sample. The QC samples were reinjected at regular intervals to
bracket the samples. The separation was performed using a Waters XBridge
column C18 (2.1 × 150 mm, 3.5 μm particle size, P/N 186003023).
The mobile phase A was water with 0.1% formic acid (v/v), and mobile
phase B was acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (v/v) (Optima LC–MS
Grade, Fisher Scientific). The gradient program was as follows: 1
min at 1% B; 1–13 min from 1 to 99% B, 13–15 min at
99% B, 15–16 min from 99 to 1% B, and 4 min at 1% B. The column
temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and a flow rate of 400 μL/min
was used.</p>
<p>The Q Exactive Focus MS method consisted of several
cycles of full MS scan (<em>R</em> = 70000) followed by three
ddMS2 scans (<em>R</em> = 17500), with a (N)CE of 30 and in
positive mode. External calibration was performed using LTQ ESI Positive
Ion Calibration Solution (Thermo Scientific) and the lock mass enabled
internal calibration. Data were obtained using the Xcalibur software
v.4.0.27.19 (Thermo Scientific). The raw MS files, as recorded by
the instrument, are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable
request.</p></section><section id="sec4.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Programming</h3>
<p>Finnee was developed using Matlab R2019a
(Mathworks). The toolbox is open-access and freely accessible, including
the new functions that were designed for this manuscript. The Matlab
code used in this study is available in Zenodo with the identifier
doi: <a href="http://10.5281/zenodo.3581436" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">10.5281/zenodo.3581436</a>.<sup><a href="#ref43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref43">43</a></sup> The code source can also be downloaded
from GitHub (<a href="https://github.com/glerny/finnee2016/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://github.com/glerny/finnee2016/</a>). For this work, functions were programmed and run using a PC equipped
with an Intel Core i7 CPU (2.80 GHz) and 18.0 GB RAM.</p>
<p>XCMS online
