</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abs0010"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="sec1_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Background</h3>
<p>Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is a neuropsychiatric disorder linked to liver dysfunction, commonly occurring in cirrhosis or acute liver failure. Existing treatments like lactulose and rifaximin provide only symptomatic relief and cause gastrointestinal side effects, emphasizing the need for alternative therapies. <em>Tapyadi Loha</em> (TL), a classical Ayurvedic herbomineral formulation, is traditionally used in clinical practice for HE lacks scientific evidence.</p></section><section id="sec2_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objectives</h3>
<p>To perform preliminary evaluation of TL in the management of HE through <em>in silico</em> and <em>in vitro</em> analysis.</p></section><section id="sec3_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Material and methods</h3>
<p>Activity markers of TL were screened using IMPPAT, SwissADME, and MolSoft based on ≥30 % oral bioavailability and drug-likeness scores of 0–1. Potential HE-related targets were identified through SwissTarget Prediction, SuperPred, BindingDB, and GeneCards, with overlapping targets analyzed via PPI networks using STRING and Cytoscape. Molecular docking in Schrodinger's Maestro revealed good binding affinity phytoconstituents, which were quantified by LC-MS/MS as per CCRAS guidelines. Their hepatoprotective activity was validated in HepG2 cells, while antioxidant effects were confirmed using the DPPH assay.</p></section><section id="sec4_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>Gene ontology and KEGG analyses revealed enrichment in cancer and hepatitis B pathways, notably NF-κB, TNF, and TLR signalling. Docking studies identified strong binding of gallic acid, quercetin, ascorbic acid, and luteolin, validated by LC-MS/MS. <em>In vitro</em>, TL treatment significantly improved HepG2 cell viability in thioacetamide-induced toxicity in a dose dependent manner, confirming its hepatoprotective potential. Additionally, TL demonstrated antioxidant activity in the DPPH assay.</p></section><section id="sec5_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p>The study examined the potential molecular targets, important genes, and signalling pathways of TL and its connection to HE using a network pharmacology approach. HepG2 cell line research showed that TL increased cell viability in a dose-dependent way. The study supports TL's possible neuroprotective and hepatoprotective benefits.</p></section><section id="kwrds0010" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Hepatic encephalopathy, Herbomineral formulation, Network pharmacology, Molecular docking, <em>Tapyadi Loha</em></p></section></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0010">Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is a neuropsychiatric disorder that often arises due to portosystemic venous shunting, which may occur with or without the presence of intrinsic liver disease. The earlier symptoms associated with HE includes reduced awareness of surroundings, yawning, and drowsiness. As the condition progresses, individuals may exhibit aggression and maniacal excitement. Precipitating factors for HE encompasses infections, alcohol consumption, gastrointestinal bleeding, constipation, a high-protein diet, zinc deficiency, and the use of medications such as benzodiazepines and diuretics [<a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1">1</a>,<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">2</a>]. The prevalence varies based on factors such as the severity of liver disease, the presence of precipitating factors, and the healthcare practices viz. minimal hepatic encephalopathy (MHE), a subclinical form of HE, is estimated to affect 20 %–60 % of patients with liver disease [<a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">3</a>]. A population-based study has indicated that, approximately 44 % of individuals with cirrhosis are at risk of developing HE within first five years [<a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4">4</a>]. The incidence of cirrhosis, a major primary risk factor for HE, has been steadily rising in recent years. As a result, an increase in the clinical and healthcare burdens associated with HE is anticipated in the coming years [<a href="#bib5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib5">5</a>].</p>
<p id="p0015">The putative mechanism underlying HE is not fully understood as numerous precipitating factors are involved leading to HE. Ammonia is considered to play an important role in the development of HE. A study conducted by Bernel et al. reported that 55 % of patients had blood ammonia concentration of &gt;200 μmol/L [<a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6">6</a>] wherein, blood ammonia level ≥150 μmol/L lead to cerebral herniation and death [<a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7">7</a>]. In healthy individual, the ammonia from the gut enters the portal circulation of liver converting it to urea through urea cycle. An ammonia that bypasses urea cycle is detoxified to glutamine with the help of glutamine synthetase present in the hepatocytes as well as in astrocytes. In acute or chronic liver condition the capacity of liver to metabolize the ammonia is compromised leading to increased systemic blood ammonia [<a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8">8</a>]. The excess of ammonia from blood goes to brain, where it is converted to glutamine by astrocytes, to increase osmotic stress and astrocyte swelling. Aldridge et al. mentioned about the ‘Trojan horse’ (Glutamine) hypothesis, in which the phosphate activated glutaminase (PAG) metabolizes the excess glutamine in the mitochondria to ammonia and glutamate leading to swelling of the astroglia cells resulting in brain edema and oxidative stress [<a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9">9</a>]. The astrocyte swelling, brain edema and oxidative stress activates the cascade of several pro-inflammatory cytokines but not limiting to NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1<em>β</em>. HE remains a complex and incompletely understood condition, and current therapeutic options are largely limited to symptomatic relief without effectively halting disease progression. The major emphasis of treatment is reduction of ammonia production, to promote its fixation and excretion. Unfortunately, only few drugs are approved still for HE treatment, Rifaximin and lactulose that work symptomatically. Rifaximin is required to be given at very high dose as well as exhibits severe side effects such as peripheral edema, nausea, dizziness, fatigue, muscle spasms and gastrointestinal upset [<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">10</a>]. On the other hand, lactulose that acts by reducing production and increasing excretion of ammonia. It suffers from gastric distress as side effect. These limitations have led many patients to seek alternative treatment approaches for HE. <em>Tapyadi Loha</em> (TL), a classical Ayurvedic herbomineral formulation, is traditionally used in clinical practice for HE lacks scientific evidence. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation of TL using <em>in silico</em>, <em>in vitro</em>, and <em>in vivo</em> methodologies is warranted. The present study aims to elucidate the beneficial effects of TL in HE through systematic <em>in silico</em> and <em>in vitro</em> evaluations, providing a scientific basis for its traditional use.</p>
<p id="p0020"><em>Tapyadi loha</em> a herbomineral formulation which is prepared as per the Indian Ayurvedic traditional medicine called ‘<em>Yogaraja’</em> (Charak Samhita). The formulation comprises of combination of herbs and minerals such as <em>Amalaki, Haritaki, Behada, Shunthi, Pippali, Chiktrakmula, Vidanga, Shilajit, Rajat bhasma, Swarnamakshik bhasma</em>, and <em>Loha bhasma</em> which are helpful in the treatment of anemia, chronic diseases, jaundice, skin disorders, diabetes, respiratory issues as well as in the detoxification of the body (<em>Pandu adhya</em>). In this preliminary study, the probable molecular and cellular targets of TL in HE was explored with the help of network pharmacology and molecular docking. Further, standardization of the TL was carried out as per General guidelines for drug development of Ayurvedic formulation (Volume 1) given by the Central Council for Research in Ayurvedic Sciences (CCRAS), Ministry of AYUSH, Government of India, New Delhi (General guidelines for drug development of ayurvedic formulations, 2018) followed by an <em>in</em><em>vitro</em> study to explore hepatoprotective activity in HepG2 cell line and antioxidant activity by 1,1-diphenyl-2-picryl hydrazyl (DPPH) assay.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Material and methods</h2>
<section id="sec2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Material</h3>
<p id="p0025"><em>Tapyadi loha</em> was obtained from Shaileya Rasashala (Mumbai, India). Network pharmacology analysis was conducted using a combination of software and databases, including the Indian Medicinal Plant, Phytochemistry and Therapeutics (IMPPAT) database, SwissADME, MolSoft, BindingDB, SwissTargetPrediction, SuperPred, GeneCards, UniProt (<a href="https://www.uniprot.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.uniprot.org</a>), STRING, Venny 2.1.0, and Cytoscape (version 3.10.3). For molecular docking studies, tools such as ChemDraw, Schrödinger's Maestro module, and the Protein Data Bank (RCSB) were utilized.</p>
<p id="p0030">HepG2 cells were procured from the National Centre for Cell Science (Pune, India). Cell culture reagents included high-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin, streptomycin were obtained from Invitrogen, USA), and bovine serum albumin (BSA), 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and silymarin were procured by (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and DPPH obtained from TCI, India.</p></section><section id="sec2.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Screening of phytoconstituents and targets of TL</h3>
<p id="p0035"><em>Tapyadi loha</em> is a herbomineral formulation consist of several herbal component such as <em>Amalaki</em> (<em>Emblica officinalis</em> Linn.), <em>Haritaki</em> (<em>Terminalia chebula</em> Retz.), <em>Behada</em> (<em>Terminalia bellirica</em> Roxb.), <em>Shunthi</em> (<em>Zingiber officinale</em> Rosc., Ginger), <em>Pippali</em> (<em>Piper longum</em>), <em>Chiktrakmula</em> (<em>Plumbago zeylanica</em> Linn.), <em>Vidanga</em> (<em>Embelia ribes</em>) along with bhasma's (minerals) that includes <em>Shilajit</em> (Asphaltum punjabianum), <em>Rajat</em> (Silver) <em>bhasma</em>, <em>Swarnamakshik</em> (Ore of Copper and Iron pyrite) <em>bhasma</em>, <em>Loha</em> (Iron) <em>bhasma</em>. The phytoconstituents of each plant were taken from IMPPAT database. The selection of phytoconstituents was carried out based on the literature, oral bioavailability of 30 % or more and/or drug likeliness between 0 and 1. The bioavailability of the phytoconstituents were checked using SwissADME and drug-likeliness using MolSoft (online platforms).</p></section><section id="sec2.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Prediction of targets and construction of protein-protein interaction network of TL for HE</h3>
<p id="p0040">Following the selection of phytoconstituents, predicted target were exported for each phytoconstituent along with their gene name and UniProt ID from Binding DB (Similarity index of &lt;0.7), Swiss Target Prediction, and SuperPred. For disease, targets were extracted from Genecards database using the term ‘Hepatic Encephalopathy.’ All the predicted target for both phytoconstituents and disease was imported to Venny 2.1.0 to identify the common targets and draw a Venn diagram. The UniProt ID of common targets were imported to STRING database to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. This PPI network was sent to Cytoscape 3.10.3 software to filter the disconnected node and identify the top 25 core targets based on the degree sorted values.</p></section><section id="sec2.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. Constructing network of TL for HE</h3>
<p id="p0045">The PPI network imported to Cytoscape yielded the top 25 core targets. A comprehensive network of TL, it's phytoconstituents and disease targets were constructed using CytoHubba using all respective drug attributes and disease attributes.</p></section><section id="sec2.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis</h3>
<p id="p0050">The top 25 core targets were analyzed for the GO functions and KEGG pathways using ShinyGO 0.81 online platform. Further, using KEGG, the pathways and the genes involved in the disease were selected for molecular docking.</p></section><section id="sec2.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Molecular docking of selected target gene</h3>
<p id="p0055">The molecular docking was conducted for the protein involved in the KEGG pathway associated with HE such as TLR-4 (PDB: <a href="https://www.rcsb.org/structure/https://www.rcsb.org/structure/3FXI" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">3FXI</a>), NF-κB (PDB: <a href="https://www.rcsb.org/structure/https://www.rcsb.org/structure/1RAM" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">1RAM</a>), TNF-α (PDB: <a href="https://www.rcsb.org/structure/https://www.rcsb.org/structure/2AZ5" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2AZ5</a>), IL-6 (PDB:<a href="https://www.rcsb.org/structure/https://www.rcsb.org/structure/1ALU" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">1ALU</a>), IL-1β (PDB: <a href="https://www.rcsb.org/structure/https://www.rcsb.org/structure/1l1B" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">1l1B</a>), Nrf2 (PDB: <a href="https://www.rcsb.org/structure/https://www.rcsb.org/structure/6QMC" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">6QMC</a>), HO-1 (PDB: <a href="https://www.rcsb.org/structure/https://www.rcsb.org/structure/1N45" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">1N45</a>). The X-ray crystallographic structures of the selected targets from the KEGG pathway were retrieved from the protein data bank (<a href="http://www.rcsb.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.rcsb.org</a>) and imported into Schrodinger's Maestro module. Protein preparation was carried out using protein preparation wizard to optimize hydrogen bond, remove excess water molecules greater than 5 Å followed by energy minimization. Further, the Sitemap tool was used to assess the potential binding sites of the proteins. One best site was chosen for each protein on the basis of their site scores and site volumes, followed by the generation of receptor grids around these sites. Ligands were separately drawn in ChemDraw software and imported into the Schrodinger software's workspace. LigPrep tool assisted in the ligand preparation and flexible ligand docking was carried out in Standard Precision mode [<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>].</p></section><section id="sec2.7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.7. Standardization of TL</h3>
<p id="p0060">Standardisation of the herbomineral formulation was conducted by performing the parameters as per General Guidelines for Drug Development of Ayurvedic Formulations, CCRAS, 2018 such as description of test drug, organoleptic characters, pH, Loss on drying, total ash, Acid-insoluble ash, <em>nishchandrica</em> (lusterless), r<em>ekha purnatva</em> (fineness), v<em>aritara</em> (float on water), n<em>irthoom</em> (smokeless), <em>niswadu</em> (tasteless), a<em>purnabhava</em> (irreversible), elemental analyses and quantification of marker compounds (General guidelines for drug development of ayurvedic formulations, 2018).</p></section><section id="sec2.8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.8. Elemental analysis of bhasma</h3>
<p id="p0065"><em>Tapyadi loha</em> consisted of <em>rajat bhasma, suvarnamashika bhasma</em> and <em>loha bhasma.</em> The elemental analysis was performed by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy to evaluate the quantity.</p></section><section id="sec2.9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.9. Quantification of some marker compounds using LC-MS/MS</h3>
<p id="p0070">Quantification was carried out using the calibration curve method. Sample analysis was performed with an LC-MS/MS instrument (4000 QTrap coupled with Shimadzu LC 20 AD). The column used was a Thermo Hypersil Gold C18 (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm), with the temperature maintained at 40 °C, and a Hamilton microliter syringe for a 20 μl injection volume. The mobile phase consisted of Solvent A (0.5 % acetic acid in water) and Solvent B (LCMS grade methanol), with a diluent of Methanol: Water (80:20). The total run time was 25 min, with a flow rate of 1 ml/min, and the gradient program, was as follows: 100 % B for 4 min, 50 % B for 6 min, 20 % B for 10 min, 50 % B for 2 min, and 100 % B for 3 min. MS conditions included electrospray ionization in negative polarity, multiple reaction monitoring scan type, curtain gas at 10 psi, collision gas at 7 psi, ion spray voltage of −4500 V, and a temperature of 500 °C. Data analysis was conducted using Analyst 1.6.3 software.</p>
<section id="sec2.9.1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.9.1. Sample preparation</h4>
<p id="p0075">A freshly prepared stock solution of TL (20 mg/ml) was vortexed and sonicated for 15 min, then brought to a total volume of 10 ml with additional methanol. It was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at room temperature. The sample was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter, and aliquots were prepared for injection.</p></section><section id="sec2.9.2"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.9.2. Optimization of standard</h4>
<p id="p0080">The 1000 ppm stock solution was utilized to prepare a 5 ng solution of gallic acid, quercetin, and ascorbic acid. Each standard was injected to verify the <em>m/z</em> values. After confirming the <em>m/z</em> values, further linearity assessment was conducted.</p></section><section id="sec2.9.3"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.9.3. Linearity</h4>
<p id="p0085">Serial dilutions of 1 ng, 10 ng, 50 ng, 100 ng, 500 ng, 1000 ng, and 2000 ng were prepared for each standard (gallic acid, quercetin, ascorbic acid) using the diluent. These dilutions were then injected, and the resulting data was used to assess linearity.</p></section></section><section id="sec2.10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">
<em>2.10</em>. <em>Invitro</em> hepatoprotective activity of TL on HepG2 cell line</h3>
<section id="sec2.10.1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.10.1. HepG2 cell culture</h4>
<p id="p0090">HepG2 cells were cultured in high glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum, FBS (Complete Media), 100IU/mL of penicillin and 100 μg of streptomycin and incubated in humidified atmosphere with 5 % CO2 at 37 °C.</p></section><section id="sec2.10.2"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.10.2. Cell viability assay</h4>
<p id="p0095">HepG2 cells were seeded at a density of 25 x 103 cells/well in 96 well plate with Complete Media. After 24h, the complete media was replaced with serum starvation media (DMEM with 0.1 % BSA) and incubated for 4h. Cells were treated with various concentrations of silymarin (used as standard) (12.5 μg/ml, 25 μg/ml, 50 μg/ml, 100 μg/ml, 200 μg/ml) and TL (31.25 μg/ml, 62.5 μg/ml, 125 μg/ml, 250 μg/ml, 500 μg/ml). Given that these are preliminary investigations and no evidence exists regarding the <em>in vitro</em> effects of TL, the concentrations of thioacetamide and TL were determined by a trial-and-error approach utilising geometric progression. After 1 h of TL and silymarin treatment, the cells were incubated with 5 mM Thioacetamide (TAA) 24h to assess the protective effect of TL and silymarin against TAA. After 24h, 100 μL of MTT solution was added to each well and incubated for 2h at 37 °C in the dark. MTT solution was then removed, and formazan crystals were dissolved with 100 μL dimethyl sulfoxide; absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. The cell viability % was calculated with the following formula:</p>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="ufd1"><tr><td class="formula"><span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML">Cell viability % = (Absorbance of treatment/ Absorbance of Control) x100</span></td></tr></table></section></section><section id="sec2.11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.11. Antioxidant activity of TL</h3>
<p id="p0100">The antioxidant activity of TL was carried out using DPPH technique. The serial dilution of TL (1 mg/ml - 64 mg/ml) and ascorbic acid (15.6 μg/ml – 1 mg/ml) was poured in respective wells. The plate was kept in dark for 30 min and absorbance was noted at 517 nm. Below formula was used to calculate the percentage of antioxidant.</p>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="ufd2"><tr><td class="formula"><span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML">% of antioxidant activity = [(Ac–As) ÷ Ac] × 100</span></td></tr></table>
<p>Where, Ac- Control absorbance; As- Test sample absorbance.</p></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results</h2>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/tbl6/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Discussion</h2>
<p id="p0155">The increasing prevalence of liver diseases has raised significant concern regarding the incidence of HE. A major contributor to the decline in patients’ quality of life is the widespread lack of awareness about this condition. HE is characterized by a spectrum of neuropsychiatric symptoms, including impairments in motor function, cognition, and behaviour. Despite its clinical burden, therapeutic options for HE remains limited and have seen little advancement over the past three decades, largely due to an incomplete understanding of its pathogenesis [<a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12">12</a>]. This underscores the urgent need for a multi-targeted therapeutic approach to effectively manage HE.</p>
<p id="p0160"><em>Tapyadi loha</em>, an Ayurvedic herbomineral formulation which consists of 7 plant components which have been previously reported for its hepatoprotective, neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory activity such as <em>Terminalia chebula</em> [<a href="#bib13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib13">13</a>], <em>Terminalia bellirica</em> [<a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">14</a>], <em>Emblica officinalis</em> [<a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a>], <em>Piper longum</em> [<a href="#bib16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib16">16</a>,<a href="#bib17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib17">17</a>], and <em>Embelia ribes</em> [<a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18">18</a>]. Moreover, TL contains more than 30 phytoconstituents, thus it is more likely to work by multi target approach.</p>
<p id="p0165">A total of 11 active phytoconstituents from TL were evaluated based on their bioavailability and drug-likeness scores. We identified 791 unique targets from a total of 1613 targets related to TL, as well as 5801 targets associated with hepatitis E, sourced from various databases. Among these, 437 common targets were recognized, leading to a network with 430 nodes and 2031 edges as provided by STRING analysis.</p>
<p id="p0170">The top 25 genes were selected based on their degree-sorted values, resulting in a network comprising 438 nodes and 872 edges. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed 184 pathways, with liver-associated hepatitis B ranking among the top five pathways containing the highest number of genes linked to cancer pathways. The inflammatory pathways identified included the NF-kappa β signalling pathway and the TNF signalling pathway, among others which are also major pathway associated with HE.</p>
<p id="p0175">The gene ontology basically helps us to understand the active structure of cellular components, biological process such as pathways involved in the process and molecular function such as binding site or enzyme activity of the specific gene [<a href="#bib19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib19">19</a>]. According to the gene ontology for biological processes, the highest number of genes involved along with significant -log10 (FDR) values were associated with responses to organonitrogen compounds, oxygen-containing compounds, cellular responses to chemical stimuli, and cell death. Given the involvement of both disease-related and formulation-related genes, hepatitis B (liver related) emerged as a key pathway warranting further investigation through <em>in vitro</em> and <em>in vivo</em> studies.</p>
<p id="p0180">Molecular docking studies were performed on proteins implicated in the KEGG enrichment analysis, including TLR-4, NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, Nrf2, HIF-1 and HO-1. Components exhibiting docking scores lower than −5 kcal/mol were deemed to have good binding potential, their 2D molecular interactions are illustrated in <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>. Among the phytoconstituents evaluated, gallic acid, quercetin, ascorbic acid, luteolin, and ellagic acid demonstrated strong binding affinities to multiple protein targets implicated in the KEGG pathway associated with Hepatitis B. Gallic acid has been reported to enhance hepatic function by modulating liver function parameters and attenuating inflammatory responses through the downregulation of FAS and caspase-3 expression in hepatic tissues. Previous studies have reported that, gallic acid exhibits neuroprotective effects by reactive oxygen species (ROS), its ability to improve synaptic plasticity, and its role in reducing amyloid-β plaque formation [<a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20">20</a>]. Moreover, gallic acid has been shown to exert antidepressant-like effects by modulating serotonergic neurotransmission, specifically through interactions with 5-HT2A/2C and 5-HT3 receptors [<a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21">21</a>,<a href="#bib22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib22">22</a>]. Similarly, quercetin is well-known for its robust antioxidant and neuroprotective properties. It exerts its effects through multiple signalling cascades, including the Nrf2 pathway, TNF-α inhibition, c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and phosphoinositide 3-kinase/Akt (PI3K/Akt) pathways [<a href="#bib23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib23">23</a>]. Study has reported that quercetin helps in ameliorating encephalitis in rats through suppression of neuronal cell apoptosis and downregulation of HMGB-1 and TLR-4 [<a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24">24</a>]. Its hepatoprotective activity has also been reported, involving the downregulation of key regulatory molecules such as NF-κB, p53, PPAR-α, and PI3K/Akt [<a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25">25</a>]. Ascorbic acid (vitamin C), another key constituent of the formulation, has been recognized for its neuroprotective role primarily through its radical scavenging and antioxidant properties [<a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26">26</a>]. Ellagic acid, as reported by Hatefi-Hesari et al., also exhibits hepatoprotective potential by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity and suppressing pro-inflammatory mediators including NF-κB and TNF-α [<a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27">27</a>]. These literature supports our results of network pharmacology and docking where we have obtained good binding affinity for TLR-4, NF-κB, TNF-α, and Nrf2, thus supporting the hypothesis that TL might act by above mentioned pathway in amelioration of HE.</p>
<p id="p0185">Standardization of TL was conducted following the General Guidelines for Drug Development of Ayurvedic Formulations (Volume 1) established by the CCRAS, Ministry of AYUSH, Government of India, New Delhi. The parameters assessed included organoleptic characteristics, ash values (both acid-insoluble and water-soluble), <em>nishchandrica</em> (lusterless), <em>rekha purnatva</em> (fineness), <em>varitara</em> (floatability in water), <em>nirthoom</em> (smokeless), <em>niswadu</em> (tasteless), and <em>apurnabhava</em> (irreversible), all of which fell within the specified limits outlined in the guidelines. Elemental analysis confirmed that all minerals present in the formulation were within acceptable ranges. The TL formulation contains three traditional Ayurvedic <em>bhasma's</em>: <em>rajat bhasma, loha bhasma</em>, and <em>suvarnamakshika bhasma</em>, each known for their antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties. In one study, various formulations of <em>rajat bhasma</em> administered at doses of 50 and 100 mg/kg significantly improved liver function parameters in CCl<sub>4</sub> induced hepatotoxicity, along with a marked enhancement in antioxidant levels [<a href="#bib28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib28">28</a>]. Silver, the primary constituent of <em>rajat bhasma</em>, has shown significant potential in its nanoparticle form by effectively reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induced hepatotoxicity [<a href="#bib29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib29">29</a>]. <em>Loha bhasma</em> is traditionally used in Ayurveda to pacify pitta and is regarded as beneficial for liver health. The iron supplementation aligned with Ayurvedic principles may aid in preventing hepatotoxicity. The presence of iron oxide, constituting 39.2 % by mass, in the TL formulation may contribute to its hepatoprotective effects. Meanwhile, <em>suvarnamakshika bhasma</em> has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in a rat model of cognitive deficits, where it enhanced spatial memory and exhibited antioxidant activity [<a href="#bib30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib30">30</a>]. These combined properties indicate that these <em>bhasma's</em> may function as antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agents in the management of HE.</p>
<p id="p0190">Based on docking scores, compounds such as gallic acid, quercetin, and ascorbic acid were selected as marker compounds for quantification using LC-MS/MS. These markers are recognized for their hepatoprotective, neuroprotective, and antioxidant properties, as previously discussed in the literature. Optimization was initially performed to determine the <em>m/z</em> values of the standard marker compounds, which facilitated the establishment of linearity. Subsequently, the concentrations of each marker compound in TL were calculated. The estimated amounts were 4098.21 ng/ml for gallic acid, 250.68 ng/ml for quercetin, and 55.17 ng/ml for ascorbic acid. The confirmed presence of these bioactive compounds supports the therapeutic potential of TL and corroborates its reported pharmacological activities.</p>
<p id="p0195">Further, TL was studied for preliminary hepatoprotective activity using HepG2 cells, TAA was employed as a hepatotoxic agent to induce liver injury. HepG2 cells are human liver cancer cell line commonly used for <em>in vitro</em> hepatotoxicity assessment.</p>
<p id="p0200">Thioacetamide undergoes metabolic conversion into its active metabolite, TAA-S-oxide, through the enzymatic activity of cytochrome P450. This metabolite contributes to hepatotoxicity primarily by elevating the levels of ROS, leading to oxidative stress and liver cell damage [<a href="#bib31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib31">31</a>]. Silymarin, a well-established hepatoprotective compound known for its antioxidant and cytoprotective properties, was used as the positive control to validate the experimental model. A study reported that gallic acid reduced the proliferation of HepG2 cells in a dose-dependent manner, accompanied by a significant decrease in IL-8 levels and an increase in IL-10 and IL-12 [<a href="#bib32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib32">32</a>]. Similarly, ascorbic acid inhibited cell proliferation and promoted apoptosis through upregulation of p53 and annexin V [<a href="#bib33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib33">33</a>]. Consistent with these findings, TL demonstrated a dose-dependent protective effect on HepG2 cell viability, comparable to that of silymarin, potentially via similar mechanisms. These findings suggest that TL possesses significant hepatoprotective activity against TAA-induced toxicity, likely by attenuating oxidative stress and downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, which may explain the observed dose-dependent improvement in cell viability.</p>
<p id="p0205">The radical scavenging activity of a compound is assessed based on its ability to donate a hydrogen atom or electron to neutralize free radicals, specifically DPPH radicals, in the DPPH assay. In this method, DPPH, a stable free radical with a deep purple colour undergoes a visible colour change to yellow upon reduction by an antioxidant, indicating the neutralization of the radical species. The degree of discoloration reflects the compound's antioxidant potential. In the present study, the antioxidant capacity of the test compound TL was evaluated in comparison to a standard antioxidant, ascorbic acid. The results demonstrated that the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC<sub>50</sub>), which represents the concentration required to scavenge 50 % of the DPPH radicals, was 98.43 μg/ml for ascorbic acid and 2.34 mg/ml for TL. This finding suggests that TL demonstrates significant antioxidant activity, which effectively reduces the elevated levels of ROS typically associated with oxidative stress. By mitigating ROS accumulation, TL may help in protecting cellular structures from oxidative damage, thereby it may help to alleviate HE symptoms and potentially slowing its progression.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">5. Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0210">In this study, we explored the potential molecular targets of the test formulation TL and its association with HE using a network pharmacology approach. This analysis enabled the identification of key genes and signalling pathways implicated in both the disease and the therapeutic activity of TL. Genes such as TLR4, NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, Nrf2, HIF-1, and HO-1 were identified as significant targets and were selected for molecular docking studies to evaluate the binding affinities of various phytoconstituents present in TL. Among the compounds tested, gallic acid, quercetin, and ascorbic acid exhibited the strongest binding affinities and were selected as marker compounds for subsequent quantitative analysis. <em>In vitro</em> assays using the HepG2 cell line demonstrated that TL enhanced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner following thioacetamide-induced hepatotoxicity, supporting its hepatoprotective potential. Additionally, TL exhibited antioxidant activity, as confirmed by results from the DPPH free radical scavenging assay. Based on these promising findings, future investigations will focus on evaluating the <em>in vivo</em> efficacy of TL in animal models of HE. and determining the major mechanisms implicated, as suggested by the current study.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Author contributions</h2>
