</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is marked by chronic neurodegeneration associated with the occurrence of plaques containing amyloid β (Aβ) proteins in various parts of the human brain. An increase in several Aβ fragments is well documented in patients with AD and anti-amyloid targeting is an emerging area of therapy. Soluble Aβ can bind to various cell surface and intracellular molecules with the pathogenic Aβ<sub>42</sub> fragment leading to neurotoxicity. Here we examined the effect of Aβ<sub>42</sub> on network adaptations in the proteome of nerve growth factor (NGF) differentiated PC12 cells using liquid-chromatography electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (LC-ESI MS/MS) proteomics. Whole-cell peptide mass fingerprinting was coupled to bioinformatic gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) in order to identify differentially represented proteins and related gene ontology (GO) pathways within Aβ<sub>42</sub> treated cells. Our results underscore a role for Aβ<sub>42</sub> in disrupting proteome responses for signaling, bioenergetics, and morphology in mitochondria. These findings highlight the specific components of the mitochondrial response during Aβ<sub>42</sub> neurotoxicity and suggest several new biomarkers for detection and surveillance of amyloid disease.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Alzheimer’s, amyloid, bioenergetics, mitochondria, prohibitin, proteomics</p></section></section><section id="sec1-cells-10-02380"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of human dementia and a major public health problem. Commonly prescribed drugs for treatment include acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors, which lessen cognitive symptoms without halting neurodegeneration [<a href="#B1-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-cells-10-02380">1</a>]. A recently approved monoclonal antibody, aducanumab (Aduhelm), which specifically targets Aβ oligomers, appears to slow disease progression [<a href="#B2-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2-cells-10-02380">2</a>]. In clinical studies, however, aducanumab was also associated with transient swelling of the brain and hemorrhaging, thus increasing the urgency for a better understanding of the cellular effects of Aβ.</p>
<p>Amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles (NFT) of aggregated hyperphosphorylated tau are the primary histopathological lesions of AD [<a href="#B3-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3-cells-10-02380">3</a>]. Amyloid plaques consist of highly structured Aβ peptides generated by β- and γ- secretase cleavage of APP in cells [<a href="#B4-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4-cells-10-02380">4</a>]. High levels of Aβ<sub>42</sub> were shown to contribute to neural damage in early and late stages of AD [<a href="#B5-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5-cells-10-02380">5</a>]. Soluble, low molecular weight, Aβ<sub>42</sub> oligomers contribute to cell damage and neurotoxicity [<a href="#B6-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6-cells-10-02380">6</a>], with the pathogenicity of amyloid proteins developing well before the emergence of measurable cognitive symptoms. In fact, memory deficits in mice correlate with the early stages of Aβ<sub>42</sub> oligomer formation during Aβ deposition [<a href="#B7-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-cells-10-02380">7</a>].</p>
<p>A mechanistic understanding of Aβ<sub>42</sub> mediated neurotoxicity was obtained in cultured neural cells exposed to various concentrations of the amyloid protein [<a href="#B8-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8-cells-10-02380">8</a>]. Several subcellular and molecular targets of Aβ<sub>42</sub> toxicity were identified [<a href="#B9-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-cells-10-02380">9</a>,<a href="#B10-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10-cells-10-02380">10</a>]. In hippocampal neurons, Aβ<sub>42</sub> peptides tend to aggregate within lipid rafts and drive Ca<sup>2+</sup>-mediated neurotoxicity (Abramov et al., 2004). Aβ<sub>42</sub> oligomers also induce mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in neurons through calcium mediated neurotoxicity [<a href="#B11-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11-cells-10-02380">11</a>]. In addition, various cell surface receptors can bind to Aβ<sub>42</sub>, including metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR5), nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR), <em>N</em>-methyl-<span class="font-variant-small-caps">d</span>-aspartic acid receptor (NMDAR), β-adrenergic receptor (β-AR), erythropoietin-producing hepatoma cell line receptor (EphR), and paired immunoglobulin-like receptor B (PirB) [<a href="#B12-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12-cells-10-02380">12</a>,<a href="#B13-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13-cells-10-02380">13</a>].</p>
<p>In this study, we examined whole cell proteomic adaptations to Aβ<sub>42</sub> (Aβ<sub>42</sub> proteome) in PC12 cells, which are an established model system for amyloid cytotoxicity [<a href="#B14-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14-cells-10-02380">14</a>]. Our findings underscore the role of mitochondrial responses to Aβ<sub>42</sub> and delineate specific pathways for ion transport, calcium signaling, and energy production that may contribute to the early stages of AD.</p></section><section id="sec2-cells-10-02380"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec2dot1-cells-10-02380"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Cell Culture and Treatment</h3>
<p>Pheochromocytoma (PC12) adherent cells (ATCC<sup>®</sup> CRL-1721.1™) were proliferated in RPMI-1640 (ATCC), which was supplemented with 10% horse serum (HS), 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1% pen/strep on collagen (50 μg/mL) at 37 °C and 5% CO<sub>2</sub>. Cells were differentiated using nerve growth factor (NGF, 200 ng/mL, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) in RPMI-1640, 2% HS, 1%FBS, and 0.2% pen/strep for 24 h before treating with 100 nM Aβ<sub>42</sub>. Soluble oligomeric Aβ<sub>42</sub> peptides were prepared as described in Arora et al. [<a href="#B15-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15-cells-10-02380">15</a>]. These preparations were shown to yield a stable oligomeric form of Aβ<sub>42</sub>. Daily media and treatment changes ensured consistent exposure to Aβ<sub>42</sub> [<a href="#B16-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16-cells-10-02380">16</a>]. After three days, cells were solubilized by the addition of a 0.1% Triton X-100 lysis buffer (Triton X-100, 1 M Tris HCl, 1.5 M NaCl, 0.25 M EDTA, and 10% glycerol, in the presence of protease inhibitors (Complete Mini, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) in 10 mL of lysis buffer) as described [<a href="#B17-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17-cells-10-02380">17</a>]. The protein concentration was determined by a Bradford assay.</p></section><section id="sec2dot2-cells-10-02380"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Liquid-Chromatography Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry</h3>
<p>Sample preparation for proteomics was conducted as described in [<a href="#B18-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18-cells-10-02380">18</a>]. Briefly, 100 μg of protein was precipitated by the addition of acetone 100 μL at 4 °C for 5 min and then centrifuged for 3 min at 16,000× <em>g</em>. The pellet was sequentially treated with 8 M urea, 1 M dithiothreitol, 0.5 M iodoacetamide, and 2 μL (0.5 μg/μL) trypsin in 0.5 M ammonium bicarbonate, then incubated at 37 °C for 5 h to denature, reduce, alkylate, and digest proteins. The sample was desalted using C-18 ZipTips (Millipore), dehydrated in a SpeedVac for 18 min, and reconstituted with 0.1% formic acid for a final volume of 20 μL for liquid-chromatography electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (LC-ESI MS/MS). LC-ESI MS/MS was performed using an Exploris Orbitrap 480 equipped with an EASY-nLC 1200HPLC system (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Peptides were separated using a reversed-phase PepMap RSLC 75 μm i.d by 15 cm long with a 2 μm particle size C18 LC column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), eluted with 0.1% formic acid and 80% acetonitrile at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. After one full scan (60,000 resolution) from 300 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> to 1200 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>, high abundance peptides were selected in a data-dependent fashion for fragmentation by high-energy collision dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy of 28%. Enabled filters included EASY-IC internal mass calibration, monoisotopic precursor selection, and dynamic exclusion (20 s). Peptide precursor ions with charge states from +2 to +4 were included. All samples were run in triplicate.</p></section><section id="sec2dot3-cells-10-02380"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Protein Identification and Quantification</h3>
<p>Proteome Discoverer <em>v</em>2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to identify proteins using the SEQUEST HT search engine to compare MS spectra to the <em>Rattus norvegicus</em> Rat_NCBI2016 database under the following parameters: mass tolerance for precursor ions = 5 ppm; mass tolerance for fragment ions = 0.05 Da; and cut-off value for the false discovery rate (FDR) in reporting peptide spectrum matches (PSM) to the database = 1%. The abundance ratios and p-values were calculated by precursor ion quantification in Proteome Discoverer <em>v</em>2.4. Non-treated NGF differentiated PC12 cells were used as a control.</p></section><section id="sec2dot4-cells-10-02380"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and Genomic Enrichment Analysis (GEA) </h3>
<p>Bioinformatic analysis was performed on filtered dataset proteins obtained from Proteome Discoverer <em>v</em>2.4 with a quantifiable spectra signal profile in at least two out of the three replicates. Proteins differentially impacted by the treatment condition were identified based on the calculated protein abundance ratio (fold-change) <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05 using Proteome Discoverer. Proteins were identified by Entrez ID gene identifiers in NCBI. </p>
<p>For GSEA or GEA, protein identifiers, abundance ratios, and individual <em>p</em>-values were uploaded into Pathway Studio (PS) (<a href="http://www.pathwaystudio.com" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.pathwaystudio.com</a>, accessed 2 March 2021 through 20 June 2021). GSEA was performed against the Gene Ontology (GO) pathways stored in the PS environment.</p></section><section id="sec2dot5-cells-10-02380"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Statistics</h3>
<p>One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Benjamini-Hochberg post-hoc, was used to calculate <em>p</em>-values within Proteome Discoverer <em>v</em>2.4. Statistical significance of GO pathways and GEA, analyzed in Pathway Studio, was obtained using a Mann-Whitney U test. <em>p</em>-values of &lt; 0.05 were considered significant. The data was organized and presented using the R package, ggplot2 [<a href="#B19-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19-cells-10-02380">19</a>], and Excel. </p></section></section><section id="sec3-cells-10-02380"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results</h2>
<section id="sec3dot1-cells-10-02380"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Whole-Cell Proteome Analysis of PC12 Cells Exposesd to Aβ<sub>42</sub>
</h3>
<p>To uncover molecular mechanisms of Aβ<sub>42</sub> mediated neurotoxicity, NGF differentiated PC12 cells were treated with 100 nM Aβ<sub>42</sub> prepared and presented in a manner consistent with the formation of pathogenic oligomeric amyloid peptides [<a href="#B20-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20-cells-10-02380">20</a>]. PC12 cells were exposed to three days of Aβ<sub>42</sub>, then analyzed using LC-ESI-MS/MS. In these experiments, NGF differentiated cells not exposed to Aβ<sub>42</sub> were used as the control condition. Using MS proteomic spectra identification and label-free precursor ion quantification for relative abundance [<a href="#B21-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21-cells-10-02380">21</a>] in Proteome Discoverer <em>v</em>2.4, we identified proteins significantly impacted by the presence of Aβ<sub>42</sub>. Pathway Studio (PS) and Gene Ontology (GO) informatic analysis was used to deduce significant molecular pathways and cellular components of the Aβ<sub>42</sub> response (<a href="#cells-10-02380-f001" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>).</p>

The Aβ42 Proteome Revolves around Mitochondria Function</h3>
<p>Using GO analysis, 199 proteins (63%) within the Aβ<sub>42</sub> proteome were classified as membrane-associated molecules (<a href="#cells-10-02380-f003" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>A, <a href="#app1-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link">Supplementary Tables S3 and S4</a>). Further subcellular analysis localized these membrane proteins to mitochondria (28%), nucleus (10%), endoplasmic reticulum (10%), Golgi apparatus (1%), endosomes (3%), cell surface (2%), and/or more than one compartment (multi-compartment) (23%) (<a href="#cells-10-02380-f003" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>B). Interestingly, the majority of multi-compartment proteins (76%) within the Aβ<sub>42</sub> proteome, showed mitochondrial and nuclear placement (<a href="#cells-10-02380-f003" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>C). For example, prohibitin 2 (PHB2), which was significantly downregulated by Aβ<sub>42</sub>, is known to shuttle between the mitochondria and nucleus and regulate transcription [<a href="#B23-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23-cells-10-02380">23</a>].</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/cells-10-02380-t001/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section><p>Earlier studies demonstrated an ability of Aβ [<a href="#B26-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B26-cells-10-02380">26</a>] and α-synuclein [<a href="#B27-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27-cells-10-02380">27</a>], to impact mitochondrial trafficking and function. Consistently, we detected significant changes in the expression of many mitochondrial proteins (95 down-regulated; and 13 up-regulated) within the Aβ<sub>42</sub> proteome dataset (<a href="#cells-10-02380-f005" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>A, <a href="#app1-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link">Supplementary Tables S6 and S7</a>). GO analysis showed that these proteins were important for mitochondrial membrane organization, inner structural scaffolding, and mitoribosome function (<a href="#cells-10-02380-f005" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>B). In particular, a significantly downregulated set of solute carriers (SLC) proteins, including SLC25A4 and SLC25A5, are known to mediate nucleotide (ADP/ATP) transport across the mitochondrial membrane [<a href="#B28-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28-cells-10-02380">28</a>]. These proteins are also known to regulate nuclear function (<a href="#cells-10-02380-f005" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>C, <a href="#app1-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link">Supplementary Tables S8 and S9</a>) [<a href="#B29-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29-cells-10-02380">29</a>].</p>

An Analysis of Aβ<sub>42</sub> Proteome Associated Genes and Possible Relevance to Human Disease </h3>
<p>Genomic enrichment analysis (GEA) of the human genome was performed on the Aβ<sub>42</sub> proteome dataset using PS. Genes that encode components of the Aβ<sub>42</sub> proteome were found randomly distributed throughout the human genome. Interestingly, ~70% of the Aβ<sub>42</sub> proteome appeared to localize to gene regions of disease, including rare copy number variation (e.g., microdeletions and/or duplications). A complete list of the GEA results is presented in <a href="#app1-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link">Supplementary Table S10</a> and lists all genes, chromosomal location, and human disease phenotype associated with the Aβ<sub>42</sub> proteome dataset. <a href="#cells-10-02380-f006" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a>, summarizes GEA of genes associated with mitochondrial proteins within the Aβ<sub>42</sub> proteome. As shown, our results suggest a link between amyloid-associated cellular responses and various genetic factors that contribute to human disease. For example, chromosome 1p36 deletion syndrome appears impacted by amyloid-sensitive proteins encoded by the <em>SDHB</em> and <em>UQCRHL</em> gene, which both reside within that gene region and can critically contribute to cell energetics. In fact, 1p36 deletion syndrome was shown to associate with congenital cardiac abnormalities and/or developmental and intellectual deficits [<a href="#B30-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30-cells-10-02380">30</a>]. Another genomic region of interest resides on chromosome 17q21, housing both the <em>BRCA1</em> and <em>PHB</em> genes, whose products are estrogen receptor (ER) sensitive [<a href="#B31-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31-cells-10-02380">31</a>,<a href="#B32-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32-cells-10-02380">32</a>]. In this case, <em>PHB</em> encodes the mitochondrial protein prohibitin that is significantly decreased within our dataset, suggesting a link between estrogen and amyloid responses in cells.</p>

Proteomic Mechanisms of Aβ<sub>42</sub> Cellular Calcium Signaling</h3>
<p>Amyloid proteins were shown to increase intracellular calcium levels, leading to calcium-mediated neurotoxicity [<a href="#B22-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22-cells-10-02380">22</a>,<a href="#B33-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B33-cells-10-02380">33</a>]. Aβ<sub>42</sub> mediates cellular calcium entry through binding to cell surface receptors, such as the α7 nACh [<a href="#B12-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12-cells-10-02380">12</a>,<a href="#B13-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13-cells-10-02380">13</a>]. Therefore, the subcellular distribution of calcium binding molecules (i.e., proteins with a known calcium binding domain) and more broadly, calcium associated molecules (i.e., those with known regulation by calcium) was analyzed within the Aβ<sub>42</sub> proteome. As shown in <a href="#cells-10-02380-f007" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>, Aβ<sub>42</sub> treatment was associated with a noticeable impact on cellular calcium signaling through the regulation of both calcium binding and associated proteins. GO domain mapping indicated that impacted proteins localize to various subcellular compartments and can traffic between important organelles. For example, FKBP1A, which was significantly up-regulated (fold-change = 4.632, <em>p</em> = 2.37 × 10<sup>−16</sup>) in the presence of Aβ<sub>42</sub>, is a calcium binding <em>cis</em>-trans isomerase that modulates ryanodine receptor (RyR) activity and protein folding in the ER. Here, FKBP1A appears to participate in RyR mediated calcium flux from the ER to the mitochondria at the mitochondrial associated membrane (MAM) [<a href="#B34-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34-cells-10-02380">34</a>,<a href="#B35-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B35-cells-10-02380">35</a>] (<a href="#cells-10-02380-f007" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a> and <a href="#app1-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link">Supplementary Tables S11 and S12</a>). Overall, our proteomic findings support existing evidence on the direct ability of amyloid peptides to disrupt intracellular calcium activity in neural cells [<a href="#B36-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B36-cells-10-02380">36</a>], and suggest a multi-compartment model for the impact of amyloids on calcium homeostasis.</p>

Discussion</h2>
<p>Neurodegeneration in the course of AD is suspected to stem from alterations in amyloid precursor protein (APP) processing, resulting in the elevation of a pathogenic Aβ<sub>42</sub> peptide in the brain and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [<a href="#B37-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B37-cells-10-02380">37</a>]. We investigated the direct impact of this pathogenic amyloid peptide on cells using whole cell proteome analysis and GO analysis. We found that Aβ<sub>42</sub> exposure preferentially impacted proteins associated with the mitochondria including its known interactions with other cellular organelles. We also identified other pathways of known importance for amyloid protein processing including two subunits of the biogenesis of lysosome-related organelle complex 1 (BLOC1S2 and BLOC1S6) that are involved in endosome recycling, another mechanism implicated in AD [<a href="#B38-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38-cells-10-02380">38</a>,<a href="#B39-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B39-cells-10-02380">39</a>,<a href="#B40-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B40-cells-10-02380">40</a>]. These proteins are consistent with the concurrent activity of lysosome and autophagy pathways that known to be activated during by amyloid protein degradation in neural cells [<a href="#B38-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38-cells-10-02380">38</a>]. </p>
<p>The Aβ<sub>42</sub> proteome reveals a mechanistic framework for understanding how amyloid peptides disrupt cell bioenergetics, calcium homeostasis, and the shape and localization of mitochondrion [<a href="#B41-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B41-cells-10-02380">41</a>]. As depicted in <a href="#cells-10-02380-f008" class="usa-link">Figure 8</a>, our data proposes an important impact on mitochondrial cristae scaffolding proteins and disruption to energy management through proteins such as prohibitin. Specifically, the energy producing cellular machinery, including components of metabolite transport, tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), the electron transport chain (ETC) (complexes I–IV), and the ATP synthase, all appear to be impacted by Aβ<sub>42</sub>. The ETC, responsible for creating and maintaining the vital H<sup>+</sup> gradient across the mitochondrial membrane, upon exposure to Aβ<sub>42</sub> is likely perturbed at the H<sup>+</sup> generating complex (I, III and IV) as based on proteomic evidence of changes to components of complex II during Aβ<sub>42</sub> exposure. Here, a down-regulation in succinate dehydrogenase (SDBH) is also predicted to disrupt the TCA cycle and its link to the ETC [<a href="#B42-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42-cells-10-02380">42</a>]. This proposed failure of the ETC should yield a drop in mitochondrial ATP synthesis and drive ROS production, leading to disruption in membrane potential (Δψ<sub>m</sub>) [<a href="#B43-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B43-cells-10-02380">43</a>]. This model is consistent with the observed features of amyloid toxicity [<a href="#B41-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B41-cells-10-02380">41</a>].</p>

For example, Aβ<sub>42</sub> was found to decrease the expression of the pyruvate carrier MPC2, a component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex that transports pyruvate into the mitochondrial matrix [<a href="#B44-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B44-cells-10-02380">44</a>]. Observed changes in several key SLC25 family transport proteins, including SLC25A4 and SLC25A5, necessary for the exchange of ATP/ADP [<a href="#B45-cells-10-02380" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B45-cells-10-02380">45</a>], and SLC25A3, involved in phosphate transport during ATP production, suggest several mitochondrial biomarker proteins in early AD detection.</p></section><section id="app1-cells-10-02380" class="app"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Materials</h2>
