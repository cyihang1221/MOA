</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>This study investigates the impact of casein hydrolysates on the poultry ceca inoculated with <em>Campylobacter</em> focusing on microbial molecular preferences for different protein sources in the presence of <em>Campylobacter jejuni</em>. Three casein sources (intact casein (IN), casein enzyme hydrolysate (EH), and casein acid hydrolysate (AH)) were introduced to cecal contents in combination with inoculated <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> in an <em>in vitro</em> model system incubated for 48 h at 42°C under microaerophilic conditions. Samples were collected at 0, 24, and 48 h. Genomic DNA was extracted and amplified using custom dual-indexed primers, followed by sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq platform. The obtained sequencing data were then analyzed via QIIME2-2021.11. Metabolite extracts were analyzed with ultra-high-performance liquid orbitrap chromatography-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS). Statistical analysis of metabolites was conducted using MetaboAnalyst 5.0, while functional analysis was performed using Mummichog 2.0 with a significance threshold set at P &lt; 0.00001. DNA sequencing and metabolomic analyses revealed that <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> was most abundant in the EH group. Microbial diversity and richness improved in casein supplemented groups, with core microbial differences observed, compared to non-supplemented groups. Vitamin B-associated metabolites significantly increased in the supplemented groups, displaying distinct patterns in vitamin B6 and B9 metabolism between EH and AH groups (P &lt; 0.05). <em>Faecalibacterium</em> and <em>Phascolarctobacterium</em> were associated with AH and EH groups, respectively. These findings suggest microbial interactions in the presence of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> and casein supplementation are influenced by microbial community preferences for casein hydrolysates impacting B vitamin production and shaping competitive dynamics within the cecal microbial community. These findings underscore the potential of nutritional interventions to modulate the poultry GIT microbiota for improved health outcomes.</p></section><section id="sec001"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>The intricate relationship between diet and gut microbiota has garnered significant attention in recent years due to profound impacts on overall health. Over the past two decades, there has been substantial growth in the utilization of enzymes in poultry feed, as noted by Bedford and Cowieson [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref001">1</a>]. Exogenous enzymes used in poultry feed can influence the composition, size, and abundance of microbial communities within the gastrointestinal tract (GIT). They can also alter the site of nutrient digestion within the GIT, impacting nutrient availability to the microbiome and influencing the balance between beneficial bacteria and pathogens [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref001">1</a>].</p>
<p>Exogenous enzymes can alter microbial communities in the GIT by shifting digestion to the anterior compartments and limiting nutrient availability to the posterior GIT microbiome. They enhance intestinal mucin integrity, extend feed retention, reduce inflammation, and boost immune response [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref001">1</a>]. Exogenous enzymes play a crucial role in minimizing variability among feed ingredients, thereby boosting feed digestibility, and maximizing nutrient absorption [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref002">2</a>]. This, in turn, reduces digesta viscosity. Changes in the viscosity of diets can influence bacterial growth, potentially reducing the competition between the host organism and bacteria for nutrients [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref002">2</a>]. Thus, the strategic use of enzyme combinations to target specific substrates in feed, which synergize with endogenous enzymes, has become highly valuable in the poultry industry. Aloo and Oh and Martinez-Lopez et al. highlight the role of hydrolysates and peptides in balancing GIT bacteria [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref003">3</a>,<a href="#pone.0303856.ref004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref004">4</a>]. Aloo and Oh’s review explores how peptides and hydrolyzed proteins shape GIT microbiota, impacting digestion and growth rate [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref003">3</a>]. Host health and pathogen presence affect dietary ingredient quality [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref001">1</a>]. Inclusion of protease, phytase, and carbohydrates alters nutrient digestion sites, reducing substrates for putrefactive organisms and increasing availability for beneficial fermentative organisms in lower GIT compartments, potentially reducing pathogen proliferation, such as <em>Campylobacter</em> that rely on peptides and amino acids for growth [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref005">5</a>–<a href="#pone.0303856.ref007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref007">7</a>]. Genes involved in the breakdown of peptides and amino acids are notably more abundant in vigorously colonizing strains compared to weakly colonizing ones [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref008">8</a>]. This suggests that the pathogenesis of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> is influenced by its phenotype, which in turn may be shaped by the dietary environment. Additionally, as the demand for poultry meat rises alongside nitrogen supplementation in diets, it becomes imperative to comprehend the co-occurrence patterns of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> with local microbiota and the metabolic effects related to nitrogen sources. <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> may have developed adaptations to bolster its survival through interactions with local microbiota [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref009">9</a>]. Understanding the intricate interplays between dietary nitrogen overload, GIT microbiota, and <em>Campylobacter</em> colonization is crucial for tackling challenges in poultry production and ensuring food safety [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref010">10</a>–<a href="#pone.0303856.ref012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref012">12</a>].</p>
<p>The digestibility of proteins can be influenced by various factors including the protein source and its concentration, dietary composition, processing methods, protein glycation, and oxidation [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref013">13</a>]. Although protein digestion in the host varies, most proteins are cleaved into peptides and amino acids [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref014">14</a>]. Amino acid composition, including sequences, types, charge, and dimensional arrangements, influences protein digestion. Post-translational alterations and protein interactions with other food particles can impact GIT microbiota, generating diverse peptides that influence microbial communities [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref014">14</a>]. Yu et al. found that feeding rats duck egg white led to a higher abundance of <em>Proteobacteria</em> and <em>Peptostreptococcaceae</em> and a lower abundance of <em>Lachnospiraceae</em> in their ceca compared to rats fed hen egg white, preserved egg white, and casein. Peptide profile differences during digestion were attributed to these observed variations [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref015">15</a>].</p>
<p>Understanding the intricate interplay among diet, exogenous feed enzymes, and GIT microbiota in poultry production creates opportunities to optimize feed formulations, improve nutrient availability, and potentially reduce harmful pathogens, ultimately boosting the efficiency and sustainability of poultry and livestock farming. Further research on the microbiome’s response to dietary interventions, including protein hydrolysates, can offer valuable insights for maximizing animal health and productivity. This understanding can also lead to more predictable outcomes and strategies to mitigate pathogens such as <em>Campylobacter</em>.</p>
<p>In our study, we used an <em>in vitro</em> poultry cecal model inoculated with <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> to investigate how different protein structures—intact casein, hydrolyzed casein, and acid hydrolyzed free amino acids derived from casein -affect <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> abundance, microbial composition, and functional changes within the cecal community. Our experimental focus was to evaluate the impact of simultaneous <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> and casein addition, assessing 1) <em>Campylobacter</em> presence on the local microbial community and 2) molecular preferences of casein hydrolysates in a microbial community when inoculated with <em>Campylobacter</em>. Our primary hypothesis was that <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> growth would be most abundant in the acid hydrolyzed casein-supplemented group. Considering the high abundance of proteolytic bacteria in poultry GIT, we hypothesized that all three supplemented groups would enhance microbial composition and diversity compared to the non-supplemented groups. Additionally, we hypothesized that casein hydrolysates with lower molecular weights would contribute to greater microbial diversity, influencing metabolome responses related to peptide and amino acid metabolism.</p></section><section id="sec002"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="sec003"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Poultry ceca acquisition</h3>
<p>On day of hatch, Ross 308 broilers (Aviagen, Huntsville, AL, USA) were brought from a commercial hatchery (Welps, Bancroft, IA, USA) to the Poultry Research Laboratory at the University of Wisconsin-Madison (Madison, WI, USA). Chickens were raised based on standard industry guidelines and ethical practices. The animal work for the current study was conducted with a University of Wisconsin-Madison Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved protocol (protocol A006627) to ensure the humane treatment of the birds. Chickens were allowed free access to water and commercial least-cost formulated, double pelleted, corn-soybean meal manufactured diets (<a href="#pone.0303856.s001" class="usa-link">S1 Table</a>). Cecal samples were obtained from six different 56-day-old chickens, which were humanely euthanized using CO<sub>2</sub>. The collection of these samples was carried out individually using tools that had been immersed in alcohol and sterilized by flame. Following collection, the cecal samples were carefully placed in sterile sample bags (VWR, Radnor, PA, USA). They were subsequently transferred to a portable anaerobic box (Mitsubishi Gas Chemical Co, Japan) which contained oxygen-scrubbing sachets to maintain an anaerobic environment. Immediately after harvest, the cecal samples were transferred to an anaerobic chamber with an atmosphere of 90% nitrogen, 5% H2, and 5% CO2 (Coy Laboratory Products, Grass Lake, MI, USA) for further processing.</p></section><section id="sec004"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Media preparation</h3>
<p>The selection of the media for evaluating microbial dynamics was based on the methodology established in the prior study conducted by Olson et al. [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref016">16</a>]. To prepare the Anaerobic Dilution Solution (ADS), the following components were used: 0.45 g/L K<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub>, 0.45 g/l KH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, 0.45 g/L (NH<sub>4</sub>)SO<sub>4</sub>, 0.9 g/L NaCl, 0.1875 g/L MgSO<sub>4</sub>-7H<sub>2</sub>O, 0.12 g/L CaCl<sub>2</sub>-2H<sub>2</sub>O, 1 ml/L 0.1% resazurin, 0.05% cysteine-HCl, and 0.4% sodium carbonate [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref017" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref017">17</a>,<a href="#pone.0303856.ref018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref018">18</a>]. Autoclaved ADS was then cooled to room temperature and allowed to equilibrate for 24 hours within an anaerobic chamber (Coy Laboratories, Grass Lake, MI, USA). The chamber was maintained at the same atmosphere as described above, effectively removing all traces of oxygen from the solution.</p></section><section id="sec005"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Bacterial cultures</h3>
<p>A <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> strain 700819, was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC<sup>®</sup>) and cryogenically preserved in 50% glycerol. To ensure purity, <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> strain was passaged on Charcoal-Cefoperazone-Deoxycholate Agar (mCCDA; Himedia, Mumbai, India) supplemented with 1 mL/L sodium cefoperazone and 200 μL/L amphotericin B through three consecutive cycles. From this purified culture, a single colony was selected and inoculated into 40 mL of Bolton Enrichment Broth (BEB) without antibiotic supplement and then incubated for 48 h at 42°C using an Advanced Anoxomat<sup>™</sup> III system (Advanced Instruments, Norwood, MA, USA) with a microaerophilic atmosphere (5% O<sub>2</sub>, 10% CO<sub>2</sub>, 85% N<sub>2</sub>). The cultures were subsequently pelleted and resuspended in 400 mL of fresh BEB, followed by incubation for 24 h under the previously described conditions. After the 24 h, the 200 mL of the fresh <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> cultures were aliquoted to 50 mL conical tubes, pelleted, decanted, and resuspended in 200 mL ADS. A 4 mL <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> starter culture of 10<sup>6</sup> CFU/mL was allocated into designated serum bottles after the pre-incubation step described below for a final concentration of 10<sup>5</sup> colony forming units (CFU)/mL.</p></section><section id="sec006"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Microaerophilic <em>in vitro</em> system for <em>Campylobacter</em>
</h3>
<p>The effect of the treatment was evaluated following a well-established protocol for the <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> cecal model, as outlined by Olson et al. and Feye et al. [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref016">16</a>,<a href="#pone.0303856.ref019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref019">19</a>]. Cecal contents were collected from six individual birds within aseptic chambers, then weighed and diluted at a ratio of 1:3000 by mixing 0.1 g of cecal content with 900 μL of ADS solution. This resuspended cecal material was further diluted by adding 1 mL of it to 299 mL of ADS for each respective cecum sample. A 36 mL aliquot of this diluted cecal content was subsequently transferred into each serum bottle, with or without the addition of 0.4 g of ground chicken feed (<a href="#pone.0303856.s001" class="usa-link">S1 Table</a>). To create an appropriate environment, the cultures were covered with aluminum foil and placed in Advanced Anoxomat<sup>™</sup> III containers (Advanced Instruments, Norwood, MA, USA), where a microaerobic atmosphere was established, containing 5% O2, 10% CO2, and 85% N2. Incubation was carried out at 42°C with continuous agitation at 150 revolutions per minute (RPM) for 24 hours. This step served as a pre-adaptation to acclimate the naturally occurring microbiota in poultry ceca to the new environment, following the method described previously [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref018">18</a>–<a href="#pone.0303856.ref020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref020">20</a>]. On the following day, the serum bottles were returned to the anaerobic chamber.</p>
<p>After 24 h of pre-adaption, the serum bottles were moved back into the anaerobic chamber where 4 ml of a freshly prepared 10<sup>6</sup> cells/mL <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> culture resuspended in ADS was added to all bottles except the non-inoculated treatment (NI) for a final concentration of 10<sup>5</sup> CFU/mL. For specific treatments involving casein supplements, 0.1 g of the appropriate vitamin free-casein type (intact (Envigo®, Madison, WI, USA), enzyme hydrolyzed (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA), or acid hydrolysate of casein (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) was added to the serum bottles. The study included five distinct treatments: non-inoculated (NI), inoculated with <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> (IN), and three supplemented groups, intact casein (IC), enzyme hydrolysate (EH), acid hydrolysate (AH), that were all inoculated with <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em>. We opted not to include additional controls for supplemented groups without <em>Campylobacter</em>, instead using non-inoculated and inoculated treatments without casein supplementations for comparative analyses with the casein-enriched groups. Because <em>Campylobacter</em> naturally inhabits poultry cecal compartments [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref021" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref021">21</a>], we confirmed the absence of culturable <em>Campylobacter</em> in the NI group and confirm the inocula, the 0-hour samples were plated on mCCDA [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref022">22</a>]. At 0, 24, and 48 h post-inoculation, duplicate 1 mL samples were taken for microbiome sequencing and metabolomic analysis. The samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C until processing. An overview of the procedure is described in <a href="#pone.0303856.g001" class="usa-link">Fig 1</a>.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/pone.0303856.g001/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></figure></section><section id="sec007"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Microbiota sequencing</h3>
<p>DNA was extracted from the aseptically collected aliquots of the microaerobic cultures taken at designated time points (0, 24, 48 h) using Qiagen QIAmp Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) as previously described by Olson et al. [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref016">16</a>]. The DNA purity was assessed via a spectrophotometer (Tecan NanoQuant Plate<sup>™</sup>, Mannedorf, Switzerland) and diluted to 10 ng/μL. The paired-end sequencing libraries were arranged by targeting the hypervariable region 4 (V4) of the 16S rRNA gene with PCR primers containing the linker and adapter sequence as described by Olson et al. and Kozich et al. to amplify each DNA sample [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref016">16</a>,<a href="#pone.0303856.ref023" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref023">23</a>]. Sample amplification was verified using gel electrophoresis. PCR products were normalized using a SequalPrep<sup>™</sup> Normalization kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Five μL of each sample was combined to generate a pooled plate library, and concentration was determined using a KAPPA Library Quantification Kit (Kappa Biosystems, Inc., Wilmington, MA). The library and PhiX control v3 were denatured and diluted to 20 pM in HT1 Buffer and 0.2 N fresh NaOH. The library and PhiX control v3 were further diluted in HT1 buffer to generate a final concentration of 6 pM/L. The resulting library was combined with the diluted PhiX control v3 (20%, v/v), and 600 μL was loaded onto a MiSeq v2 (500 cycles) reagent cartridge (Illumina, San Diego, CA, United States).</p></section><section id="sec008"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Microbiota bioinformatic analysis</h3>
<p>The demultiplexed data were downloaded locally and then transferred to QIIME2 (2021.11) via the Casava1.8 paired-end pipeline [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref024" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref024">24</a>]. Within QIIME2, the data were denoised and filtered using DADA2, applying the chimera consensus pipeline [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref025">25</a>]. To evaluate the diversity of the microbial populations, both alpha and beta diversity analyses were conducted. The QIIME2 phylogeny align-to-tree-mafft-fasttree methodology (q2-phylogeny) was employed for this purpose [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref026">26</a>]. For the taxonomic classification of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), the classify-sklearn tool within QIIME2-2021.11 was utilized in conjunction with the SILVA database (silva-138-99-nb-classifier.qza), employing a 97% confidence threshold [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref027">27</a>,<a href="#pone.0303856.ref028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref028">28</a>]. Taxonomic data at the genus level were exported from QIIME2 and visualized in JMP Pro 16 (SAS, Cary, North Carolina, USA) with taxa representing less than 1% of the relative abundance being displayed as “other.”</p>
<p>All available metrics related to alpha and beta diversity were computed through QIIME2 diversity core-metrics-phylogeny, with a chosen sampling depth of 6150, retaining 37% (492,000) of features in 80 (95%) samples. The adequacy of this sampling depth was validated by examining alpha rarefaction plots. Additionally, the statistical differences between treatments on the alpha and beta diversities were assessed via q2-diversity. The alpha diversity was analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test the interaction of variables via q2-longitudinal [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref029" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref029">29</a>]. Alpha diversity was assessed for both richness (using the Shannon Diversity Index) and evenness via Pielou’s Evenness [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref030">30</a>]. Kruskal-Wallis was used to determine pairwise differences [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref031">31</a>]. Differential abundance analysis was performed using ANCOM via q2-composition [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref032">32</a>]. Interaction analysis for beta diversity metrics was carried out using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) via ADONIS [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref033">33</a>]. Beta diversity metrics were evaluated using quantitative indicators, specifically the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index and the Weighted Unifrac distance matrix [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref034" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref034">34</a>], employing the Analysis of Similarity (ANOSIM) function which considers the mean population variation and dispersion [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref035">35</a>]. Microbiota main effects were considered statistically significant if the main effect exhibited a P &lt; 0.05, and the pairwise effect exhibited a Q &lt; 0.05, with each statistical measurement conducted within the QIIME2-2021.11 pipeline. The Q-value reflected the P-value adjusted for a stringent false discovery rate. Both alpha and beta diversity results were compiled and exported from QIIME2 using the metadata tabulate function. All results were uploaded to JMP Pro 16 where volatility plots, boxplots, and principal coordinate plots were generated.</p>
<p>Core microbiota analysis was conducted using core members and the phyloseq package in RStudio Version 1.3 for five different treatment groups. The core microbiota was defined as ASVs representing at least 5% of the population and detected in 50% of the samples. The Venn diagrams were generated via jvenn [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref036" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref036">36</a>]. The sequences were submitted to Short Read Archive (PRJNA1018262) under the project title "DNA-Response of Poultry Cecal Microbiota Inoculated with <em>Campylobacter jejuni</em>.<em>"</em> Detailed sample identification and descriptions are available in the metadata file uploaded to the Short Read Archive managed by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI).</p></section><section id="sec009"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolite extraction</h3>
<p>As larger proteins do not reach lower GIT compartments [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref037" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref037">37</a>] and metabolomic changes precede microbial compositional differences [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref038" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref038">38</a>], our focus was on EH and AH supplemented groups, along with NI and IN treatments at 0 and 24 h, to describe metabolomic profiles (<a href="#pone.0303856.g001" class="usa-link">Fig 1</a>). Cell extraction was performed using 0.5 mL of digesta diluent from samples for 0 and 24 h (total samples (N) = 44). Cell lysis was performed using three freeze-thawing cycles which consisted of thawing at room temperature for 10 min followed by a 30 s sonication on ice. A 1 mL aliquot of an acetonitrile:methanol:water (2:2:1 v/v) solvent mixture was added to cell extracts and sonicated for 30 s and stored at -20°C overnight to allow cellular debris and protein to precipitate. The next day samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 13,000 revolutions per minute (rpm) at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to new Eppendorf tubes (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) and dried for 4 h on a SpeedVac Concentrator (Thermo Scientific Savant DNA 120). Dried samples were then reconstituted in an acetonitrile:water (1:1 v/v) solvent mixture based on the normalized protein concentration of all samples. Normalized volumes were determined using the Bradford method, where the highest protein concentration is equal to 100 μL. Samples were then vortexed for 30 s, sonicated on ice for 10 min, and centrifuged for 15 min at 13,000 RPM at 4°C to remove any residual debris. The metabolite extracts were transferred into autosampler vials with inserts and stored at -80°C until analysis.</p></section><section id="sec010"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Untargeted metabolomics</h3>
<p>Metabolite extracts were analyzed with ultra-high-performance liquid orbitrap chromatography mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS) (Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer). A Kinetex Core-Shell 100 Å column C-18 column (1 x 150 mm, 1.7 μm, Phenomenex) was used for separation of metabolites in positive mode. Sample injection volume was 3 μL/min at a flow rate of 0.250 mL/min. Mobile phase A was composed of water with 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was 0.1% in acetonitrile. The gradient began with 5% B from 0–3 min, followed by an increase and held to 95% B until 18 min, followed by a decrease to 5% B at 18.50 min and hold at 5% B until 22 min. Data-dependent acquisition was used for the tandem MS workflow. The MS1 spectra were matched to spectra and compounds in the Human Metabolome Database using MetaboAnalyst 5.0.</p>
<p>MetaboAnalyst 5.0 was used for statistical analysis (P &lt; 0.05). The raw files were converted to mzXML files and centroided (MS-1, orbitrap, positive mode) using ProteoWizard version 3.0 [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref039" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref039">39</a>]. Pairwise comparisons were performed to investigate potential differences of metabolite features between treatment groups using t-test and volcano plots for visualization of the results. Functional analysis was conducted using Mummichog 2.0 (with a significance threshold of P-value &lt; 0.00001) utilizing the KEGG <em>Escherichia coli</em> library within MetaboAnalyst. To identify dysregulated microbial metabolites and metabolic pathways, metabolite features were annotated using Compound Discoverer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), and subsequently mapped to the metabolic pathways of representative cecal bacterial members <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni jejuni 51037</em>, <em>F</em>. <em>prausnitzii</em> A2-165, and <em>P</em>. <em>faecium</em> JCM 30894 in BioCyc [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref037" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref037">37</a>].</p></section></section><section id="sec011"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="sec012"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Casein supplements exerted a significant influence on microbial composition</h3>
<p>In this study, we investigated the influence of different molecular sizes of casein proteins—namely intact casein, enzyme hydrolysate of casein, and acid hydrolysate of casein—on the cecal microbial composition inoculated with <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> over a 48-hour period under microaerophilic conditions (total (N) = 84, treatments (k) = 5). To discern potential combined effects of time and treatment on microbial compositions, we employed ANOVA for assessing variable interactions related to evenness and richness parameters within the samples. This revealed there was no significant interaction between treatment and time on microbial richness (ANOVA, Shannon’s Entropy; P &gt; 0.05, <a href="#pone.0303856.s002" class="usa-link">S2 Table</a>). However, a trend towards a significant interaction between time and treatment was observed in regard to microbial evenness (ANOVA, Pielou’s Evenness; P = 0.09, <a href="#pone.0303856.s002" class="usa-link">S2 Table</a>). When considered individually, treatment drove a significant effect on microbial evenness and richness (P &lt; 0.05, <a href="#pone.0303856.s002" class="usa-link">S2 Table</a>). Additionally, when compared to the NI and IN treatments, which exhibited a decline in microbial homogeneity and richness over time, the supplemented groups demonstrated a sustained homogeneity and richness of microbial populations (<a href="#pone.0303856.g002" class="usa-link">Fig 2A and 2B</a>). Additionally, ADONIS was utilized to examine phylogenetic diversity and abundance disparities between the samples. Time and treatment exhibited a significant interaction concerning non-phylogenetic-based microbial abundance, as evidenced by the Bray-Curtis analysis (P &lt; 0.05, <a href="#pone.0303856.s003" class="usa-link">S3 Table</a>), while no such significance was observed for phylogenetic-based diversity analysis via Weighted Unifrac (P &gt; 0.05, <a href="#pone.0303856.s003" class="usa-link">S3 Table</a>). When evaluated individually, treatment demonstrated a significant effect on the phylogenetic composition of microbial communities (P &lt; 0.05, <a href="#pone.0303856.s003" class="usa-link">S3 Table</a>). Diverse responses were observed in microbial abundance among the treatment groups. The NI group exhibited stability over time, while both the IN and IC groups initially experienced a decrease in microbial abundance at 24 h, followed by a recovery to their original levels by 48 hours. Conversely, the EH and AH groups demonstrated stability at 24 h but exhibited a decline by the 48-h mark (<a href="#pone.0303856.g002" class="usa-link">Fig 2C</a>). Additionally, while the phylogenetic composition of most groups increased with time, the IN treatment exhibited a decreasing trend in phylogenetic composition over time (<a href="#pone.0303856.g002" class="usa-link">Fig 2D</a>). These findings reveal that <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em>-inoculated microbial communities, subjected to supplementation with casein hydrolysates, exhibit significant alterations in microbial evenness and richness, impacting abundance dynamics and phylogenetic composition of microbial communities over time.</p>

<section id="sec017"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Microbial dynamics in response to casein hydrolysates: Interactions, diversity, and metabolomic signatures within poultry GIT</h3>
<p>Gaining insights into how hydrolyzed casein proteins (peptides and amino acids) can influence the microbiota population profiles within poultry’s GIT may impact the growth of pathogens such as <em>Campylobacter</em>. Additionally, these insights can provide further insight for identifying dietary strategies for modulating <em>Campylobacter</em> proliferation. The respective availability of proteins, peptides, and amino acids originating from the animals’ diet can play a pivotal role in shaping the microbial composition within the GIT, consequently affecting nutrient digestion and the resulting microbial metabolomic profiles [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref038" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref038">38</a>]. In the present study, <em>Bacteroides</em> and <em>Enterococcus</em> constituted a fundamental microbiota component across all treatment groups. This observation aligns with earlier research that has indicated the elevated proteolytic activity of these taxa [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref040">40</a>,<a href="#pone.0303856.ref041" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref041">41</a>]. Consequently, the association of these taxa with all treatment groups is not unexpected. However, these taxa may be associated with cross-feeding interactions among microbial communities that are pivotal for maintaining system homeostasis.</p>
<p>An illustrative example is the <em>Bacteroides</em>-associated fermentation of non-digestible carbohydrates, which yields short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), CO<sub>2</sub>, and H<sub>2</sub> [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref042" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref042">42</a>]. Importantly, these gases are stabilized within the community through mutual cross-feeding, as other community members consume CO<sub>2</sub> and H<sub>2</sub> generated by <em>Bacteroides</em> [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref042" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref042">42</a>]. Interestingly, <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> does possess a hydrogenase enzyme that can oxidize H<sub>2</sub>, allowing it to grow beyond its microaerophilic limits [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref043" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref043">43</a>,<a href="#pone.0303856.ref044" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref044">44</a>]. Carbon dioxide can also be utilized by <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em>, as many <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> strains require 1 to 10% of CO<sub>2</sub> for growth [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref045">45</a>]. This evidence suggests that <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em>’s survival and growth may benefit from cross-feeding interactions with other microbial members, particularly through the utilization of fermentation byproducts, such as CO<sub>2</sub> and H<sub>2</sub>, generated by its fellow community members. Patuzzi et al. uncovered a positive correlation between the presence of Bacteroidales and <em>Campylobacter</em>-positive birds, while Clostridiales were associated with <em>Campylobacter</em>-negative birds, collectively comprising 80 to 90% of the total cecal microbial community [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref046" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref046">46</a>]. Although the <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> inoculum in the current study did not significantly increase microbial richness in the absence of protein supplementation, it is worth noting that <em>Campylobacter</em> abundance was highest in the EH group compared to the other treatment groups. Patuzzi et al. observed a significant impact on microbial richness with <em>Campylobacter</em> colonization, which varied depending on the timing of infection [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref046" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref046">46</a>]. The intricate interactions within the microbiota highlight the complexity and adaptability of microbial communities within the gut ecosystem. These interactions can be influenced by the dietary composition and the resulting metabolic end products, which, in turn, may impact the environment of the system, such as pH levels.</p>
<p>Martinez-Lopez et al. demonstrated a higher Shannon diversity index in dogs fed a hydrolyzed protein diet than a high-protein diet [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref004">4</a>]. This observation underscores the importance of the molecular weight and size of dietary proteins and peptides in influencing microbial community structure. Similarly, our current study investigated the effects of an EH and AH treatment on poultry cecal microbiota. We found that the EH treatment resulted in a higher mean Shannon diversity index than the IC treatment, with values of 4.2 in AH, 3.7 in EH, and 3.5 in IC. Moreover, our core microbiota analysis showed fewer IC-associated core microbial members at 48 h than the AH and EH groups. At 48 hours, we identified eight core microbial members associated with the IC treatment, while the EH and AH treatments exhibited 11 core members. This finding aligns with Martinez-Lopez et al. observations, suggesting that dietary components with lower molecular weights can contribute to greater microbial diversity [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref004">4</a>].</p>
<p>In our study, to characterize the overall function of the cecal community, we mapped the identified metabolites to the <em>E</em>. <em>coli</em> to represent the pathways associated with the microbial community in poultry ceca. <em>E</em>. <em>coli</em> is widely studied as a prokaryotic model organism and plays a significant role in the cecal community, closely linked to <em>Campylobacter</em> colonization [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref047" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref047">47</a>–<a href="#pone.0303856.ref049" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref049">49</a>]. Our study has unveiled disparities in protein order of structure and molecular size, indicating differences among the core microbiota and the abundance of vitamin B-associated metabolites. For example, <em>Bacteroides</em> and <em>Ruminococcus</em>, capable of vitamin B2 production [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref050">50</a>], were consistently identified as core taxa at 24 h in all treatment groups, except for NI and IC at 48 h. Riboflavin plays a pivotal role as an oxidation-reduction mediator and stimulates the growth of auxotrophic bacteria, such as <em>Faecalibacterium</em> [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref051" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref051">51</a>]. An intriguing observation emerged regarding the core microbiota associated with EH and AH at 24 h, where nine core taxa were identical (<a href="#pone.0303856.g005" class="usa-link">Fig 5</a>; <a href="#pone.0303856.s007" class="usa-link">S7 Table</a>). The remaining differentiated taxa were <em>Phascolarctobacterium</em>, linked with EH treatment, and <em>Faecalibacterium</em>, associated with AH treatment. This distinction may hold significance considering our B vitamin-associated metabolism findings (<a href="#pone.0303856.s010" class="usa-link">S10</a> and <a href="#pone.0303856.s011" class="usa-link">S11</a> Tables). At the 24-h time point, the EH treatment demonstrated enhanced metabolic processes compared to the AH treatment. It is reasonable to speculate that these specific taxa may be connected to the observed variations in metabolomic pathways related to vitamin B6 and B9 metabolism. Prior research has suggested that <em>Faecalibacterium</em> does not produce vitamins B6 and B9, while <em>Phascolarctobacterium</em> does [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref052" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref052">52</a>,<a href="#pone.0303856.ref053" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref053">53</a>]. Thus, the presence or absence of these taxa within the core microbiota could play a pivotal role in influencing the metabolic profiles associated with these vital vitamins.</p></section><section id="sec018"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Decoding <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> metabolic strategies: Vitamin B dynamics and microbial interactions in the cecal system</h3>
<p>In contrast to other pathogens, such as <em>Salmonella</em> that can alter the structure of the cecal microbial community and metabolome, as demonstrated in prior research by Mon et al. (2020), our current study found that <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> did not exert a significant impact on the composition of the microbial community or the metabolomic profile within the cecal system, particularly in the absence of supplemented EH. Compared to the <em>Salmonella</em> genome of 5.1 million base pairs, the <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> genome is only slightly over 1.5 million base pairs in size [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref054" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref054">54</a>,<a href="#pone.0303856.ref055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref055">55</a>]. Potentially, the smaller genome of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> indicates a metabolic dependency on the products made by other microbial members in the local community, such as the cecal ecosystem. The cross-feeding interactions between the microbial community and <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> possibly play a crucial role in the survival and persistence of <em>Campylobacter</em> within the system [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref009">9</a>,<a href="#pone.0303856.ref055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref055">55</a>].</p>
<p>This cross-feeding dependency between <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> and the local cecal microbiota may be rooted in their molecular preference for peptides over amino acids and intact casein and the concurrent production of vitamin B compounds within the system. When comparing the expression of B vitamin-associated metabolites among different treatments at each time point in the current study, we can postulate that these metabolic changes are a result of the <em>Campylobacter</em>-associated metabolism in relation to cecal microbiota based on treatment. Due to the 24 h preadaptation phase, during which the cecal community remained metabolically active and received fresh <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> inoculations, we can reasonably conclude that the initial presence of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> at time 0 h did not have a substantial impact on the metabolic profile. Therefore, the metabolism of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em>, based on the treatments applied, may have affected the metabolomic profiles associated with B vitamin metabolism at 24 h (<a href="#pone.0303856.g007" class="usa-link">Fig 7</a>). Moreover, no significant dysregulation of metabolites was observed between the NI and IN groups at 24 h, indicating that the supplementation with EH had a pronounced effect on <em>Campylobacter</em>.</p>
<p>Various vitamin B6-associated metabolites (166.0509_572.07; 167.0826_545.79) exhibited differential abundance patterns for both AH and EH treatments (P &lt; 0.05; <a href="#pone.0303856.g006" class="usa-link">Fig 6A and 6B</a>). However, the EH treatment showed a considerably more pronounced change in metabolite abundance compared to the AH group. Notably, in the EH group, vitamin B6-associated metabolites were nearly undetectable at 0 h but showed a significant increase in abundance at 24 h (<a href="#pone.0303856.g006" class="usa-link">Fig 6B</a>). <em>Campylobacter</em> species have been shown to synthesize vitamin B6, and it is possible that the utilization of EH sources, rather than amino acids, may enhance <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni’</em>s ability to synthesize vitamin B6. Asakura et al. established a connection between vitamin B6-dependent enzymes and flagellar glycosylation in <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref056" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref056">56</a>]. Likewise, <em>Campylobacter</em>-related bacteria, such as <em>Helicobacter pylori</em>, have demonstrated a reliance on vitamin B6 for motility and virulence [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref057" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref057">57</a>].</p>
<p>When examining riboflavin metabolism, it is worth noting that the AH treatment led to a decrease in riboflavin-associated metabolites. In contrast, there was a significant increase in the abundance of the same metabolites in the EH treatment (<a href="#pone.0303856.g006" class="usa-link">Fig 6C</a>). Possibly, <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> improved the abundance of riboflavin since <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> was most abundant in the EH group and can internally synthesize vitamin B2 or assimilate it from metabolites present in the host environment [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref058" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref058">58</a>]. More so, the uptake of riboflavin by <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> plays a role in ferritin reductase activity and iron assimilation, thereby contributing to bacterial pathogenicity. Additionally, pentose phosphate pathway and pyrimidine metabolism were increased in abundance in EH treatment at 24 h compared to 0 h, indicating potential biosynthesis of riboflavin and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) and flavin mononucleotide (FMN) compounds (<a href="#pone.0303856.s010" class="usa-link">S10</a> and <a href="#pone.0303856.s011" class="usa-link">S11</a> Tables) [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref058" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref058">58</a>]. Remarkably, in the EH treatment, riboflavin metabolism appears to have undergone a shift from one metabolite to another. Specifically, there was a significant increase in the abundance of one metabolite (162.0541_565.24) at 24 h, while another metabolite (316.1275_524.8) experienced a notable decrease in abundance during the same time frame (<a href="#pone.0303856.g006" class="usa-link">Fig 6C and 6D</a>). Riboflavin metabolism was not significantly different for AH treatment for 0 and 24 h. These findings suggest that the presence of larger peptides in distal GIT compartments, such as the ceca, could potentially contribute to the prevalence, colonization, and virulence of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> by providing the pathogen with an additional source of energy production.</p>
<p>The biosynthesis of folate by GIT bacteria is influenced by a low-carbohydrate diet and an elevated protein content [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref059" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref059">59</a>]. In our study, in contrast to the dysregulation observed in other B vitamin metabolites, the AH treatment group exhibited a remarkable increase in folate-associated metabolites at 24 h when compared to the EH treatment (204.032_616.46; 232.0446_437.58; 210.0633_449.33; 254.0907_474.21; <a href="#pone.0303856.g007" class="usa-link">Fig 7A–7D</a>). Bai et al. observed that when laying hens were supplemented with folate, unabsorbed folate was detected in the lower GIT compartments, specifically the ceca [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref060">60</a>]. Similar to the findings of the current study, while cecal microbial diversity remained unaffected, there was a negative correlation observed between folate supplementation and the presence of <em>Faecalibacterium</em> and <em>Campylobacter</em> [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref060">60</a>]. In comparison, <em>Lactobacillus</em> and <em>Bifidobacterium</em> have been observed to be positively correlated with folate status [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref059" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref059">59</a>,<a href="#pone.0303856.ref060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref060">60</a>]. This finding indicates that the use of proteases to decrease the abundance of larger peptides in the lower GIT compartments may improve the ratio of beneficial bacteria to potential pathogens, thus decreasing transmission of pathogens throughout the flock and improving poultry performance and production.</p>
<p>B vitamins play pivotal physiological roles within the host by supporting the fitness of symbiotic microorganisms while simultaneously inhibiting the proliferation of competitive species, as highlighted by Uebanso et al. [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref059" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref059">59</a>]. Our study showed that <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> inoculated EH resulted in a drastic increase in an abundance of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> (<a href="#pone.0303856.g003" class="usa-link">Fig 3E</a>) and vitamin B6 (<a href="#pone.0303856.g007" class="usa-link">Fig 7A and 7B</a>), compared to AH treatment. At the same time, vitamin B9-associated metabolites were drastically increased in the AH group from 0 to 24 h compared to EH treatment. Although dietary B vitamins are primarily absorbed in the small intestine, any excess B vitamins may find their way into the distal GIT compartments. Within these distal GIT compartments, B vitamins can function as nutrients for the local microbial inhabitants, thereby promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria and suppressing the colonization of pathogens. Hence, investigating the molecular preferences of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> and the local microbiota for peptides and amino acids is imperative. This understanding can help in the development of dietary interventions, such as the incorporation of exogenous enzymes aimed at supporting beneficial bacteria while concurrently starving pathogens like <em>Campylobacter</em>.</p>
<p>Furthermore, <em>Faecalibacterium</em> has been shown in multiple studies to engage in competitive interactions with <em>Campylobacter</em>. Our findings suggest potential competitive factors may involve vitamin B-associated metabolism in peptide-rich environments (<a href="#pone.0303856.s011" class="usa-link">S11 Table</a>) [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref046" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref046">46</a>]. Findings by Patuzzi et al. showed that <em>Campylobacter</em> does not interact positively with the cecal microbial community. However, <em>Campylobacter</em> accounted for 32 negative interactions with taxa, such as <em>Pseudomonadaceae</em>, <em>Parabacteroides</em>, and three other taxa that positively interacted with <em>Lactobacillus</em> and <em>Faecalibacterium</em>. Thus, <em>Campylobacter</em> can potentially suppress bacterial populations promoted by <em>Faecalibacterium</em> and <em>Lactobacillus</em> under suitable conditions. Moreover, <em>Campylobacter’s</em> capacity to synthesize B vitamin-associated metabolites grants a competitive advantage over <em>Faecalibacterium</em>, as our research findings indicate that potential competitive factors may involve metabolic pathways associated with vitamin B6 and B9.</p></section><section id="sec019"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Supporting a healthy microbial barrier: Peptides as modulators of GIT microbial balance</h3>
<p>Research conducted by Aloo and Oh underscores the ability of peptides to influence the microbial balance between beneficial microbiota and pathogens [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref003">3</a>]. An intriguing observation was the presence of <em>Faecalibacterium</em> as a core microbiota member in the AH group at 48 h, while <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> was notably absent from the core community at the same time point. Conversely, in the EH group, <em>Faecalibacterium</em> was not part of the core microbial community at 24 or 48 h, whereas <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> was consistently present (<a href="#pone.0303856.g005" class="usa-link">Fig 5</a>; <a href="#pone.0303856.s007" class="usa-link">S7 Table</a>). Increased relative abundance of <em>Campylobacter</em> in EH treatment may indicate a potential connection between the type of dietary protein and the prevalence of this pathogen, supporting the idea of Aloo and Oh on the ability of peptides to impact microbial compositions [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref003">3</a>]. Furthermore, these findings support the intricate interplay occurring between <em>Faecalibacterium</em> and <em>Campylobacter</em> [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref046" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref046">46</a>], which is influenced by the dietary environment.</p>
<p>In another study, a shift towards beneficial bacteria was noted using a protease. Hua et al. reported an increase in the relative abundance of <em>Lachnospiraceae</em>, <em>Bacteroides</em>, <em>Ruminococcaceae</em>, and various other beneficial bacteria in the rats’ GIT after administering a protease hydrolysate treatment [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref061" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref061">61</a>]. The presence of a protease associated with protein breakdown could signify the existence of both larger and smaller peptides, as seen in the EH and AH groups utilized in the current study. Notably, the groups <em>Lachnospiraceae</em>, <em>Bacteroides</em>, and <em>Ruminococcus torques</em> persisted in the supplemented groups until 48 h, except for <em>the Ruminococcus torques group</em>, which was absent in the IC group at the 48-h time point. Additionally, Wang et al. showed that administering enzymatically hydrolyzed collagen peptide in mice decreased the abundance of bacteria associated with GIT inflammation, such as <em>Erysipelatoclostridium</em> [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref062" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref062">62</a>]. In the current study, the abundance of <em>Erysipelatoclostridium</em> decreased by 27% (ANCOM, P &lt; 0.05) with time across treatments. Also, <em>Erysipelatoclostridium</em> was a core microbial community member in EH at 0 h and was absent at 24 and 48 h in all treatments. As a member of the <em>Clostridia</em> class, <em>Erysipelatoclostridium</em> is known for its ability to ferment amino acids through coupled reduction and oxidation of amino acids to organic acids [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref063" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref063">63</a>]. This characteristic may explain this genus’s rapid decline in the EH group, suggesting its inability to utilize peptides for growth. While not all Clostridial members are pathogenic, it is essential to note that commensal <em>Clostridia</em> play a crucial role in improving GIT function, as demonstrated by Lopetuso et al. [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref064" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref064">64</a>]. Maintaining an abundance of resident <em>Clostridia</em> species in the poultry GIT, possibly achieved through the utilization of proteases in the diet or hydrolyzed proteins to increase digestion rates in the upper GIT compartments while delivering smaller protein particles into the lower compartments, could potentially help reduce <em>Campylobacter</em> abundance in the ceca. This reduction could potentially be attributed to <em>Campylobacter</em>’s preference for peptides over amino acids and polypeptides, as observed in this study, while promoting the local growth of <em>Clostridia</em> in the lower GIT compartments, such as the ceca.</p>
<p>Enhancing protein digestion in the upper GIT compartments and reducing the presence of larger peptides in the lower GIT, achieved by using proteases and protein hydrolysates, can also induce a tolerogenic immune response that promotes systemic anti-inflammation [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref042" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref042">42</a>]. For instance, the administration of casein glycomacropeptide hydrolysate (CGP) has demonstrated beneficial effects in diabetic mice by modulating the GIT microbiota [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref065">65</a>]. GGP supplementation significantly reversed the abundance of <em>Bacteroides</em> and <em>Proteobacteria</em>, leading to an increased <em>Bacteroides</em> to <em>Firmicutes</em> ratio and an elevated abundance of <em>Ruminiclostridium</em>, <em>Allobaculum</em>, and <em>Blautia</em>. Simultaneously, it reduced the abundance of Helicobacteriacea compared to the type 2 diabetes-induced group. Notably, these microbial taxa can produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), which enter the systemic circulation and possess anti-inflammatory properties [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref042" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref042">42</a>]. The decrease in pathogenic taxa such as Helicobacteriacea is a promising indicator of the positive impact of peptides on the microbiota ratios within the GIT. Incorporating exogenous enzymes and enzyme hydrolysates in poultry feed can be advantageous, particularly when supplemented with various polysaccharides. By utilizing the Maillard reaction, this combination can facilitate molecular rearrangement and modification, producing new, smaller molecules that enhance the stability and bioactivity of peptides [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref014">14</a>]. In a study by Zhang et al., soybean peptides, L-cysteine, and xylose were combined and subjected to the Maillard reaction at 140°C for 2 hours [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref056" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref056">56</a>]. The administration of these reaction products into the diets of mice demonstrated an improvement in the abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria, such as <em>Bifidobacterium</em> and <em>Lactobacillus</em>.</p></section></section><section id="sec020"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p>While the current study acknowledges experimental limitations, such as the absence of non—<em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> inoculated casein supplemented groups and being focused on cecal incubations only to the exclusion of the upper GIT, it does offer insight into the direct impact of different casein supplements on cecal microbial populations inoculated with <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em>, aiming to model the natural ecology of the cecal system that would potentially contain a wide range of protein and corresponding hydrolysate products. The chosen media supports a diverse range of microbial members, including <em>Campylobacter</em>, enabling a comprehensive comparison of all supplemented groups against the non-supplemented treatment with <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> inocula. Additionally, the non-inoculated group and inoculated groups without casein supplementation provide insights into microbial dynamics solely based on the presence of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> within the cecal system. More specifically it appears that the form of casein supplementation impacts <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> prevalence in the ceca and may serve to enrich for this pathogen in the presence of other cecal microbiota.</p>
<p>Our study suggests that there is complex interplay between dietary protein composition and the <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> inoculated poultry cecal microbiota inoculated with <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> and the corresponding metabolomic profiles within the <em>in vitro</em> poultry cecal system. Our study suggests that there is complex interplay between dietary protein composition and the <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> inoculated poultry cecal microbiota inoculated with <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> and the corresponding metabolomic profiles within the <em>in vitro</em> poultry cecal system. Microbial communities inoculated with <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> and the metabolomic profiles within the <em>in vitro</em> poultry cecal system. <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> did not significantly alter the composition of the cecal microbial community or metabolomic profile without supplemented peptides. Indeed, casein supplementation improved microbial evenness and richness compared to non-supplemented groups. The abundance of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> was increased in the casein enzyme hydrolysate group, indicating a preference for peptides over amino acids and intact casein in the current study. The potential cross-feeding interactions during casein modifications may be based on B vitamin-associated metabolism. The study highlighted several noteworthy findings regarding the impact of different treatments on B vitamin metabolism. The presence of <em>C</em>. <em>jejuni</em> in enzyme hydrolysate treatment notably influenced the abundance of vitamin B6 and B9-associated metabolites. Moreover, whether hydrolyzed or not, the type of dietary protein significantly impacted microbial diversity, with hydrolyzed protein treatments resulting in higher diversity. The presence of specific core microbial taxa, such as <em>Bacteroides</em>, <em>Ruminococcus</em>, <em>Faecalibacterium</em>, and <em>Phascolarctobacterium</em>, appeared to play a role in shaping the metabolic profiles associated with B vitamin metabolism. These findings emphasize the intricate relationship between the GIT ecosystem’s dietary components, microbial communities, and metabolic pathways.</p>
<p>Furthermore, our findings corroborate earlier research regarding the modulation of microbial ratios through the utilization of hydrolyzed proteins, promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria while diminishing the prevalence of potential pathogens. The significant increase in <em>Campylobacter</em> abundance noted in the enzyme hydrolysate group suggests that a decrease in peptide presence in the lower GIT compartments, such as poultry ceca, could potentially diminish <em>Campylobacter</em> prevalence. However, this observation warrants further validation through in vivo studies that integrate host factors and relevant protein digestion mechanisms based on endogenous processes. Any reduction would likely manifest as a decrease in <em>Campylobacter</em> abundance in fecal and cecal droppings. Understanding these interactions holds promise for developing strategies to control pathogens such as <em>Campylobacter</em> while promoting beneficial microbial populations, ultimately improving animal health and food safety in the poultry industry [<a href="#pone.0303856.ref060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="pone.0303856.ref060">60</a>].</p></section><section id="sec021"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supporting information</h2>
