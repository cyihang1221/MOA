</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/615d/11983342/89bce6f30819/ao5c00866_0006.jpg" loading="lazy" id="ab-tgr1" height="372" width="664" alt="graphic file with name ao5c00866_0006.jpg"></p>
<p>This is the report of the HPLC-MSn-guided isolation of
new anti-inflammatory
prenylated isoflavonoids and pterocarpans from the stems of <em>Acosmium diffusissimum</em> using GNPS molecular networking
as the main tool. Cluster analysis guided the isolation of five new
prenylated isoflavonoids, diffusiflavone A–E (<strong>1–4</strong> and <strong>9</strong>), two prenylated pterocarpans, diffusicarpan
A and B (<strong>5</strong> and <strong>6</strong>), and two known compounds
6-prenylorobol (<strong>7</strong>) and 3-<em>O</em>-methylquercetin
(<strong>8</strong>). The in vitro anti-inflammatory potential of compounds <strong>1–6</strong> and <strong>9</strong> was assessed in macrophages
induced with lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Compounds <strong>2</strong>, <strong>3</strong>, <strong>5</strong>, and <strong>9</strong> were observed to have
a reduction in NO levels in at least one of the concentrations tested
(1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 μg/mL) and also reduced IL-1β
and IL-6 cytokines, especially diffusicarpan A which reduced cytokine
levels in all the concentrations tested.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Inflammation is a physiological response
of the immune system aimed
at defending the organism against various agents such as pathogens,
toxins, and injuries in order to return to homeostasis.<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a></sup> Dysregulation of this process can lead to various
disorders, including metabolic, cardiovascular, neuropsychiatric,
and neurological diseases.<sup><a href="#ref2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref2">2</a>−<a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup></p>
<p>Natural products derived
from plants are important sources of new
bioactive compounds. Plants from the Fabaceae–Papilionoideae
family have a large chemical diversity and high biological potential<sup><a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a>,<a href="#ref6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref6">6</a></sup> with a notable occurrence of isoflavonoids and pterocarpans which
have anti-inflammatory activity among their many associated pharmacological
activities.<sup><a href="#ref7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref7">7</a>−<a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a></sup><em>Acosmium diffusissimum</em> (Mohlenbr.)
Yakovlev (Fabaceae–Papilionoideae) is a species endemic to
Brazil, occurring in the states of Minas Gerais and Bahia in the caatinga
regions. Despite the existing pharmacological potential of species
within <em>Acosmium sect. Acosmium</em> (clade
Dalbergieae), their chemistry and biological potential is still poorly
explored.</p>
<p>Molecular networking is one of the most explored features
of Global
Natural Product Social Molecular Networking (GNPS). The MS/MS data
obtained experimentally are converted, added to the online platform,
and compared with internal spectral libraries showing a chemical relationship
of similarity between the compounds which are arranged in molecular
networking. For this reason, the use of this tool has been reported
as one of the main tools in dereplication studies, a strategy adopted
to target the discovery of new compounds.<sup><a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a>,<a href="#ref13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref13">13</a></sup></p>
<p>Natural products are promising sources of anti-inflammatory
compounds
due to their structural diversity and innovative mechanisms of action.
Flavonoids isolated from <em>Kalanchoe brasiliensis</em> have demonstrated significant anti-inflammatory activity, reinforcing
their therapeutic potential.<sup><a href="#ref14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref14">14</a></sup> Similarly,
vermelhotin, a fungal metabolite, inhibited nitric oxide production
in RAW 264.7 macrophages via suppression of the p38 MAPK pathway.<sup><a href="#ref15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref15">15</a></sup> These findings highlight the importance of natural
metabolite prospecting as a strategic approach for the discovery of
new anti-inflammatory agents.</p>
<p>In this work, we report the application
of GNPS molecular networking
to guide the isolation of five new prenylated isoflavonoids (<strong>1–4</strong> and <strong>9</strong>) and two prenylated pterocarpans
(<strong>4</strong> and <strong>5</strong>) (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>) and their anti-inflammatory activity by
evaluating inflammatory mediators (NO, IL-1β, and IL-6) in LPS-induced
macrophages, as well as the isolation of two already known compounds
6-prenylorobol (<strong>7</strong>) and 3-<em>O</em>-methylquercetin
(<strong>8</strong>) from the stems of <em>A. diffusissimum</em>.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="fig1"><h3 class="obj_head">Figure 1.</h3>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=11983342_ao5c00866_0001.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/615d/11983342/ba44f7983de9/ao5c00866_0001.jpg" loading="lazy" id="gr1" height="378" width="722" alt="Figure 1"></a></p>

<figcaption><p>Compounds from the stems of <em>A. diffusissimum</em>.</p></figcaption></figure></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results and Discussion</h2>
<p>The flavonoid-rich fraction
from the stems of <em>A.
diffusissimum</em> was analyzed by LC–MS/MS in positive
mode, and its data were processed using GNPS. The molecular networking
obtained showed 32 clusters and 83 nodes. The clusters were evaluated
according to their combination with the spectral library, retention
times, and comparison of the LC–MS/MS data with the literature.
The nodes are connected by lines whose thickness is directly related
to the cosine values, where thicker lines correspond to a higher cosine
value and therefore greater similarity between the nodes. Based on
this, the compounds were compiled into clusters A and B (prenylated
isoflavonoids), C (polymethoxylated flavonoids), D (pterocarpans),
and E (flavonoids) (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>).</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="fig2"><h3 class="obj_head">Figure 2.</h3>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=11983342_ao5c00866_0002.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/615d/11983342/f19e92094f58/ao5c00866_0002.jpg" loading="lazy" id="gr2" height="386" width="693" alt="Figure 2"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/fig2/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>Molecular networking of the flavonoid-rich fraction of <em>A. diffusissimum</em> stems. Cluster A and B (prenylated
isoflavonoids), Cluster C (polymethoxylated flavonoids), Cluster D
(pterocarpans), and Cluster E (flavonoids). Nodes with bold borders
were isolated and characterized substances and identified with their
respective numbers, nodes with blue borders were substances annotated
and confirmed with an internal standard (substances isolated and characterized
from <em>A. diffusissimum</em> stems) and nodes
with green borders were annotated substances.</p></figcaption></figure><p>Cluster A exhibited a node at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 367 which showed a base peak ion in the MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum
of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 183. The MS<sup>3</sup> spectrum
of the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 183 fragment ion showed
product ions of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> = 165 (–H<sub>2</sub>O) and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> = 155 (−CO).
The fragmentation pattern for precursor ion <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 367 [M + H]<sup>+</sup> was not found in the literature.
However, evaluation of the hit library match from the GNPS database
revealed a structural similarity with the compound corylin, an isoflavonoid
with a pyran ring inserted into its basic skeleton, whose fragmentation
pattern with a base peak ion in the MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum is obtained
by an RDA fragmentation reaction of the C ring.<sup><a href="#ref16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref16">16</a>,<a href="#ref17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref17">17</a></sup> This information suggested that the nodes present in this cluster
could be compounds not yet described for this class, and the isolation
of these compounds was prioritized.</p>
<p>The nodes at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 383 and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 397 and their respective adducts [M
+ H + Na]<sup>+</sup> 405 and [M + H + Na]<sup>+</sup> 419 shared
fragment ions in the MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 365 and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 337. These fragments were generated from losses of H<sub>2</sub>O (18 Da) and CO + H2O (46 Da) and neutral losses of HOCH<sub>3</sub> (32 Da) followed by the loss of CO (28 Da), for precursor ions <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 383 and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 397, respectively (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.5c00866/suppl_file/ao5c00866_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Schemes S1 and S2</a>). The product ion of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 183 (RDA fragmentation)<sup><a href="#ref18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref18">18</a>−<a href="#ref24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref24">24</a></sup> was also observed for both compounds, but with intensities of 100
and 49%, respectively (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.5c00866/suppl_file/ao5c00866_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figures S2 and S12</a>).</p>
<p>In the MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum of precursor ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 397, the base ion was observed at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 382, corresponding to the loss of a
methyl group
(15 Da), a feature absent in the ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 383. The product ion was also identified at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 200, indicating cleavage of the C ring by RDA
after radical elimination. These data confirm that radical elimination
occurs due to the presence of a methoxyl at C-3. In the MS<sup>3</sup> spectrum of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 382, the following
fragment ions were detected at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 346 (−2H<sub>2</sub>O), <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 364 (−H<sub>2</sub>O), <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 200 (RDA and –CH<sub>3</sub>), and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 155 (RDA and –CO). On the other hand, the MS<sup>3</sup> spectrum of the ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 383 showed a loss of 32 Da (−HOCH<sub>3</sub>), giving rise
to the fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 151.</p>
<p>The nodes at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 397 and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 411 showed common fragment ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 379 (–H<sub>2</sub>O and –HOCH<sub>3</sub>, respectively) and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 351
(−CO + H<sub>2</sub>O and –CO + HOCH<sub>3</sub>), as
well as the same RDA pathway product ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 197, with relative intensities of 100% and 50%, respectively
(<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.5c00866/suppl_file/ao5c00866_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figures S23 and S33</a>). In the MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum of the precursor ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 411, the base ion was identified at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 396, resulting from a radical loss of 15 Da (−CH<sub>3</sub>). The MS<sup>3</sup> spectrum suggests that the ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 363 and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 200 (after RDA) are also dystonic ions, representing a
fragmentation pathway that differentiates the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 411 ion from its analogue at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 397.</p>
<p>Also, in the MS<sup>3</sup> spectrum of the
ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 411, fragment ions were
observed at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 378 (−H<sub>2</sub>O), <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 363 (−H<sub>2</sub>O and –CH<sub>3</sub>), <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 197 (RDA), and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 179
(RDA and loss of –H<sub>2</sub>O). In contrast, in the MS<sup>3</sup> spectrum of the precursor
ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 397, fragments were observed
at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 197 (RDA), <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 182 (RDA and loss of –CH<sub>3</sub>),
and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 151 (RDA followed by loss
of –CO and –H<sub>2</sub>O) (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.5c00866/suppl_file/ao5c00866_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Scheme S5</a>).</p>
<p>Cluster D showed nodes at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 369 and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 383 which
shared neutral
losses of 164 Da, generating the base peak ions in the MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 205 and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 219, respectively. The MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum of the ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 369
also showed product ions of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 341, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 177, and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 153. The MS<sup>3</sup> spectrum showed a common fragment
ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 187 for both precursor
ions, as well as product ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 201 (−H<sub>2</sub>O) and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 147 (−CH3 and –OC(CH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>) for
the ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 383. Analysis of
the fragmentation pattern of this cluster with data in the literature
indicated the presence of pterocarpans with a dimethyl pyran ring.<sup><a href="#ref23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref23">23</a>−<a href="#ref25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref25">25</a></sup> On the other hand, there was no correlation between the LC–MS/MS
data and the literature, so these compounds were also prioritized
for guided isolation.</p>
<p>The flavonoid-rich fraction obtained from
the stems of <em>A. diffusissimum</em> was fractionated
and monitored by
LC–MS using the fragmentation pattern and UV absorption profile.
The guided isolation allowed us to isolate and characterize five prenylated
isoflavonoids (<strong>1–4</strong> and <strong>9</strong>) and two
pterocarpans (<strong>5</strong> and <strong>6</strong>) and known compounds
6-prenylorobol (<strong>7</strong>)<sup><a href="#ref26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref26">26</a>−<a href="#ref28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref28">28</a></sup> and 3-<em>O</em>-methylquercetin
(<strong>8</strong>),<sup><a href="#ref31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref31">31</a>,<a href="#ref32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref32">32</a></sup> which were used as internal standards
to confirm the nodes noted on the molecular networking. In continuing
the study of the chemical profile of <em>A. diffusissimum</em>, fractionation of higher-polarity fractions was also carried out
and it was possible to isolate and characterize compound <strong>9</strong>, demonstrating the potential of <em>A. diffusissimum</em> as a source for obtaining other compounds of this class.<sup><a href="#ref29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref29">29</a>,<a href="#ref30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref30">30</a></sup></p>
<p>Compound <strong>1</strong> was obtained as a yellow, amorphous
solid.
The molecular formula was determined as C<sub>21</sub>H<sub>19</sub>O<sub>7</sub> by HRESIMS from <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 383.1125 [M + H] <sup>+</sup> (calcd for 383.1128, error: −0.6
ppm). In the <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum were observed signals at δ
8.38 s (1H, s) and its corresponding olefinic carbon signal at δ
154.49 indicated the presence of an isoflavone skeleton.<sup><a href="#ref33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref33">33</a>,<a href="#ref34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref34">34</a></sup> The <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum also exhibited one singlet at δ
6.77 (1H, s) and two aromatic proton signals with <em>meta</em>-coupling at δ 6.73 (1H, d) and δ 6.92 (1H, d) with <em>J</em> = 2.0 Hz, indicating the presence of a penta- and tetrasubstituted
ring in the structure. In the <sup>13</sup>C NMR spectrum was observed
a methine carbon signal at δ 90.82, which corroborates the presence
of a penta-substituted ring due to the ortho-protective effect of
the substituents on the ring.<sup><a href="#ref35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref35">35</a></sup> The <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum also observed the presence of signals typical
of a 2,2-dimethylpyran at δ 1.38 (6H, s) and two <em>cis</em>-coupled vinyl protons at δ 5.74 (1H, d, <em>J</em> = 9.8 Hz) and δ 6.37 (1H, d, <em>J</em> = 9.8 Hz)
together with the signals at δ 27.50, δ 76.03, δ
131.29, and δ 122.08 in the <sup>13</sup>C NMR spectrum.<sup><a href="#ref36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref36">36</a>−<a href="#ref42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref42">42</a></sup> The presence of a singlet at δ 12.62 characteristic of a chelated
hydroxyl in the <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum indicates the insertion
of a hydroxyl at position 5. The correlations between H-8 (δ
6.77) and carbons C-9 (δ 150.01), C-10 (δ 105.78), C-7
(δ 154.60), C-4 (δ 180.43), and C-5 (δ 146.53) present
in the HMBC correlation spectrum confirmed the presence of a trioxygenation
in ring A of the isoflavone. The insertion of the methoxyl was confirmed
by the correlation of the protons at δ 3.89 of the methoxyl
with C-7 (δ 154.60). The pyran portion was confirmed at positions
C-4′ and C-5′ by the correlations of H-4″ (δ
6.37) with C-6′ (δ 140.5) and C-5′ (δ 110.3)
(<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.5c00866/suppl_file/ao5c00866_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S71</a>). From these spectroscopic
data, the structure of compound <strong>1</strong> was named diffusiflavone
A.</p>
<p>The molecular formula of compound <strong>2</strong> was determined
as C<sub>22</sub>H<sub>21</sub>O<sub>7</sub> by HRESIMS at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 397.1282 [M + H] <sup>+</sup> (calcd
for 397.1290, error: −2.0 ppm). The NMR spectroscopic data
of compound <strong>2</strong> was very similar to that of compound <strong>1</strong>. The <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum also exhibited typical signals
of the isoflavone skeleton and 2,2-dimethylpyran in the structure.
However, signals were observed at δ 3.87 in the <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum and at δ 56.43 in the <sup>13</sup>C NMR spectrum,
which indicated that <strong>2</strong> was a methoxylated derivative
of <strong>1</strong> whose insertion at the C-3′ position was
confirmed by HMBC experiments through the correlations between the
protons at δ 3.87 with C-3′ (δ 148.25). Analysis
of the spectroscopic data led to the identification of compound <strong>2</strong>, which is named diffusiflavone B.</p>
<p>Compound <strong>3</strong> was shown to be an isomer of compound <strong>2</strong> with the molecular
formula of C<sub>22</sub>H<sub>21</sub>O<sub>7</sub> by HRESIMS of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 397.1282 [M + H]<sup>+</sup> (calcd
for 397.1290, error: −0.9
ppm). The NMR data were also very similar to compound <strong>2</strong>. However, a signal at δ 60.94 was observed in the <sup>13</sup>C NMR spectrum which indicated the presence of a sterically hindered
methoxyl<sup><a href="#ref23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref23">23</a></sup> in <strong>3</strong> suggesting
insertion at the C-6 position, which was confirmed by HMBC experiments
through the correlations between δ 3.89 and C-7 (δ 159.25).
Based on these spectroscopic data, the structure of compound <strong>3</strong> has been determined and named diffusiflavone C.</p>
<p>Compound <strong>4</strong> was obtained as a white, amorphous solid.
The molecular formula was determined as C<sub>23</sub>H<sub>23</sub>O<sub>7</sub> by HRESIMS from <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 411.1447 [M + H]<sup>+</sup> (calcd for 411.1438, error: −2.2
ppm). As with compounds <strong>2</strong> and <strong>3</strong>, the <sup>1</sup>H and <sup>13</sup>C data were very similar, except for the
presence of the signals at δ 3.93 (3H, s) and δ 3.90 (3H,
s) in the <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum and their corresponding carbons
at δ 60.91 and δ 56.47, in addition to δ 3.88 and
δ 56.40, which indicated that <strong>4</strong> was a polymethoxylated
derivative of <strong>1</strong> whose insertion of methoxyls in the C-6
and C-3′ carbons was suggested by the maintenance of the chelated
hydroxyl signal at δ 12.97 and confirmed by HMBC experiments.
Spectroscopic data confirm the structure of compound <strong>4</strong>, designated as diffusiflavone D.</p>
<p>Compound <strong>9</strong> was
isolated as a white, amorphous solid.
The molecular formula was C<sub>21</sub>H<sub>21</sub>O<sub>7</sub> by HRESIMS at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 385.1282 [M
+ H]<sup>+</sup> (calcd for 385.1286, error: −1.0 ppm). The
NMR data observed the signals at δ 8.05 (1H, s) in the <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum and δ 155.05 in the <sup>13</sup>C NMR
spectrum, one singlet at δ 6.68 (1H, s) and δ 91.51 in
the <sup>13</sup>C NMR spectrum, which indicated the presence of the
same pattern observed in compounds <strong>1</strong> to <strong>4</strong> for ring A. The aromatic proton signals with <em>meta</em>-coupling were also observed at δ 6.73 (1H, d) and δ
6.90 (1H, d) with <em>J</em> = 2.1 Hz. However, in the <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum were observed a triplet at δ 5.35 (1H,
t, <em>J</em> = 7.5 Hz) and a duplet at δ 3.34 (1H,
d, <em>J</em> = 7.5 Hz) and δ 1.74 (6H, s). The <sup>13</sup>C NMR spectrum also were observed signals at δ 17.89,
δ 25.94, and δ 29.36 which suggested the presence of a
prenyl unit at <strong>9</strong>.<sup><a href="#ref42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref42">42</a>,<a href="#ref43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref43">43</a></sup> In the HMBC spectrum
of <strong>9</strong> (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>), correlations were observed between H-1″ and C-3′
(δ 129.76), C-4′ (δ 144.61), and C2″ (δ
123.96) and between H-2′ and C-1′(δ 124.70), C1″
(δ 29.36), and C-6′ (δ 114.81) which confirmed
the insertion of the prenyl into C-3′. The spectroscopic data
described allowed <strong>9</strong> to be defined as diffusiflavone E.<sup><a href="#ref44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref44">44</a>,<a href="#ref45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref45">45</a></sup></p>

The NMR data indicate a similar structure to compound <strong>5</strong>. However, the signals at δ 3.86 and δ 61.06 demonstrated

where the insertion at the C-9 position was confirmed by HMBC spectra
through the correlations between the δ 3.86 signals and C-9

<p>The effects of the compounds on cell viability were assessed using
the MTT assay to ensure the noncytotoxic concentration ranges. The
substances tested did not produce cell death in RAW 264.7 macrophages,
when compared to the control group, at concentrations of 1.25, 2.5,
5, 10, and 20 μg/mL (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.5c00866/suppl_file/ao5c00866_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S72</a>).</p>
<p>The compounds tested did not induce NO in RAW 264.7 macrophages
without exposure to LPS (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">4</a></a>A). Additionally, compounds <strong>1</strong>, <strong>4</strong>, <strong>5</strong>, and <strong>6</strong> reduced NO levels in macrophage
cultures stimulated with LPS compared to cells stimulated and treated
with vehicle (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.001), at concentrations of
1.25 to 5 μg/mL (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">4</a></a>B). These data indicate that the compounds have anti-inflammatory
activity.</p>

<section id="sec3.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">General Experimental Procedures</h3>
<p>The IR spectra were
recorded on a Shimadzu FTIR Spirit spectrometer. The 1D and 2D nuclear
magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were obtained on Bruker AVANCE III
Hb spectrometers at 400 and 500 MHz (Bruker Co., Ltd., Bremen, Germany).
The residual nondeuterated solvent peaks were used as references.
High-resolution mass spectrometric data analyses were recorded using
a microTOFII ESI-TOF mass spectrometer, and tandem mass spectrometry
(MSn) was performed on an AmazonX ESI-IT instrument (Bruker, Billerica,
MA, USA). Preparative C18 (Shim-pack GIST, 250 mm × 20 mm, 5
μm) and analytical C18 columns (Shim-pack GIST, 250 mm ×
4.6 mm, 5 μm) were used for the separation. Silica gel with
particles of 40–63 μm, 230–400 mesh, and 60 Å
was used for column chromatography (CC) and vacuum liquid chromatography
(VLC).</p></section><section id="sec3.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Plant Material</h3>
<p>The stems of <em>A. diffusissimum</em> were collected in January 2021 at Fazenda Esperança (14°43′28″S
and 40°42′37″W) located in the municipality of
Boa Vista do Tupim, Bahia, Brazil, and identified by Domingos Bencio
Oliveira Silva Cardoso. An exsiccate is deposited in the Herbarium
of the State University of Feira de Santana (HUEFS), in Bahia, under
number 246074. The species was registered for access to the genetic
heritage in SisGen with process no. A39AEA5.</p></section><section id="sec3.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Analysis by LC-ESIMS<sup>n</sup>
</h3>
<p>The rich fraction
of flavonoids was analyzed by HPLC (Shimadzu), using an analytical
chromatographic C18 column (Kromasil; 250 × 4.6 × 5 μm)
coupled to a mass spectrometer (Ion-Trap AmazonX, Bruker), with electrospray
ionization (ESI). The analyzed samples were solubilized in MeOH (1
mg/mL) and filtered with PVDF filters (0.45 μm). The separation
process was performed through a gradient system by using acidified
water (0.1% HCOOH) as solvent A and MeOH as solvent B, with an injection
volume of 10 μL and a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. The ESI source
parameters were defined as follows: capillary, 4.5 kV; end plate offset,
500 V; nebulizer, 35 psi; dry gas, N2; flow rate of 8 mL/min; and
temperature of 300 °C. The analyses were performed by operating
in the positive mode with a range of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 50–1500.</p></section><section id="sec3.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Molecular Networking</h3>
<p>The data obtained by LC–MS/MS
were converted into mzXML format directly from Bruker Data Analysis
4.2. The molecular networks were generated using GNPS (<a href="http://gnps.ucsd.edu" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://gnps.ucsd.edu</a>) with the following
parameters: parent mass tolerance of 2.0 Da, MS/MS fragment ion tolerance
of 0.5 Da, cosine score of 0.7 and minimum matching peaks of 6, analogue
search, and filter spectra of G6 as blanks before the network was
enabled. The data were subsequently visualized using Cytoscape 3.10.2
software. The dereplicated compounds were identified based on the
comparison of MS/MS data with the literature.</p></section><section id="sec3.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Extraction and Isolation</h3>
<p>The dried and powdered plant
material was obtained from 5.7 kg of stems, which was extracted at
room temperature with 96% EtOH (×3 for 72 h) with later concentration
through a rotary evaporator at 40 °C to obtain 220 g of crude
extract (CE). One portion of CE (150 g) was subjected to defatting
with hexane (3 × 500 mL), yielding 130 g of crude defatted ethanolic
extract which was subjected to vacuum liquid chromatography using
silica gel and eluted with <em>n</em>-hexane, <em>n</em>-hexane/CHCl<sub>3</sub>, CHCl<sub>3</sub>, EtOAc, EtOAc/MeOH, and
MeOH. The CHCl<sub>3</sub> phase (1.4 g) was submitted to column chromatography
on silica gel, using an elution gradient of hexane/EtOAc (95:5, 90:10,
85:15, 80:20, 75:25, 1:1, 25:75, and 0:100), EtOAc/MeOH (1:1), and
MeOH. Fractions of 125 mL were collected, and 14 fractions were obtained.
Fractions 8 and 9 (68 mg) were grouped and subjected to preparative
RP-HPLC using MeOH and H<sub>2</sub>O with 0.1% HCOOH as the eluent
(8 mL/min) through a gradient system with the following parameters:
0–15 min (40–70% B) and 15–60 min (70% B), obtaining
compounds <strong>4</strong> (1.5 mg) and <strong>6</strong> (1.0 mg). Fractions
10–13 (328 mg) were grouped and subjected to preparative RP-HPLC
using MeOH and H2O with 0.1% HCOOH as the eluent (8 mL/min) through
a gradient system with the following parameters: 0–60 min (65%
B), obtaining compounds <strong>1</strong> (11.5 mg), <strong>2</strong> (6.0
mg), <strong>3</strong> (6.9 mg), and <strong>5</strong> (12.8 mg). The AcOEt
phase (4.2 g) was submitted to column chromatography on silica gel,
using an elution gradient of hexane/CH<sub>2</sub>Cl<sub>2</sub> (1:1
and 20:80), CH<sub>2</sub>Cl<sub>2</sub>, CH<sub>2</sub>Cl/<sub>2</sub>EtOAc (80:20, 1:1, and 20:80), EtOAc, EtOAc/MeOH (80:20 and 1:1),
and MeOH. Fractions of 125 mL were collected, and 13 fractions were
obtained. Fractions 6 and 7 (1.35 g) were fractionated to column chromatography
on silica gel, using an elution gradient of CHCl<sub>3</sub>/EtOAc
(85:15, 80:20, and 1:1), EtOAc, EtOAc/MeOH (1:1), and MeOH. Fractions
of 50 mL were collected, and 13 fractions were obtained. Fraction
67–2 (78 mg) was subjected to preparative RP-HPLC using MeOH
and H<sub>2</sub>O with 0.1% HCOOH as the eluent (8 mL/min) through
a gradient system with the following parameters: 0–15 min (40–70%
B), 15–20 min (70–80% B), and 20–60 min (80%
B), obtaining compound <strong>7</strong> (2 mg) and compound <strong>9</strong> (11.7 mg). Fraction 67–4 (302 mg) was subjected to preparative
RP-HPLC using MeOH and H<sub>2</sub>O with 0.1% HCOOH as the eluent
(8 mL/min) through a gradient system with the following parameters:
0–60 min (42–70% B), obtaining compound <strong>8</strong> (7 mg).</p>

<p>RAW 264.7 cell line (ATCC
No. TIB71) was used in the study, which is monocytes/macrophages derived
from a tumor induced in the <em>Mus musculus</em> species. For in vitro stimulation assays, they were cultured in
DMEM (VITROGEN) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotic
(streptomycin and penicillin) and were maintained in cell culture
flasks in an incubator at 37 °C with a 5% CO<sub>2</sub> atmosphere.</p></section><section id="sec3.7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Cell Viability</h3>
<p>Cell viability was estimated by an MTT
assay (Mosmann 1983). For that, 100 μL of complete RPMI-1640
medium containing 10 μL of MTT solution (MTT 5 mg/mL in PBS)
was added to each well. After 4 h of incubation, the MTT-containing
medium was discarded, and the precipitate was solubilized in 100 μL
of DMSO. Optical density was read at 570 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer
BioTek ELx800, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, MA, US).</p></section><section id="sec3.8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Nitric Oxide and Cytokine Production</h3>
<p>Nitric oxide production
was indirectly estimated by measurement of nitrite, a major stable
product of nitric oxide, using Griess solution (Griess, 1879). Briefly,
50 μL of the culture supernatant was mixed with an equal amount
of Griess solution (0.1% <em>N</em>-[1-naphthyl] ethylenediamine
and 1% sulfanilamide in 5% <em>ortho</em>-phosphoric acid)
at room temperature for 10 min. Optical density was read at 540 nm
using a BioTek ELx800 microplate spectrophotometer, Thermo Fisher
Scientific Inc. (Waltham, MA, US). Sodium nitrite was used for the
standard curve.</p>
<p>IL-1β and IL-6 levels in the culture supernatant
were determined by sandwich ELISA, according to the manufacturer’s
instructions. Optical density was read at 450 nm using a microplate
spectrophotometer from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, MA,
US).</p></section><section id="sec3.9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical Analysis</h3>
<p>Inhibition curves were fitted
by nonlinear regression analysis assuming a sigmoidal concentration–response
curve model and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration was estimated.
All data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean
(SEM) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism software version 9.1.0, GraphPad
Software Inc. (San Diego, CA, US), using one-way analysis of variance
followed by Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons. The results
were considered statistically significant when <em>p</em> &lt;
0.05.</p></section></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
