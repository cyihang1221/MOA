</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Simple Summary</h2>
<p>The Taklamakan Desert is a harsh and extreme environment, yet its microorganisms hold untapped potential to benefit agriculture and medicine. In this study, we discovered a new microbial species, <em>Streptomyces xanthus</em>, isolated from desert soil. Genomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that this microorganism produces several unknown compounds. We isolated one novel and four known compounds from this strain, all reported for the first time in desert environments. Notably, one known compound, thiolutin, showed strong activity against five local plant pathogenic fungi, with a production yield of 270 mg/L. These findings highlight the desert as a reservoir of new microbial species, holding great potential for discovering important bioactive compounds.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> actinomycete, polyphasic taxonomy, genomics, metabolomics, thiolutin, Taklamakan desert</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>The Taklamakan Desert is an extreme environment supporting a unique and diverse microbial community with significant potential for exploration. Strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup>, isolated from desert soil, shares 98.43% 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity with <em>Streptomyces alkaliterrae</em> OF1<sup>T</sup>. Polyphasic taxonomy confirmed <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> as a novel species, named <em>Streptomyces xanthus</em>. Genomic analysis revealed that only one of the strain’s 25 biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) formed a cluster of gene families (CGFs) within the MIBiG database, emphasizing its genomics uniqueness. LC-MS/MS and Feature-Based Molecular Networking (FBMN) identified 33 metabolites across various categories, including alkaloids, saponins, benzoic acids, and benzofurans, most of which remain uncharacterized. Further chemical investigation led to the isolation of one novel compound, aconicarpyrazine C, and four known compounds: thiolutin, dibutyl phthalate, bis(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate, and <em>N</em>-acetyltryptamine. Thiolutin exhibited strong activity against five local fungal pathogens that cause plant diseases, with a production yield of 270 mg/L. These results establish a foundation for pilot-scale thiolutin production and its potential development as an antifungal agent for agricultural applications. Our findings highlight deserts as a valuable source of novel actinomycetes and bioactive natural products with immense potential for future research and development.</p></section><section id="sec1-biology-14-00164"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>The Taklamakan Desert is the second most mobile desert in the world, and is characterized by intense solar radiation, significant diurnal temperature fluctuations, prolonged periods of drought and heat, nutrient scarcity, and soil salinization. The microbial community has evolved with a greater degree of complexity in its adaptive mechanisms and a higher level of diversity in this extreme environment [<a href="#B1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-biology-14-00164">1</a>]. Microorganisms continuously adapt and evolve in this environment to develop unique metabolic abilities. Li Shuai [<a href="#B2-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2-biology-14-00164">2</a>] recently identified numerous novel bacteria in desert soil using culturomics-based metagenomics. Ting Wang’s research [<a href="#B1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-biology-14-00164">1</a>], which was conducted in the Taklamakan Desert, led to the discovery of 320 strains of actinomycetes, which were distributed across 23 genera and included 16 potential new species. Notably, strains from 20 of these genera exhibited activity against at least one pathogen. The continuous discovery of new compounds and genes from desert actinomycetes highlights the role of deserts as repositories of microbial diversity and novel genetic material [<a href="#B3-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3-biology-14-00164">3</a>,<a href="#B4-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4-biology-14-00164">4</a>,<a href="#B5-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5-biology-14-00164">5</a>]. Despite recent reports on desert microorganisms [<a href="#B6-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6-biology-14-00164">6</a>], the Taklamakan Desert remains largely unexplored, emphasizing the need for further exploration to harness its potential and contribute to the efforts to address antibiotic resistance.</p>
<p>For many years, natural products from microorganisms, particularly actinomycetes, have been a rich source of bioactive compounds [<a href="#B7-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-biology-14-00164">7</a>]. Renowned for their extensive secondary metabolic capabilities, these microorganisms produce more than two-thirds of the antibiotics currently in clinical use [<a href="#B8-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8-biology-14-00164">8</a>]. In 1943, Waksman and Henrici first described the genus <em>Streptomyces</em> and isolated the antibiotic streptomycin [<a href="#B9-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-biology-14-00164">9</a>], marking a new era in antibiotic research and highlighting <em>Streptomyces</em> species for their robust metabolic capacity. The genomes of <em>Streptomyces</em> are vast and intricate, and harbor numerous biosynthetic gene clusters [<a href="#B10-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10-biology-14-00164">10</a>]. Antibiotics produced by <em>Streptomyces</em>, such as erythromycin [<a href="#B11-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11-biology-14-00164">11</a>], tetracycline [<a href="#B12-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12-biology-14-00164">12</a>], avermectin [<a href="#B13-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13-biology-14-00164">13</a>], and validamycin [<a href="#B14-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14-biology-14-00164">14</a>], have provided significant resources and drug candidates for the pharmaceutical, agricultural, and industrial sectors. Furthermore, Wang isolated a novel antibiotic from the streptogramin family, named desulfurizing griseo-viridin, from a strain of <em>Streptomyces</em> discovered in the Taklamakan Desert. Additionally, a representative member of the established polyunsaturated macrolactones family, griseoviridin, was also identified, and both compounds exhibited antimicrobial activity through the inhibition of translation [<a href="#B1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-biology-14-00164">1</a>]. However, despite these achievements, the discovery of new exploitable antibiotics remains a significant challenge owing to the growing prevalence of antibiotic resistance and other application issues.</p>
<p>In this study, we isolated an actinomycete strain from the desert and identified it as a novel species, named <em>Streptomyces xanthus</em>, using polyphasic taxonomic methods. To further explore the biosynthetic potential of this strain, we conducted comprehensive genomics and Feature-Based Molecular Networking (FBMN) metabolomic analyses. The results revealed that the strain exhibits not only genomic uniqueness but also significant diversity in its metabolic products. The crude extracts of this strain exhibited significant activity against fungi, gram-positive bacteria, and gram-negative bacteria. Consequently, we proceeded to isolate and purify the bioactive substances, ultimately identifying a novel pyrazine compound, along with four known compounds. We further evaluated their activity against five local plant diseases.</p></section><section id="sec2-biology-14-00164"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec2dot1-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Isolation and Maintenance of the Microorganism</h3>
<p>Strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> was isolated from soil samples collected in the Taklamakan Desert, Xinjiang, China, and the samples were stored at 4 °C prior to cultivation. To prepare the samples for isolation, they were dried in a hot-air oven at 60 °C for 2 h. From the pre-treated soil samples, 1 g was weighed, dissolved in 9 mL of 0.9% saline, and transferred to a 50 mL triangular flask. The solution was then incubated at room temperature (120 rpm) for 30 min. The solution was serially diluted with sterile distilled water (10, 100, 1000, and 10,000 times) and spread on isolation media, which were incubated at 37 °C for 30 days. The isolation media consisted of soluble starch 20 g, KNO<sub>3</sub> 1 g, K<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub> 0.5 g, MgSO<sub>4</sub>·7H<sub>2</sub>O 0.5 g, NaCl 1 g, FeSO<sub>4</sub>·7H<sub>2</sub>O 0.01 g, CaCO<sub>3</sub> 1 g, agar 18 g, soil extract 1000 mL, pH 8.0, and supplemented with nalidixic acid (50 mg/L) and anti-fungal (100 mg/L) to inhibit the growth of most bacteria and fungi. The isolates were purified using International Actinomycete Project (ISP-4) medium, and the purified strains were stored in 20% glycerol and freeze-dried skim milk.</p></section><section id="sec2dot2-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Molecular Characterization</h3>
<p>DNA was isolated using an E.Z.N.A.<sup>®</sup> Tissue DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Guangzhou, China). PCR amplification of 16S rRNA gene sequences was performed according to the method described by Nicole [<a href="#B15-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15-biology-14-00164">15</a>]. PCR products were purified using the E.Z.N.A.<sup>®</sup> Gel Extraction Kit (Omega Bio-Tek) and sent to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) for sequencing in both directions. The 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity was calculated for related type strains using the EzBioCloud web server [<a href="#B16-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16-biology-14-00164">16</a>], and subsequently submitted to the GenBank database.</p>
<p>Genome sequencing was conducted by Personal Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) using the Nanopore PromethION48 (New York, NY, USA) and Illumina NovaSeq platforms (San Diego, CA, USA). A5-MiSeq [<a href="#B17-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17-biology-14-00164">17</a>] and SPAdes [<a href="#B18-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18-biology-14-00164">18</a>] were used for ab initio assembly, and base correction was performed using Pilon to obtain the final genomic sketch. DNA sequences of 89 housekeeping genes were extracted and MLSA was conducted using autoMLST (<a href="https://automlst.ziemertlab.com" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://automlst.ziemertlab.com</a>, accessed on 3 December 2024) [<a href="#B19-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19-biology-14-00164">19</a>]. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the NJ, ML, and ME methods using MEGA-X software [<a href="#B20-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20-biology-14-00164">20</a>], with <em>Kitasatospora setae</em> KM-6054<sup>T</sup> as an outgroup. Bootstrap replications (1000 replicates) were performed. The ANI was determined using OrthoANI 0.93.1 [<a href="#B21-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21-biology-14-00164">21</a>]. The phylogenomic tree of the whole-genome sequences was constructed using the type (strain) Genome Server platform (<a href="https://tygs.dsmz.de/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://tygs.dsmz.de/</a>, accessed on 2 December 2024.) [<a href="#B22-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22-biology-14-00164">22</a>].</p></section><section id="sec2dot3-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Physiological and Chemotaxonomic Characterization</h3>
<p>The cultural characteristics of the strain TRM 70308<sup>T</sup> were observed with confidence on ISP 1–7 media after exactly two weeks of incubation at 37 °C [<a href="#B23-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23-biology-14-00164">23</a>]. The colors of the aerial mycelia, substrate mycelia, and spores were assessed by comparison with ISCC-NBS color charts [<a href="#B24-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B24-biology-14-00164">24</a>]. Mycelial and spore morphologies were observed by light microscopy (OLYMPUS CX23, Tokyo, Japan) and scanning electron microscopy (Quanta; FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA) after incubation on ISP4 plates at 37 °C for 7 days. The strains were tested for growth at different temperatures, pH values, and NaCl tolerances on Gauze’s medium after 14 days of incubation. Sole carbon utilization, cellulose degradation, and starch and nitrate reduction were investigated as described previously [<a href="#B23-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23-biology-14-00164">23</a>]. The sugar composition and isomers of diaminopimelic acid, polar lipids, fatty acids, and menaquinones in the whole cell were analyzed using established methods [<a href="#B25-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B25-biology-14-00164">25</a>,<a href="#B26-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B26-biology-14-00164">26</a>,<a href="#B27-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27-biology-14-00164">27</a>].</p></section><section id="sec2dot4-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. BGCs Mining and Network Analysis</h3>
<p>BGCs were identified using antiSMASH (v7.1.0) online [<a href="#B28-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28-biology-14-00164">28</a>]. BiG-SCAPE (v1.1.5) was executed in auto mode, and experimentally validated BGCs from the MIBiG database 3.0 served as a reference [<a href="#B29-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29-biology-14-00164">29</a>]. The protein sequences predicted by antiSMASH for each BGC were searched within the predicted Pfam domains, and a similarity network was constructed using a Jaccard index of 0.3 to classify the diverse BGCs into GCFs. The resulting clustering network graphs were visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape 3.10.0 [<a href="#B30-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30-biology-14-00164">30</a>].</p></section><section id="sec2dot5-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Metabolites Analysis Using LC-MS/MS and GNPS</h3>
<p>A single colony of the <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> strain was inoculated into 50 mL of tryptic soy broth (TSB) medium in a 250 mL flask and incubated at 120 rpm and 37 °C for 48 h to obtain the seed culture. Then, 2 mL of the seed culture was transferred into a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 150 mL of ISP3 medium and fermented at 120 rpm and 37 °C for 5 days. The fermentation broth was filtered to remove the bacterial cells, and 10 mL of the broth was concentrated under reduced pressure. It was then dissolved sequentially in 1 mL each of methanol and water and adjusted to a final volume of 500 μL in methanol solution. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was conducted on an AB SCIEX TripleTOF 6600 system with the following ESI conditions: capillary voltage at 2500 V, source temperature at 120 °C, and dissociation temperature at 350 °C. Detection was performed in positive ion mode using MSE acquisition.</p>
<p>The raw LC-MS/MS data were converted to mzML format, and the MS2 spectra were analyzed using MSDIAL with parameters: sigma window value of 0.1, MS/MS abundance cutoff of 5, MS1 tolerance of 0.01, and MS2 tolerance of 0.0025, with default values for other parameters. The MS2 spectra, exported in mgf format, were analyzed on the FBMN platform within the GNPS ecosystem. Molecular networks were generated using the default parameters of the FBMN workflow. Further analysis and refinement of these networks were performed using Cytoscape 3.10.0 [<a href="#B30-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30-biology-14-00164">30</a>].</p></section><section id="sec2dot6-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Preliminary Assessment of Activity</h3>
<p>The method for obtaining crude extracts from the strain is consistent with the procedure described in <a href="#sec2dot5-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link">Section 2.5</a>. The activity of the crude extract was initially assessed using the filter paper agar diffusion method. Specifically, 20 μL of both methanol and aqueous- phase crude extracts were added onto a 6 mm diameter sterile filter paper disk. After methanol evaporation and drying, the disks were placed on media inoculated with 100 μL each of <em>Candida albicans</em> (ATCC 64550), <em>Escherichia coli</em> (ATCC 25922), and <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em> (ATCC 6538). Methanol was used as the blank control. Inhibition tests were conducted in triplicate, and the average diameter of the inhibition zones was calculated.</p></section><section id="sec2dot7-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.7. Isolation and Yield Determination of Compound</h3>
<p>A single colony of the <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> strain was inoculated into 50 mL of TSB medium in a 250 mL flask and incubated at 120 rpm at 37 °C for 48 h to obtain the seed culture. Then, 2 mL was inoculated into a 500 mL triangular flask containing 150 mL ISP3 medium, and fermented at 120 rpm and 37 °C for 5 days, with a total volume of 15 L. The upper layer of biomass was adsorbed onto D101 macroporous resin and subsequently eluted with pure water and 100% methanol. After concentrating the methanol eluate under reduced pressure and washing it three times with a small amount of methanol, a yellow solid insoluble in methanol was obtained, identified as compound <strong>12</strong>. The methanol-soluble fraction was further eluted with methanol on a Sephadex LH-20 column, resulting in four subfractions (S1–S4). Subfraction S2 underwent HPLC analysis using a ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18 column (250 × 9.4 mm, 5 µm, 4.0 mL/min) and was further purified by HPLC, eluting with 70% methanol/water to obtain compound <strong>35</strong>. Subfraction S3 was purified by HPLC and eluted with 45% methanol/water to yield compounds <strong>21</strong> and <strong>34</strong>. Subfraction S4 was purified by HPLC and eluted with 60% methanol/water to obtain compound <strong>36</strong>. NMR spectra were obtained using a 500 MHz NMR instrument (Bruker, Switzerland). <sup>1</sup>H-NMR, <sup>13</sup>C-NMR, HSQC, and HMBC NMR spectra of the compounds were analyzed to determine their structures.</p>
<p>The standard compounds were accurately weighed and diluted to the desired concentration using dimethyl sulfoxide. The standard solution was then further diluted to a series of concentrations. A Shim-pack GIST C18 column (4.6 mm × 25 cm, 5 µm) was used, with methanol and water as the mobile phases. The detection conditions were set with a methanol gradient from 10% to 100% over 0 to 40 min, at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. A 10 µL sample was injected into the column, and the peak areas of thiolutin at different concentrations were detected at 388 nm at 18.32 min. The standard curve was plotted using these peak areas and was utilized to analyze the yield of compounds in the crude extract of the strain.</p></section><section id="sec2dot8-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.8. Antibacterial Activity and MIC Assay</h3>
<p>The MIC for the pathogens were determined using the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute method. Compounds were prepared at defined concentrations using DMSO and determined against <em>Verticillium dahliae</em> ACCC 36211, <em>F. oxysporum</em> ACCC 31038, <em>Valsa pyri</em> WTZ-19, <em>Erwinia amylovor</em> TRM-03, and <em>Alternaria gaisen</em> TRM-09. Amphotericin B was used as a reference drug, DMSO was used as a blank control, and blank medium was used as a negative control.</p>
<p>A volume of 100 µL of a prepared compound solution was added to the first well of a 96-well plate, and the drug was serially diluted 2-fold to the final well. In the first ten wells, 100 µL of each pathogen should be added at a concentration of approximately 2 × 10<sup>6</sup> organisms/mL. while the 11th well contained only the blank medium, and the 12th well included the pathogen without thiolutin. After 48 h of incubation at 35 °C, the lowest concentration of each test compound at which a color change occurred was recorded as the primary MIC value.</p></section></section><section id="sec3-biology-14-00164"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results</h2>
<section id="sec3dot1-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Morphological and Cultural Characteristics</h3>
<p>Strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> was cultured in ISP4 medium at 37 °C for five days. During this period, yellow substrate mycelia were observed alongside white aerial mycelia on the surface. The colonies were flat and dry with filiform margins, displaying typical morphological characteristics of actinomycetes (<a href="#biology-14-00164-f001" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>A). Scanning electron microscopy revealed that the mycelia were sparsely branched and lacked transverse septa. The abundant aerial mycelia formed spore chains of varying lengths. The spores were ovoid and featured smooth spineless surfaces, whereas the spore chains exhibited a spiral shape (<a href="#biology-14-00164-f001" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>B).</p>

Genomic Features and Phylogeny</h3>
<p>Sequence analysis of the 16S rRNA gene of strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> indicated that the similarity (98.43%) was observed between <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> and strains <em>Streptomyces alkaliterrae</em> OF1<sup>T</sup> and <em>Streptomyces lycii</em> TRM 66187<sup>T</sup>, followed by <em>Streptomyces chumphonensis</em> KK1-2<sup>T</sup> and <em>Streptomyces gobiensis</em> 1-25<sup>T</sup> (98.15%). A multi-locus sequence analysis (MLSA) was conducted using 89 housekeeping genes (<a href="#biology-14-00164-f002" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>). The results demonstrated that strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> formed a stable branch with strain <em>S. chumphonensis</em> KK1-2<sup>T</sup>, achieving a bootstrap value of 100%. Moreover, the MLSA distance significantly exceeded the species-level threshold of 0.007, and the evolutionary trees constructed using the ML and ME methods yielded consistent results (<a href="#app1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link">Figures S1 and S2</a>).</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> genome has a size of 5.6 Mb, a (G+C) content of 74.5 mol%, and contains 4910 protein-coding genes. Whole-genome comparisons and phylogenomic analyses are recommended to provide greater taxonomic resolution when comparing potentially novel <em>Streptomyces</em> species with closely related taxa. In this study, a phylogenomic tree was generated, revealing that strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> was most closely related to <em>S. chumphonensis</em> KK1-2<sup>T</sup>, forming a distinct clade with high bootstrap support (100%). Although both strains evolved on the same branch, notable discrepancies were observed in genome size, protein content, number of gene clusters, and (G+C) mol%. In contrast, the remaining <em>Streptomyces</em> type strains, <em>S. alkaliterrae</em> OF1<sup>T</sup>, <em>S. lycii</em> TRM 66187<sup>T</sup>, and <em>S. gobiensis</em> 1-25<sup>T</sup>, were more distantly related to strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup>. These observations collectively supported the hypothesis that this strain represents a potentially novel species of <em>Streptomyces</em> (<a href="#biology-14-00164-f003" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>). <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> exhibited an average nucleotide identity (ANI) of 81.89% with its closest related strain <em>S. chumphonensis</em> KK1-2<sup>T</sup>. Additionally, the ANI values for all other potentially related strains remained significantly below the 95–96% threshold for species delimitation (<a href="#app1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link">Figure S6</a>).</p>

<p>Phenotypic differences were observed between <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> and its closely related phylogenetic neighbors, such as <em>S. chumphonensis</em> KK1-2<sup>T</sup>, S. <em>lycii</em> <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM66187" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM66187</a><sup>T</sup>, <em>S. gobiensis</em> 1-25<sup>T</sup>, <em>S. alkaliterrae</em> OF1<sup>T</sup><sub>,</sub> and <em>S. durbertensis</em> NEAU-S1GS20<sup>T</sup> (<a href="#biology-14-00164-t001" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). Unlike these neighbors, the growth tolerance temperature range of strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> is 15–45 °C, with a pH range of 6–10 and NaCl tolerance of 0–10%. The optimal growth conditions for strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> are 37 °C, 1% NaCl, and pH 7. The strain can utilize several sugars, including lactose, L-arabinose, sucrose, D-mannitol, glucose, D-fructose, and L-rhamnose, but cannot use raffinose, xylose, or meso-inositol as the sole carbon source. Positive results were obtained for urease, nitrate reduction, cellulose hydrolase, and starch hydrolase activity. Conversely, the results of gelatin liquefaction and catalase production tests were negative (<a href="#app1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>).</p>

Novel Desert Strain Harbor Diverse and Unique BGCs</h3>
<p>Strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> was predicted by antiSMASH to contain 25 BGCs encoding eight types of natural products: RiPPs (<em>n</em> = 3), terpenoids (<em>n</em> = 4), NRPS (<em>n</em> = 6), type I PKS (<em>n</em> = 1), other PKS (<em>n</em> = 2), and others (<em>n</em> = 9). Among these, there are PKS-NRPS hybrids (<em>n</em> = 2) and other hybrids (<em>n</em> = 8). According to BiG-SCAPE grouping, all 25 BGCs were distinct gene cluster families (GCFs), with only one NRPS type (GCF009) categorized as a GCF with reference BGCs in the MIBiG database (<a href="#biology-14-00164-f004" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>A). The remaining BGCs exhibited unique structures and lower similarity modules (5–20%), highlighting the distinctiveness of most BGCs within the <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> genome (<a href="#app1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link">Figure S9</a>). Further examination of GCF009, associated with BGC0001593 in the MIBiG database, revealed its association with Ficellomycin. However, the gene cluster corresponding to GCF009 in antiSMASH is BGC0001746, with the predicted product being s56-p1. For evolutionary analysis, 12 genes with the same function in the three BGCs, including BGC0001593 and BGC0001746, were linked in tandem. The remaining genes in BGC009 were used as exogenous sequences. The results indicated that BGC009 is closer to BGC0001746 (<a href="#biology-14-00164-f004" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>B). The biosynthetic pathways initiated by s56-1 and Ficellomycin reportedly originate from the same amino acid unit, with Ficellomycin forming s56-1 through the action of amide P450 enzymes [<a href="#B35-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B35-biology-14-00164">35</a>].</p>

Metabolomics Confirmed BGC Expression and Detected Unannotated NPs</h3>
<p>To explore the diversity of secondary metabolites, an untargeted mass spectrometry-based metabolomics approach was used to assess the metabolome of <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a> <sup>T</sup> in crude ISP3 medium extract samples. Through tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) spectral denoising and comparison with the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) online platform, 437 features were identified, resulting in 90 clusters. Among these, 36 networks contained fewer than 10 nodes, and 41 single nodes were removed from the network graph, suggesting the detection of many structurally distinct compounds (<a href="#biology-14-00164-f005" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>). Following extensive database searches, manual inspection, and other chemoinformatics predictions, 33 nodes were annotated into 13 categories (<a href="#app1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link">Figure S9</a>). Of these, four compounds were annotated by MS/MS features in the feature-based molecular network (FBMN) (<a href="#app1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link">Figure S10</a>), 28 compounds were annotated by other databases according MSDIAL (<a href="#app1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link">Figures S11–S29</a>), and 10 compounds were co-annotated by the FBMN and other databases (<a href="#app1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link">Figures S30–S39</a>). The majority of the molecular features did not match known compounds. The annotated compounds included Thiolutin, an organonitrogen compound with broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, two saponins with antifungal activity, and a range of peptides, indoles, and fatty acids (<a href="#biology-14-00164-f005" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>).</p>

Activity Assay of Crude Extract of Fermentation Broth</h3>
<p>The crude extract of strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> was evaluated for preliminary antimicrobial activity against fungi (<em>Candida albicans</em> ATCC 64550), gram-negative bacteria (<em>Escherichia coli</em> ATCC 25922), and gram-positive bacteria (<em>Staphylococcus aureus</em> ATCC 6538) using the filter paper agar diffusion method. The results indicated that the methanol extract exhibited activity against all three pathogens, demonstrating the most significant inhibition against <em>C. albicans</em> ATCC 64550 (26.1 ± 0.36 mm), followed by <em>E. coli</em> (24.0 ± 0.17 mm), and <em>S. aureus</em> (9.42 ± 0.40 mm) (<a href="#app1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link">Table S3</a>), while the aqueous extract showed no activity against any of the tested pathogens (<a href="#app1-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link">Figure S40</a>). Methanol served as the control and exhibited no antimicrobial activity.</p></section><section id="sec3dot7-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.7. Isolation and Structure Identification</h3>

Discussion</h2>
<section id="sec4dot1-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.1. Deserts Are a Rich Source of Novel Actinomycetes</h3>
<p>Based on genotypic, phenotypic, physiological, biochemical, and chemical characteristics, it is evident that strain <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> is a novel species in the genus <em>Streptomyces</em>, which is herein named <em>Streptomyces xanthus</em>. Since the first description of <em>Streptomyces</em> in 1943 [<a href="#B9-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-biology-14-00164">9</a>], numerous species have been identified, making it one of the most diverse genera. Nevertheless, new <em>Streptomyces</em> species continue to be identified. Over the past decade, approximately 20 novel <em>Streptomyces</em> species have been discovered in deserts [<a href="#B7-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-biology-14-00164">7</a>]. Most desert-derived <em>Streptomyces</em> exhibit anti-cancer, anti-tumor, anti-viral, anti-oxidant and anti-bacterial properties [<a href="#B40-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B40-biology-14-00164">40</a>,<a href="#B41-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B41-biology-14-00164">41</a>]. Desert <em>Streptomyces</em> also demonstrate a diverse array of plant growth-promoting and biocontrol properties [<a href="#B42-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42-biology-14-00164">42</a>,<a href="#B43-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B43-biology-14-00164">43</a>], enhance plant resistance to environmental stress [<a href="#B44-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B44-biology-14-00164">44</a>], and mitigate abiotic stress [<a href="#B42-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42-biology-14-00164">42</a>]. Desert <em>Streptomyces</em> have been used in various industrial applications. For instance, <em>Streptomyces fragilis</em> DA7-7 synthesizes a thermostable α-amylase with commercial potential in industries such as paper, food, starch saccharification, and pharmaceuticals [<a href="#B45-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B45-biology-14-00164">45</a>]. <em>Streptomyces leeuwenhoekii</em> C34 possesses two novel oxidative enzymes with potential industrial applications [<a href="#B46-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B46-biology-14-00164">46</a>]. This evidence supports the claim that deserts are a rich source of novel actinomycetes. This reinforces the notion that <em>Streptomyces</em> species identified in deserts can play a pivotal role in addressing challenges, such as drug resistance, scarcity of industrial raw materials, and environmental pollution.</p></section><section id="sec4dot2-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.2. Isolation and High Production of the Active Thiolutin</h3>
<p>Compound <strong>12</strong>, Thiolutin, belongs to the dithiopyrrolidone class with RNAase inhibitor activity and is renowned for its broad-spectrum antibiotic efficacy against numerous gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, including <em>Mycobacterium bovis</em>, as well as its pronounced anti-tumor activity [<a href="#B47-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B47-biology-14-00164">47</a>]. Other members of this chemical class include Holomycin and Aureothricin. In this study, we discovered the novel biological activity of Thiolutin, which exhibited significant efficacy against several local plant pathogens, including <em>Verticillium dahliae</em>, <em>Fusarium oxysporum</em>, <em>Venturia pyri</em>, <em>Erwinia amylovora</em> (the causative agent of fire blight in pears), and <em>Alternaria gaisen</em> (responsible for black spot in pears). This is the first reported isolation of thiolutin from a desert-origin actinomycete.</p>
<p>Owing to significant biological properties, substantial research has been devoted to the biosynthesis of thiolutin. Although crucial biosynthetic genes have been identified [<a href="#B48-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B48-biology-14-00164">48</a>], the synthesis of these compounds remains an active area of research [<a href="#B49-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B49-biology-14-00164">49</a>]. A patent reported the discovery of thiolutin; however, its yield was extremely low, with only 1.8 mg isolated from 200 L of fermentation broth. Additionally, the separation medium comprises five components, necessitating complex separation procedures [<a href="#B50-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B50-biology-14-00164">50</a>]. In this study, thiolutin was prepared from the fermentation broth of an oat culture medium in a single step, yielding 270 mg/L of thiolutin, the highest titer reported to date. The first report on the efficacy of thiolutin was published in 1958 [<a href="#B51-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B51-biology-14-00164">51</a>], highlighting its use in spraying apple trees to control apple tree wilt caused by <em>E. amylovora</em>. In 2017, Merrouche identified thiolutin in <em>Saccharothrix algeriensis</em> and demonstrated its effectiveness in reducing <em>Fusarium oxysporum</em> [<a href="#B52-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B52-biology-14-00164">52</a>]. Thiolutin holds significant potential for the prevention and control of plant diseases and bacterial susceptibility. In our study, the high yield and simplicity of the isolation method offer a viable economic foundation for pilot-scale production of thiolutin, facilitating its future design and development as a new generation of drugs targeting RNA polymerase.</p>
<p>Dithiopyrrolidones are a class of natural antibiotics with RNase inhibitory activity and are characterized by significant antibacterial, antifungal, and anticancer properties. Their unique biosynthetic and regulatory mechanisms suggest substantial potential applications, with thiolutin as a notable representative compound [<a href="#B48-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B48-biology-14-00164">48</a>]. In 1948, Umezawa et al. isolated the first dithiopyrrolidone antibiotic, aureothricin, from <em>Streptomyces</em> sp. 26A-Chrysomycin [<a href="#B53-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B53-biology-14-00164">53</a>]. Two years later, thiolutin was isolated from the metabolites of <em>Streptomyces albus</em> [<a href="#B54-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B54-biology-14-00164">54</a>]. Thiolutin is a typical representative of the <em>N</em>-methyl-<em>N</em>-acylpyrrothine class of dithiopyrrolidones. Therefore, this class of compounds is also known as thiolutin analogs [<a href="#B48-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B48-biology-14-00164">48</a>]. Subsequently, this class of compounds attracted the attention of numerous research groups. This is not only because of their unique structure and outstanding antibacterial and anti-fungal activities but also because of their unique biosynthetic and regulatory mechanisms. Many members of this family exhibit strong broad-spectrum resistance with good inhibitory activity against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, tumors, yeasts, fungi, and even parasites [<a href="#B55-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B55-biology-14-00164">55</a>]. These compounds are the precursors for the design of a new generation of RNA polymerase-related drugs. Yakushiji et al. recently developed a new series of RNA polymerase inhibitors by doping holomycin into several myxopyronine backbones with good antimicrobial activity [<a href="#B56-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B56-biology-14-00164">56</a>], implying that the use of pyrrolidines as building blocks to form heterodimeric drugs is an important direction for the development of new drugs.</p></section><section id="sec4dot3-biology-14-00164"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.3. Isolation and Activity of Other Compounds</h3>
<p>Compounds <strong>21</strong> and <strong>35</strong> are phthalate ester (PAE) compounds. DBP and DEHP, commonly used as plasticizers and softeners in industry, are often released into the environment through industrial wastewater. This is not the first report of such compounds being isolated from microorganisms. The inhibitory effects of DBP and DEHP on pathogenic fungi and bacteria have been documented, with DBP showing greater inhibition than DEHP. DBP was among the top 10 metabolites of <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup> in terms of ionic abundance. Studies have shown that the biosynthesis of these compounds may originate from the glucose substrate oxalic acid pathway [<a href="#B57-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B57-biology-14-00164">57</a>]. Key enzymes of this pathway were found in tandem in the genome of <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/TRM70308" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TRM70308</a><sup>T</sup>, suggesting that this strain can synthesize DBP, thereby indirectly confirming that these compounds are derived from the strain rather than the medium.</p>
<p>Compound <strong>34</strong> belongs to a new class of pyrazine compounds, primarily isolated from plants, known for their hypoglycemic activity [<a href="#B38-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38-biology-14-00164">38</a>]. This is the first instance of such compounds being isolated from a microorganism. The biosynthetic pathway of these compounds remains unreported. Compound <strong>36</strong>, <em>N</em>-acetyltryptamine, has been shown to act as a partial agonist of retinal melatonin receptors. It likely originates from the tryptophan pathway, which degrades over 95% of tryptophan into various bioactive compounds [<a href="#B39-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B39-biology-14-00164">39</a>].</p>
<p>The top 10 compounds by ion abundance in the metabolome analysis were grouped into six types, three of which were not annotated, while the remaining eight were grouped into five types. Compounds of the thiolutin type were isolated, and two compounds of the phthalic acid group were also isolated. The rest were not isolated, likely due to the need for more diverse separation methods. For instance, compound <strong>1</strong>, presumed to lack UV absorption, should be separated by TLC color development. These N-containing compounds can be detected using bismuth potassium iodide. As another example, s56-1, predicted to be acid-base unstable, requires pH adjustment to neutral during purification [<a href="#B58-biology-14-00164" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B58-biology-14-00164">58</a>]. Furthermore, enhancing genome sequencing depth and refining the metabolic profiling database, in conjunction with transcriptome analysis, will facilitate more precise metabolomic data analysis while determining gene expression levels.</p></section></section><section id="sec5-biology-14-00164"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">5. Conclusions</h2>
<p>In this study, we identified a novel strain, <em>Streptomyces xanthus</em>, which exhibited unique genomic and metabolic profiles. Genomic and metabolomic analyses demonstrated that this strain is a source of diverse bioactive compounds. One novel compound, aconicarpyrazine C, and four known compounds were isolated from its metabolites, with all being reported for the first time from desert-derived actinomycetes. Among these, thiolutin exhibited strong activity against plant pathogenic fungi, with a production yield of 270 mg/L. These findings highlight not only the importance of desert environments in discovering microbial species and bioactive compounds, but also the potential of <em>S. xanthus</em> for pilot-scale thiolutin production. Continued exploration of desert actinomycetes may uncover additional novel metabolites and provide a strong foundation for developing innovative solutions to combat plant diseases and address challenges in agriculture and medicine.</p></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
