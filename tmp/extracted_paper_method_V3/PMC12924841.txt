</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="Par1">Electromyograms (EMGs) of the orbicularis oculi muscle are commonly used to assess the blink reflex, which provides clinically important information. However, mechanomyograms (MMGs) may serve as a promising alternative. This study aimed to evaluate the utility of MMGs in assessing orbicularis oculi responses to stimulation. Electrical stimulation was applied to the facial nerve axons of 25 healthy adult participants, and MMGs were recorded using an accelerometer. The reliability of amplitude-based parameters (<em>A</em><sub><em>max</em></sub> and <em>A</em><sub><em>p−p</em></sub>) and time-based parameters (<em>T</em><sub><em>rise</em></sub>, <em>T</em><sub><em>zero</em></sub>, <em>T</em><sub><em>fall</em></sub>, and <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub>) characterizing MMG waveforms was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), standard error of measurement (SEM), and coefficient of variation (CV). Additionally, the amplitude and temporal characteristics of MMGs evoked by varying stimulus intensities were compared with simultaneously recorded EMGs. All parameters, except <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub>, showed high relative reliability (95% confidence interval of ICC of &gt; 0.75) and high absolute reliability (%SEM and CV &lt; 10%). MMG amplitudes (<em>A</em><sub><em>max</em></sub> and <em>A</em><sub><em>p−p</em></sub>) and EMG amplitudes (<em>V</em><sub><em>max</em></sub> and <em>V</em><sub><em>p−p</em></sub>) increased linearly with stimulus intensity. A strong correlation was found between the amplitudes of both signals (<em>V</em><sub><em>max</em></sub> vs. <em>A</em><sub><em>max</em></sub>: <em>r</em> = 0.932, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001; <em>V</em><sub><em>p−p</em></sub> vs. <em>A</em><sub><em>p−p</em></sub>: <em>r</em> = 0.937, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001). The first positive MMG peak consistently appeared at a fixed interval (5.52 ± 2.10 ms) after the EMG peak, irrespective of stimulus level. These findings suggest that MMG amplitude and latency (stimulus-to-peak time) are reliable indicators of orbicularis oculi muscle activity and can be effectively used alongside EMG in clinical and research settings.</p>
<p id="Par2">Communicated by Bill Yates.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Accelerometer, Mechanomyogram, Orbicularis oculi muscle, Reliability, Stimulus response</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par3">The orbicularis oculi muscle is an essential structure frequently selected for evaluation in clinical practice. For instance, it is employed in assessing the normality of reflex pathways in patients with schizophrenia via the prepulse inhibition response of the eyeblink reflex (Edwards et al. <a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">2008</a>; Meincke et al. <a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">1999</a>; Taiminen et al. <a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">2000</a>) as well as in the diagnosis and monitoring of facial palsy (Kanaya et al. <a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">2009</a>; On et al. <a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">2007</a>; Takahashi et al. <a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">2017</a>) and ocular myasthenia gravis (Matsuoka and Katsuda <a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">1968</a>; Oey et al. <a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">1993</a>; Valls-Canals et al. <a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">2003</a>) through electrodiagnostic techniques such as electroneurography and single-fiber electromyography. The active state of the orbicularis oculi is often identified using electromyograms (EMGs) as a key indicator. Recently, Bayasgalan et al. (<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">2024</a>) reported that mechanomyograms (MMGs) could serve as a promising alternative for evaluating the blink reflex. MMGs offer several advantages over EMGs, including faster and simpler recordings and the absence of artifacts from electrical stimulation.</p>
<p id="Par4">MMGs are noninvasive recordings of surface vibrations generated by the contractile activity of muscle fibers and contain sufficient data to assess skeletal muscle activity (Orizio <a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">2004</a>). While EMG reflects the electrical aspect of muscle contraction, MMG captures its mechanical counterpart (Orizio <a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">1993</a>; Stokes and Cooper <a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">1992</a>). Moreover, MMG is known to reveal information not discernible from surface EMG—such as changes at the motor unit level (Orizio <a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">2004</a>)—and its combination with EMG can provide insights into muscle electromechanical efficiency (Cè et al. <a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">2017</a>). Due to its mode of generation, MMG is particularly advantageous for evaluating muscles that operate without joints between their origin and insertion. Nonetheless, studies on MMG of the orbicularis oculi muscle remain limited.</p>
<p id="Par5">Various types of transducers have been used to detect skin surface microvibrations resulting from muscle fiber contraction, including piezoelectric contact sensors (Barry <a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">1991</a>; Orizio et al. <a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">1994</a>), condenser microphones (Stokes and Dalton <a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">1991</a>; Watakabe et al. <a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">2001</a>), accelerometers (Akataki et al. <a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1999</a>; Orizio et al. <a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">1996</a>; Watakabe et al. <a href="#CR43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR43">2003</a>), and digital displacement transducers (Paravlić et al. <a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">2017</a>; Tous-Fajardo et al. <a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">2010</a>). Signals recorded by these different transducer types may reflect distinct physical quantities (e.g., displacement, velocity, or acceleration) (Orizio <a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">2004</a>; Watakabe et al. <a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">2001</a>, <a href="#CR43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR43">2003</a>). However, signals obtained using accelerometers can be directly compared across studies (Orizio <a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">2004</a>), because, unlike other transducers, they are expressed in standard physical units (m s<sup>− 2</sup>) rather than in device-dependent units. Additionally, accelerometers are lightweight and compact and can detect surface microvibrations simply by being attached to the skin with double-sided tape, making them especially suitable for examining small muscles.</p>
<p id="Par6">The aim of this study was to evaluate the usefulness of accelerometer-derived MMG for assessing electrically evoked activity of the orbicularis oculi muscle. To achieve this, the relative and absolute reliabilities of MMGs elicited by electrical stimulation of facial nerve axons directly innervating the orbicularis oculi were investigated. Changes in MMG responses with varying stimulation intensities were also compared to those of EMGs.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Participants</h3>
<p id="Par7">A total of 25 healthy adults (20 males and 5 females; age, 20.1 ± 0.6 years; height, 169.5 ± 6.2 cm; body mass, 63.6 ± 11.4 kg; mean ± standard deviation) with no history of ophthalmological or neuromuscular disorders participated in this study. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee of Osaka Electro-Communication University approved the experimental protocol (Approval number: 22-013; Approval date: 1 March 2023). All participants were fully informed of the approved procedures and provided written informed consent prior to the experiment.</p></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Experimental design</h3>
<p id="Par8">All measurements were conducted with participants in the right semi-lateral decubitus position. To account for MMG changes influenced by skin temperature (Mito et al. <a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">2007</a>), the room temperature was maintained between 24 and 26 °C during all measurements. Sessions involving repetitive supramaximal stimulation for reliability assessment and those using varying stimulation intensities to compare MMG and EMG were performed on separate days. To address crosstalk from facial nerve–innervated muscles other than the orbicularis oculi, cases showing clear activity in these muscles were excluded from the analysis.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Stimulation protocol</h3>
<p id="Par9">To elicit orbicularis oculi twitches, electrical stimulation was delivered to the facial nerve through the participant’s left stylomastoid foramen near the temporal bone using a surface-stimulating electrode (NM-420 S, Nihon Kohden Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The stimulation signal used in both sessions consisted of a single square wave with a duration of 0.2 ms, generated by an electromyography/evoked potential measuring system (MEB-2200, Nihon Kohden Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). To minimize post-activation depression and fatigue, a rest interval of at least 10 s was maintained between successive stimuli in each session (Palmieri et al. <a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">2004</a>; Paravlić et al. <a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">2017</a>).</p>
<p id="Par10">The repetitive supramaximal stimulation session was conducted with all participants. First, the maximum stimulus intensity for each participant was determined as the current at which the evoked EMG amplitude no longer increased with further increments in stimulus intensity. The maximum stimulation intensities determined here ranged from 33.0 to 79.0 mA. Subsequently, twitch contractions of the orbicularis oculi muscle were elicited 10 times per participant at 120% of the maximum stimulus intensity.</p>
<p id="Par11">The intensity-varying stimulation session was conducted with 20 participants who were available for additional testing. First, the maximum stimulation intensity for each participant was determined using the same procedure as in the repetitive supramaximal stimulation session. Next, a single twitch contraction at the maximum stimulus intensity (100% level) was elicited. Then, electrical stimulation at various intensities ranging from 120 to 30% was applied once at each level. The stimulus intensity step was approximately 3 mA.</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Recording</h3>
<p id="Par12">MMG was recorded using a single-axis compact accelerometer (MP110-10-101, a rectangular solid; width, 9 mm; depth, 9 mm; height, 5 mm; 0.8 g; Medi Sens Inc., Tokyo, Japan) attached to the left lower eyelid using double-sided tape (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>). Throughout all experiments, the measurement axis of the accelerometer coincided with the direction normal to the skin surface, and the acceleration directed outward from the skin was recorded as positive. Simultaneously, surface EMG was recorded using two disposable Ag/AgCl gel electrodes (NM-31, Nihon Kohden Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) placed on the left lower eyelid and the nasion. The ground electrode was positioned on the mental protuberance. Electrode placement was determined based on the location where surface EMG, evoked at the same stimulation intensity, showed the highest amplitude (Takahashi et al. <a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">2017</a>). The MMG and EMG signals were amplified and filtered using their respective amplifiers (MPS110, Medi Sens Inc., Tokyo, Japan; bandpass filter: 0.1–1000 Hz; MEB-2200, Nihon Kohden Corporation, Tokyo, Japan; bandpass filter: 0.1–2000 Hz). The signals were then digitized and stored on a personal computer via an A/D converter data acquisition system (ML880, ADInstruments, Dunedin, New Zealand) at a sampling frequency of 10 kHz.</p>

<section id="Sec8"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Extracting parameters</h4>
<p id="Par14">Following a previous study (Than et al. <a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">2018</a>), two amplitude-based parameters (<em>A</em><sub><em>max</em></sub>, <em>A</em><sub><em>p−p</em></sub>) and four time-based parameters (<em>T</em><sub><em>rise</em></sub>, <em>T</em><sub><em>zero</em></sub>, <em>T</em><sub><em>fall</em></sub>, <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub>) characterizing the MMG waveform were extracted (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>). These parameters have the following significance during the contraction and relaxation phases of the twitch response: at the onset of the contraction phase, a steep increase in twitch force is observed. The maximum positive change in the rising gradient is defined as <em>A</em><sub><em>max</em></sub>, and the time required to reach this point is <em>T</em><sub><em>rise</em></sub>. Toward the end of the contraction phase, the rising gradient of the twitch force becomes more gradual as it approaches the peak, and the maximum negative change in the gradient is defined as <em>A</em><sub><em>min</em></sub>. During the contraction phase, <em>T</em><sub><em>zero</em></sub> represents the time from the maximum positive change in the rising gradient to the point at which the gradient becomes negative, and <em>T</em><sub><em>fall</em></sub> represents the time required to reach the maximum negative change. And, <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub> is the time required for the twitch force to rise, pass its peak, and then decline, extending from the end of the contraction phase into the relaxation phase of the twitch response.</p>

<p id="Par21">Here, the mean data was computed from <em>k</em> observations (<em>k</em> = 2,3,…, <em>K</em>), randomly selected from among the <em>K</em> repeated measurements. <p id="Par22">To evaluate correlations between any two parameters, both Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (<em>r</em><sub>s</sub>) and Pearson’s correlation coefficient (<em>r</em>) were calculated. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA 11.1 (StataCorp LLC., Texas, USA).</p></section><section id="Sec10"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Spectral analysis</h4>
<p id="Par23">The power spectrum of the MMG signal was calculated using a fast Fourier transform algorithm. The analysis window (data length = 0.2048 s, 2048 points) was defined by extracting the segment from − 0.8 ms (i.e., 0.8 ms before the stimulus) to 50.4 ms of the MMG recording. A Tukey window (window width = 0.0512 s, 512 points) was applied, and zero was assigned to the remaining data points. The mean power frequency (MPF) was then calculated from the 0–200 Hz frequency band of the resulting spectrum.</p></section></section></section><section id="Sec11"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<p id="Par24">As shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>, the MMG waveform of the orbicularis oculi muscle evoked by supramaximal intensity stimulation consistently exhibited an initial positive wave with the highest peak amplitude, followed by a negative wave in all participants. However, the characteristics of the decaying oscillations following the negative wave varied among individuals.</p>
<p id="Par25">The results of the reliability indices for each parameter characterizing the MMG waveform are summarized in Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>. In terms of absolute reliability, %SEM values were below 9% for all parameters except for <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub>, which exceeded 10% substantially. The CVs were ≤ 6.20% for all parameters except <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub>, where they also exceeded 10%. Regarding relative reliability, the 95% CIs of ICC(1,1) for the amplitude-derived parameters (<em>A</em><sub><em>max</em></sub> and <em>A</em><sub><em>p−p</em></sub>) clearly surpassed 0.90. For the time-derived parameters, the 95% CIs of ICC(1,1) ranged from 0.75 to 1.00 for <em>T</em><sub><em>rise</em></sub>, <em>T</em><sub><em>zero</em></sub>, and <em>T</em><sub><em>fall</em></sub>, whereas the CI for <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub> was below 0.75. However, the 95% CIs of ICC(1,<em>k</em>) for <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub> increased with the number of averaged trials (<em>k</em>), exceeding 0.75 when <em>k</em> &gt; 5 (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>).</p>

<p id="Par37">The evoked MMGs of the orbicularis oculi muscle exhibited waveforms resembling underdamped oscillations, beginning with a positive wave of maximum amplitude (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>). This pattern is consistent with previously reported MMG waveforms of limb muscles during twitch contractions (Bichler <a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">2000</a>; Orizio et al. <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">1999</a>). According to Bichler (<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">2000</a>), the initial and largest amplitude fluctuations in MMG recordings occur during the contraction phase of a twitch and are associated with force generation in the muscle fibers. The report also noted that the slower oscillations observed during the subsequent relaxation phase—when twitch tension decreases—were attributed to the characteristics of the transducer (a piezoelectric microphone) used in those measurements. However, that study employed paraffin oil to fill the space between the microphone and the muscle surface, which differs from our method, where accelerometers were directly attached to the skin over the muscle. Because accelerometers detect object acceleration, our recordings likely reflect the fluctuating acceleration of the skin surface. It is generally understood that the twitch tension behavior during the relaxation phase is governed by the passive mechanical properties of muscle. In mathematical models of skeletal muscle, tension decay during relaxation is represented by a viscoelastic element composed of muscle fibers and surrounding connective tissue (Lemos et al. <a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">2008</a>; Phillips et al. <a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">2004</a>). Given the relationship between geometric changes in muscle fibers and vibrations on the skin surface, the oscillations observed after the negative wave in accelerometer-recorded signals are likely to reflect these passive viscoelastic properties of both the muscle and the skin—rather than being artifacts of the transducer itself. Moreover, the viscoelastic properties of the skin may vary because of individual differences, such as skin stiffness and gender, as well as various external factors, which may explain the variability in MMG waveforms among participants in the present results.</p>
<p id="Par38">This study examined the reliability of six parameters characterizing the initial oscillations of the evoked MMG of the orbicularis oculi muscle under supramaximal stimulation. According to previous reports (Cè et al. <a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">2013</a>; Ng et al. <a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">2017</a>), acceptable thresholds for reliability are %SEM and CV values below 10%. Based on their criteria, the %SEM and CV results suggest that all parameters—except <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub>—demonstrate sufficient absolute reliability. The ICC(1,1) values indicate that all parameters except <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub> were rated as excellent or good to excellent according to established guidelines, reflecting high relative reliability. The lower reliability of <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub> may be attributed to the fact that, among the six parameters, only <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub> is associated with the relaxation phase of the twitch contraction. In other words, during the relaxation phase, skin vibration is more readily influenced by individual differences and various external factors. In addition, unavoidable crosstalk from facial nerve–innervated muscles other than the orbicularis oculi may occur. The crosstalk that appears in the MMG waveform with a time delay proportional to the distance between these muscles and the accelerometer may further reduce <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub> reliability. Nevertheless, as shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>, <em>T</em><sub><em>rec</em></sub> achieves sufficiently high relative reliability (good to excellent) when averaged over five or more repeated measurements.</p>
<p id="Par39">EMG amplitude has frequently been used to assess the intensity of the orbicularis oculi muscle response (Unal et al. <a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">2016</a>; Valls-Solé et al. <a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">1997</a>). The amplitude of evoked EMG is generally known to increase proportionally with stimulus intensity, and our results confirmed this linear relationship in the orbicularis oculi muscle up to the 100% stimulus level (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>b and c. Conversely, based on known MMG characteristics, the rate of increase in evoked MMG amplitude is expected to change if the type of recruited muscle fiber shifts with increasing stimulus intensity. Specifically, MMG amplitude generated by slow-twitch (ST) fibers is lower than that from fast-twitch (FT) fibers, meaning that the amplitude increase per additional active fiber is also small (Marchetti et al. <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">1992</a>). During twitch contractions elicited by motor nerve stimulation, FT fibers are recruited first—contrary to the “size principle”—followed by ST fibers as stimulus intensity rises (Baratta et al. <a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">1989</a>). Consequently, the rate of MMG amplitude increase might be expected to slow beyond a certain stimulus level. However, the present results show that, similar to EMG, MMG amplitude also increases linearly with stimulus intensity (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>e and f). In addition, although the upward slope of the MMG amplitude relative to the EMG amplitude tended to become slightly more gradual at higher stimulation levels, a strong correlation was generally observed between the two (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>). These findings suggest that the peak amplitude of MMG, like EMG, can serve as a reliable indicator of the response intensity of the orbicularis oculi muscle. The linear increase in MMG amplitude may be attributed to the muscle’s structural characteristics. According to previous anatomical studies (Hwang et al. <a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">2011</a>; Johnson et al. <a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">1973</a>), FT fibers constitute more than 80–90% of the orbicularis oculi muscle, which could account for the observed linearity in MMG response. MMG is known to reflect the activity of muscle fibers located closer to the surface more strongly than that of deeper fibers (Orizio <a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">2004</a>). As a result, the activity of ST fibers—which are fewer in number and typically located deeper within the muscle—may have little to no influence on the evoked MMG of the orbicularis oculi. This interpretation is supported by our spectral analysis results (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>). Specifically, since MMG signals from FT fibers are known to contain higher frequency components than those from ST fibers (Orizio <a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">1993</a>), any change in the type of active muscle fibers should be reflected in the spectral shape, bandwidth, or mean frequency. However, our findings showed no changes in these spectral characteristics across different stimulus levels, suggesting no apparent influence of ST fiber recruitment on the MMG. Incidentally, the data shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>c become more scattered at lower stimulus levels, which is likely attributable to unavoidable crosstalk from facial nerve–innervated muscles other than orbicularis oculi. In other words, such crosstalk may have substantially reduced the signal-to-noise ratio of the small-amplitude MMGs recorded at low stimulus levels, resulting in poor data reproducibility.</p>
<p id="Par40">Our results showed that the first positive peak of the MMG occurred clearly later than that of the EMG (Fig. <a href="#Fig7" class="usa-link">7</a>). The delay time (<em>T</em><sub><em>del</em></sub>) remained nearly constant at approximately 5.5 ms, regardless of stimulus level. Incidentally, variability in the data increased at lower stimulus levels, which is likely attributable to the effect of small amounts of crosstalk on both EMG and MMG signals. According to a previous study (Sun et al. <a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">1996</a>), there is a delay of about 10 ms between the action potential and the onset of force development due to the excitation-contraction coupling process. MMGs recorded with laser displacement meters—which reflect skin displacement—have been reported to precede twitch tension due to differences in the propagation pathway (Orizio et al. <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">1999</a>). From a mathematical perspective, skin acceleration is expected to reach its maximum shortly after the onset of skin displacement. Therefore, the consistent time lag (<em>T</em><sub><em>del</em></sub>) between MMG and EMG, independent of stimulus intensity and averaging about 5.5 ms, may be directly related to the muscle’s force generation process.</p>
<p id="Par41">Traditionally, the time from stimulus to the peak EMG response is used as a latency marker in reflex pathway assessments (Unal et al. <a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">2016</a>; Valls-Solé et al. <a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">1997</a>). Thus, our findings suggest that the time from stimulus to the peak MMG response can also serve as a suitable indicator for reflex pathway evaluation, despite differences in absolute values. Moreover, analysis of <em>T</em><sub><em>del</em></sub> may provide additional insights into changes in the muscle force generation process.</p>
<p id="Par42">We selected a lightweight single-axis accelerometer to detect skin vibrations caused by muscle fiber activity. Nevertheless, even when using a three-axis accelerometer, skin vibration represents a scalar projection of the three-dimensional spatial movement of muscle fibers on the direction normal to the skin surface. Conversely, external pressure applied to the skin during sensor attachment, including effects related to sensor mass, can affect skin vibration. For accelerometers, no external pressure is required for attachment, and this effect can be ignored; however, sensor mass may affect the amplitude of skin acceleration. Nonetheless, the frequency response characteristics of an accelerometer theoretically remain unchanged during measurement. Accordingly, it can be assumed that the mass of the accelerometer does not exert a significant effect on the waveform of the recorded signal.</p></section><section id="Sec13"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="Par43">The mechanomyographic twitch response of the orbicularis oculi muscle, as detected by accelerometry, demonstrated sufficiently high absolute and relative reliability. Furthermore, the amplitude and latency of the MMG’s first positive peak—similar to conventional EMG—were shown to be valid indicators for assessing the intensity of the orbicularis oculi muscle response and the integrity of reflex pathways.</p></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgements</h2>
