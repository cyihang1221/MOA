</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ab0005"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>To overcome the subjectivity of traditional bacon authentication, this study developed an objective discrimination framework integrating LC–MS/MS-based metabolomics with machine learning. Metabolomic profiling of bacon samples from pork belly (LRW) and pork rump (LRT) identified 100 differential metabolites common to both groups. The Kruskal–Wallis H test (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) identified 75 significant features, which were further reduced to 22 key metabolites through random forest–based feature selection and low-variance filtering. Subsequently, a multidimensional evaluation of these 22 key metabolites (VIF &lt; 75, label correlation &gt;85%, and information gain = 1) highlighted four candidate discriminatory metabolites, including 6-hydroxyoctanoylcarnitine characterizing LRT, and dimethylethanolamine, xanthine, and ethyl hydrogen fumarate associated with LRW. Bidirectional validation using RF, KNN, SVM, and FNN models demonstrated robust discriminatory performance. This framework provides a basis for optimizing bacon production and offers a generalizable paradigm for data-driven biomarker discovery in meat products.</p>

<p id="p0030">Bacon, as a typical traditional meat product, is highly valued by consumers for its distinctive flavor profile and relatively long shelf life (<a href="#bb0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0030">Du et al., 2021</a>; <a href="#bb0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0170">Ruan et al., 2023</a>). With growing consumer demand and increasing health awareness, stricter requirements are being placed on bacon processing, quality control, and safety evaluation. Traditional sensory evaluation provides a comprehensive assessment based on multiple indicators, including visual inspection of color and surface appearance, olfactory assessment, taste perception, and tactile assessment of textural and elasticity (<a href="#bb0115" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0115">Liu et al., 2025</a>; <a href="#bb0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0170">Ruan et al., 2023</a>). Although this method is widely applied, evaluation results are often influenced by subjectivity (e.g., inter-evaluator variability) and environmental conditions (e.g., lighting, temperature, and humidity) (<a href="#bb0140" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0140">Modesti et al., 2022</a>; <a href="#bb0180" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0180">Song et al., 2025</a>), which may, to some extent, hinder innovation in bacon production processes and the establishment of objective quality-control and evaluation systems.</p>
<p id="p0035">Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), particularly when operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, provides picogram-level sensitivity and high selectivity, enabling the quantification of trace metabolites, isomer discrimination, and mitigation of complex matrix interferences (<a href="#bb0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0045">Emwas, 2015</a>; <a href="#bb0175" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0175">Shao et al., 2019</a>). In recent years, LC–MS/MS has been employed for meat species identification (<a href="#bb0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0035">Duft et al., 2025</a>), spoilage detection (<a href="#bb0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0110">Liao et al., 2022</a>), and elucidation of mechanisms underlying flavor compound formation (<a href="#bb0190" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0190">Sun et al., 2024</a>). Overall, LC–MS/MS represents a powerful tool for comprehensive meat quality evaluation and flavor characterization; however, the high dimensionality and nonlinearity of the resulting datasets pose challenges for conventional statistical analyses (<a href="#bb0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0130">Luo et al., 2025</a>).</p>
<p id="p0040">Machine learning algorithms provide new approaches for in-depth exploration of metabolomics data owing to their outstanding performance in handling complex datasets (<a href="#bb0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0005">Cai et al., 2024</a>). Currently, machine learning has been widely applied in the food sector to optimize production processes, improve product quality, and support nutritional analyses (<a href="#bb0220" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0220">Yang et al., 2025</a>). The integration of metabolomics and genomics with machine learning enables precise regulation of food flavor profiles and accurate classification of food products by variety or processing stage, thereby contributing to food quality and safety assurance (<a href="#bb0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0095">Li et al., 2025a</a>). <a href="#bb0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0100">Li et al. (2025b)</a> developed a geographical origin discrimination model for tangerine peel using machine learning algorithms with GC and MIR data. <a href="#bb0260" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0260">Zhou et al. (2025)</a> achieved organic milk authentication and geographical origin identification through integrated lipid and peptide analyses using MALDI-TOF MS combined with machine learning. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the significant potential of integrating machine learning with omics data, providing novel approaches for intelligent food quality classification and monitoring.</p>
<p id="p0045">Based on the research background described above, this study integrates untargeted metabolomics with machine learning techniques to investigate the dynamic changes in non-volatile metabolites in the belly and rump cuts of Zhenba bacon during the smoking process. Using LC–MS/MS, the study systematically analyzes differential metabolites, elucidates key metabolic pathways with the KEGG database (KEGG-based pathway analysis), and constructs a bacon-cut discrimination model based on Random Forest (RF), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) algorithms. This research not only helps to reveal the formation of traditional bacon flavor at the metabolic level but also introduces a machine-learning-based intelligent classification method for bacon products, providing theoretical support and a methodological reference for modernized production and precise quality control of traditional foods.</p></section><section id="s0010"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="s0015"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Sample preparation</h3>
<p id="p0050">The Zhenba bacon used in this study was provided by Shaanxi Zhenhong Shule Food Technology Development Co., Ltd. The raw materials were strictly selected from the belly and rump cuts of Hanjiang black pigs aged 8 to 12 months. According to the manufacturer, the preparation process involved the following steps: First, fresh meat was cut into uniformly sized pieces, thoroughly cleaned to remove surface impurities, and subjected to a temperature-controlled air-drying step (20 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 5% relative humidity) for 2 h to remove surface moisture and facilitate uniform adhesion of curing agents. Next, the meat pieces were evenly coated with a salt-based composite curing agent and arranged in layers within camphor wood barrels for room-temperature curing for 8 to 10 days. After curing, the meat was transferred to specialized smoking equipment and smoked using a mixed fuel of <em>Cyclobalanopsis glauca</em> wood and cypress branches. The smoking process lasted 40 days, during which the chamber temperature was maintained within 25 to 45 °C.</p>
<p id="p0055">Samples were collected at three time points during smoking (days 8, 24, and 40) and coded according to cut type and smoking time. Belly bacon samples were designated as LRW8, LRW24, and LRW40, whereas rump bacon samples were designated as LRT8, LRT24, and LRT40. After collection, all samples were immediately stored at −80 °C until analysis.</p>
<p id="p0060">To minimize processing variability, LRW and LRT samples were obtained from the same production batch and processed under standardized and controlled smoking conditions, following a consistent protocol in the same smoking chamber. At the batch level, key processing parameters (e.g., smoking duration and chamber settings) were kept consistent. However, salt uptake at the individual sample level (e.g., final salt content) and local microenvironmental conditions within the chamber, such as spatial heterogeneity in humidity and airflow, were not quantitatively monitored or standardized. Accordingly, minor inter-sample variability in metabolite levels cannot be completely excluded and is acknowledged as a limitation in the Discussion.</p></section><section id="s0020"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Untargeted metabolomics analysis using LC–MS/MS</h3>
<section id="s0025"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2.1. Metabolite extraction</h4>
<p id="p0065">To investigate metabolic changes in LRW and LRT across three smoking stages, two groups (LRW and LRT) were analyzed, with six biological replicates per smoking stage in each group. In total, 36 samples (2 groups × 3 stages × 6 replicates) were included for untargeted metabolomics analysis. Metabolite extraction was performed according to the protocol described by <a href="#bb0230" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0230">Ying et al. (2023)</a>.</p></section><section id="s0030"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2.2. LC–MS/MS</h4>
<p id="p0070">Untargeted metabolomics profiling was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Chromatographic separation was carried out on an HSS T3 reversed-phase column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, USA) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phases consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water/acetonitrile, 95:5, <em>v</em>/v) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile/isopropanol/water, 47.5:47.5:5, v/v/v), where the ratios denote the volume ratios of each component in the total solvent. The flow rate was set at 0.40 mL/min, and the injection volume was 3 μL.</p>
<p id="p0075">Mass spectrometric detection was conducted in high-sensitivity mode, with a scan range of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 70–1050 in both positive and negative ionization modes. The ion source parameters were as follows: sheath gas flow, 50 psi; auxiliary gas flow, 13 psi; ion transfer tube temperature, 325 °C; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 425 °C; and electrospray voltage, ±3.5 kV (positive/negative mode). The mass analyzer was operated at a resolution of 60,000 for full MS scans and 7500 for MS/MS scans, with stepped normalized collision energies (NCE) of 20, 40, and 60. Data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode with dynamic exclusion to enhance the detection of low-abundance metabolites.</p></section><section id="s0035"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2.3. LC–MS/MS data processing and analysis</h4>
<p id="p0080">The raw data were initially preprocessed using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Each feature was normalized to the total ion current, generating metabolite features characterized by retention time and mass-to-charge ratio (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em>), along with their corresponding normalized peak areas. Subsequent data preprocessing and analysis were performed on the Majorbio Cloud Platform. Multivariate statistical analysis was performed using the R package <em>ropls</em> (version 1.6.2). Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to reveal overall clustering patterns and identify outlier samples, followed by partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) with 7-fold cross-validation to evaluate model stability.</p>
<p id="p0085">Common differential metabolites (CDMs) were screened and queried against the public databases HMDB and METLIN for structural comparison and annotation via the I-Sanger Cloud Platform (Majorbio, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, metabolic pathway annotation was performed using the KEGG database, and pathway enrichment analysis was conducted in Python (v3.13.2) using the scipy.stats package. Fisher's exact test was applied to identify significantly enriched pathways associated with the experimental groups.</p></section></section><section id="s0040"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Construction of machine learning models</h3>
<section id="s0045"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3.1. Data dimensionality reduction</h4>
<p id="p0090">Significance testing of common differential metabolites was first performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) in Python (v3.13.2). To further reduce dimensionality, a random forest feature selection approach based on Gini-impurity feature importance was combined with low-variance filtration, After Min–Max normalization, a variance threshold of 0.085 was applied to remove features with limited variability. The intersection of metabolites retained by both methods was used as key candidates.</p>
<p id="p0095">To further refine the selection of candidate biomarkers, additional filtering criteria were applied through a multidimensional evaluation, with thresholds set for the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF &lt; 75), feature–label correlation (&gt; 85%), and information gain (IG = 1). These thresholds were optimized based on the dataset characteristics and the high correlation typically observed in metabolomics data, aiming to balance redundancy control with the retention of biologically meaningful features. Through this stepwise optimization process, the selected metabolites were expected to retain both biological relevance and discriminatory power for classification.</p></section><section id="s0050"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3.2. RF, KNN, SVM, and FNN classification models</h4>
<p id="p0100">As illustrated in <a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>, four machine-learning algorithms—Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and a Feedforward Neural Network (FNN)—were employed to develop classification models for both forward evaluation and reverse validation.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="f0005" title="Fig. 1"><h5 class="obj_head">Fig. 1.</h5>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=12887885_gr1.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/13fc/12887885/b81557c9e635/gr1.jpg" loading="lazy" id="lk0005" height="700" width="789" alt="Fig. 1"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/f0005/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>Flowchart of the machine-learning model training and evaluation workflow. The process begins with data preprocessing, followed by model selection, training, and performance evaluation. The left panel presents schematic representations of the Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifiers, whereas the right panel depicts the k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) classifiers.</p></figcaption></figure><p id="p0105">Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble learning algorithm that aggregates multiple decision trees to improve predictive performance. It employs two main randomization strategies: (1) bootstrap sampling (sampling with replacement) to generate training subsets for each tree, and (2) random feature subsetting at each node to determine the split. This dual randomization helps reduce overfitting and variance associated with individual decision trees, thereby improving robustness and generalization; for classification, predictions are typically obtained by majority voting across the ensemble (<a href="#bb0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0065">Halabaku and Bytyçi, 2024</a>).</p>
<p id="p0110">Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a maximum-margin classifier that seeks a decision hyperplane by maximizing the margin between classes while penalizing classification errors (soft margin). The support vectors are the training samples closest to the decision boundary and thus determine the position of the hyperplane (<a href="#bb0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0070">Hao et al., 2022</a>). For nonlinearly separable data, SVM uses kernel functions to map the original features into a higher-dimensional space, where linear separation may become feasible, making it well suited for high-dimensional classification tasks.</p>
<p id="p0115">k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) is a classic non-parametric supervised learning method used for both classification and regression. Predictions are made based on the “similarity” principle by computing distances between a query sample and training samples, selecting the k nearest neighbors, and then applying majority voting for classification or (weighted) averaging for regression (<a href="#bb0240" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0240">Zhang, 2022</a>). Model performance depends strongly on the choice of k and the distance metric (e.g., Euclidean or Manhattan distance), and k directly influences the bias–variance trade-off (<a href="#bb0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0020">Deng et al., 2016</a>).</p>
<p id="p0120">A Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) is a neural network architecture comprising an input layer, one or more fully connected hidden layers, and an output layer. Input data are propagated forward through trainable weight matrices; each layer performs a linear transformation followed by a nonlinear activation function (e.g., ReLU) to capture nonlinear relationships and extract higher-level features (<a href="#bb0160" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0160">Park et al., 2021</a>). For classification, the output layer commonly uses the Softmax function to produce a probability distribution over classes. Model parameters are optimized by minimizing a loss function via backpropagation, and performance is assessed using an independent validation/test set to evaluate classification accuracy and generalization.</p></section><section id="s0055"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3.3. Data processing for machine learning</h4>
<p id="p0125">The RF, SVM, KNN, and FNN models were constructed, and the metabolite dataset was randomly split into independent training and test sets, with 70% of the data used for model training and 30% for model testing. Given that the study included a total of 36 samples, this split resulted in 11 samples in the test set. For RF, KNN, and SVM, seven-fold cross-validation (CV = 7) was applied during model training, whereas the FNN was trained for 50 epochs. Model performance was evaluated using four metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. These metrics range from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating better model performance (<a href="#bb0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0075">Ji et al., 2023</a>).</p>
<p id="p0130">Furthermore, confusion matrices were visualized, and the predicted probabilities (or decision scores, where applicable) were examined against the true labels for each model. To ensure transparency and reproducibility of the machine learning workflow, Supplementary Tables S4 and S5 summarize the data preprocessing procedures, feature scaling, dimensionality reduction, and model-specific hyperparameter settings.</p></section></section><section id="s0060"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="p0135">Metabolomics data analysis and visualization were performed using the Majorbio Cloud Platform and GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, LLC, San Diego, CA, USA). Common differential metabolites (CDMs) were screened using the criteria of <em>P &lt;</em> 0.05, variable importance in projection (VIP) &gt; 1, and |log2FC| &gt; 1. Machine learning model construction, multicollinearity assessment, label correlation analysis, and information gain calculations were implemented in Python 3.13.2 using the PyCharm 2024.3 integrated development environment (IDE). Differences between the two groups were assessed using two-tailed independent-samples <em>t</em>-tests. Power analysis and sample size adequacy were evaluated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 with α = 0.05 and power (1 − β) = 0.95, to estimate the minimal detectable standardized effect size under the given sample size.</p></section></section><section id="s0065"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results and discussion</h2>
<section id="s0070"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Metabolomic differential analysis between LRW and LRT</h3>
<p id="p0140">Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to assess inter-group and intra-group variation among samples, as well as overall variability. As shown in <a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>, samples from the LRW and LRT groups exhibited a certain degree of separation in both positive and negative ion modes, suggesting distinct differences in metabolite profiles between the two types of bacon. With the exception of a few samples collected at day 24 of smoking, the remaining samples showed clear group-wise clustering. The separation was most pronounced at day 40 of smoking. In the positive and negative ion modes, the cumulative explained variance of PC1 and PC2 was 64.2% and 61.6%, respectively, providing preliminary evidence that the metabolite compositions of the two cured-meat groups differ.</p>

This supervised multivariate method provides advantages in dimensionality reduction, classification, and identification of condition-specific patterns, while offering an intuitive visualization of group discrimination (<a href="#bb0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0085">Lee et al., 2018</a>). As shown in Fig. S1, the PLS-DA score plots showed clear separation between the sample groups in both positive and negative ion modes, suggesting differences in metabolite profiles between the two groups. To assess model reliability, permutation testing was performed. As shown in Fig. S2, the original models were clearly distinguished from the permuted models, and both R<sup>2</sup> and Q<sup>2</sup> generally decreased with increasing permutation, suggesting no obvious overfitting and supporting the robustness of the PLS-DA models.</p></section><section id="s0075"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Screening and classification of CDMs in LRW and LRT</h3>
<p id="p0150">Differential metabolites were screened using the criteria of <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05, VIP &gt; 1, and |log₂FC| &gt; 1. The differential metabolites between the LRW and LRT groups were then compared across smoking stages. As shown in <a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>a, the comparison between LRW and LRT after 8 days of smoking identified 719 differential metabolites, including 196 upregulated and 523 downregulated (defined relative to LRW vs. LRT). After 24 days of smoking, 451 differential metabolites were identified, with 250 upregulated and 201 downregulated. After 40 days of smoking, a total of 775 differential metabolites were identified, of which 442 were upregulated and 333 were downregulated.</p>

Given their consistent identification across smoking periods, these shared metabolites may serve as candidate discriminative markers distinguishing LRW from LRT. Because LRW and LRT originate from anatomically distinct muscles with differences in fiber-type composition and metabolic capacity, a proportion of these stage-consistent CDMs may reflect cut-intrinsic metabolic features that persist throughout processing, while curing/smoking may further modulate their abundances (<a href="#bb0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0150">Muroya, 2023a</a>; <a href="#bb0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0195">Tan et al., 2023</a>).</p>
<p id="p0160">The Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) was used to classify the 100 stage-consistent CDMs between LRW and LRT during the smoking process based on subclass hierarchies. As shown in <a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>c, 78 metabolites were annotated within the top 20 categories. Among these, “Amino acids, peptides, and analogs” accounted for the largest proportion (20 metabolites, 25.64%), followed by “Not available” (10 metabolites, 12.82%), “Fatty acid esters” (9 metabolites, 11.54%), “Fatty acids and conjugates” (6 metabolites, 7.69%), and “Carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates” (3 metabolites, 3.85%). The distribution pattern dominated by amino acid–related metabolites is likely associated with protein degradation during meat processing. Meat proteins can be gradually hydrolyzed into small peptides and free amino acids by endogenous proteolytic enzymes (e.g., calpains and other proteases) (<a href="#bb0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0105">Li et al., 2018</a>; <a href="#bb0250" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0250">Zhao et al., 2025a</a>, <a href="#bb0255" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0255">Zhao et al., 2025b</a>). In addition, muscle-specific physiology (e.g., fiber-type composition and associated enzymatic/mitochondrial capacity) may influence baseline protein turnover and postmortem metabolism, thereby contributing to cut-dependent differences in peptide/amino-acid pools during processing. These degradation products not only serve as nutrients but can also be converted into key flavor compounds through Strecker degradation and Maillard reactions. For instance, alanine and glutamic acid contribute to sweet and umami tastes, respectively, whereas leucine, arginine, and lysine are associated with bitterness (<a href="#bb0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0145">Mottram, 1998</a>; <a href="#bb0165" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0165">Rizzi, 2008</a>; <a href="#bb0185" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0185">Spanier et al., 2004</a>).</p></section><section id="s0080"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3. Metabolite levels and KEGG pathways in LRW and LRT</h3>
<p id="p0165">The relative abundances of the top 30 metabolites in LRW and LRT were visualized using a heat map (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Figs. 3</a>e–g). Several metabolites, including Glu-Asp-Glu, Pro-Pro-Pro,1-[(2<em>R</em>,3<em>S</em>,5<em>R</em>)-3,4-dihydroxy-5-(hydroxymethyl)oxolan-2-yl]pyrimidine-2,4-dione, glutamylvaline, N<sup>2</sup>-succinyl-L-ornithine, Val-Val, N-α-acetyllysine, succinylcarnitine, 6-hydroxyoctanoylcarnitine, and seryl-isoleucine, showed higher levels in LRT. In contrast, epsilon-caprolactone, xanthosine, xanthine, ethyl hydrogen fumarate, D-digitoxose, <em>N</em>-acetyl-DL-leucine, adipic acid, fenvalerate, succinyladenosine, Fe(II)-nicotianamine, and malic acid were more abundant in LRW.</p>
<p id="p0170">The enrichment of short peptides (e.g., Glu-Asp-Glu and Pro-Pro-Pro) in LRT may be associated with cut-dependent differences in connective tissue content and proteolysis during smoking. Endogenous proteases (e.g., cathepsins B and D) may facilitate the release of oligopeptides from connective tissue. Proline and hydroxyproline—key components of connective tissue—have been linked to water-holding properties of meat products and may also influence metabolite formation through heat-induced reactions with nitrites and other compounds. Furthermore, glutamylvaline, a proteolytic derivative, may enhance umami perception (<a href="#bb0225" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0225">Yang et al., 2021</a>), potentially via umami receptors such as the T1R1/T1R3 heterodimer (<a href="#bb0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0060">Fu et al., 2024</a>). The higher levels of succinylcarnitine and 6-hydroxyoctanoylcarnitine in LRT may also suggest differences in mitochondrial-associated energy metabolism and fatty-acid utilization between muscle types, consistent with reported metabolic differentiation between oxidative and glycolytic muscles (<a href="#bb0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0155">Muroya, 2023b</a>; <a href="#bb0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0195">Tan et al., 2023</a>).</p>
<p id="p0175">In LRW, the accumulation of adipic acid may inhibit lipoxygenase activity by lowering local pH, thereby potentially delaying lipid oxidation and contributing to product quality (<a href="#bb0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0120">Liu et al., 2013</a>). Meanwhile, the relatively higher malic acid level may reflect cut-dependent differences in postmortem energy metabolism (including glycolysis- and TCA-cycle–associated intermediates) and/or microbial transformation during processing (<a href="#bb0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0010">Chauhan and England, 2018</a>; <a href="#bb0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0155">Muroya, 2023b</a>). Collectively, these cut-specific metabolic features may provide an intrinsic biochemical baseline upon which smoking-driven oxidative and thermal reactions further shape metabolite trajectories and contribute to flavor development (<a href="#bb0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0155">Muroya, 2023b</a>).</p>
<p id="p0180">KEGG-based annotation and enrichment analysis further showed that multiple pathways were significantly enriched for CDMs under the criterion of <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05 (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>d), including vitamin B6 metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), pyruvate metabolism, caffeine metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and pyrimidine metabolism. Notably, arginine and proline metabolism exhibited a high enrichment factor, and both the citrate cycle and pyruvate metabolism were significantly enriched, highlighting the involvement of amino acid metabolism and energy-related pathways in shaping metabolic differences during bacon smoking.</p></section><section id="s0085"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.4. Dimension reduction-based screening of key differential metabolites</h3>
<p id="p0185">The Kruskal–Wallis H test (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) was used to evaluate the statistical significance of the 100 common differential metabolites. As summarized in Table S1, 75 metabolites differed significantly between LRW and LRT, including Trp-Thr, tetrahydrouridine, cyclo(His-Pro), and Ile-Arg.</p>
<p id="p0190">To reduce dimensionality and obtain a robust set of discriminative features, two complementary selection strategies were applied to these 75 metabolites. A random forest–based approach ranked variables by importance and retained 43 metabolites with relatively strong discriminatory ability (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>a). In parallel, variance-based filtering was performed after Min–Max normalization (0–1 scaling); using a variance threshold of 0.085, 33 metabolites were retained (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>b). The intersection of the two feature sets yielded 22 consensus key differential metabolites (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>c), which consistently differed between LRW and LRT during the smoking process.</p>

The protocol incorporated multicollinearity assessment (variance inflation factor, VIF &lt; 75; Table S2), feature-importance evaluation (information gain = 1; Table S2), and label association assessment (metabolite–label correlation &gt;85%; <a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>a). Establishment and assessment of the discriminant model</h3>
<p id="p0200">This study evaluated the discriminative ability of four core differential metabolites using machine-learning classifiers to distinguish LRW from LRT. A forward evaluation and comparative validation design was adopted: models were first built using the four core metabolites, and a comparative model was then constructed using the remaining 18 metabolites (i.e., the 22 key metabolites excluding the four core metabolites).</p>
<p id="p0205">Four algorithms—random forest (RF), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), support vector machine (SVM), and a feedforward neural network (FNN)—were implemented. The dataset (<em>n</em> = 36) was randomly split into an independent training set (70%, 25 samples) and test set (30%, 11 samples). RF, KNN, and SVM were optimized using 7-fold cross-validation within the training set, whereas the FNN was trained for 50 epochs. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.</p>
<p id="p0210">Overall, the models based on the four core metabolites outperformed the 18-metabolite models across these metrics (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>b). Although the 18-metabolite models achieved relatively high accuracy for several algorithms (often &gt;0.8; <a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>b), accuracy alone may not fully reflect discrimination reliability. Therefore, we further evaluated model performance using the confusion matrices (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Figs. 5</a>c–d) and examined feature separability and prediction confidence. Many metabolites in the 18-metabolite set showed substantial distribution overlap between LRW and LRT (Fig. S3), indicating weaker separability and potential redundancy. In contrast, the four core metabolites exhibited minimal overlap in their frequency distributions (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>e), consistent with improved class discrimination. Predicted-probability analyses further showed that the four-metabolite models generally produced higher-confidence probability distributions and better alignment with true labels than the 18-metabolite models (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>; Fig. S4), supporting the superior discrimination performance, stability, and interpretability of the parsimonious four-metabolite panel. These observations are consistent with previous reports that appropriate feature selection can improve classification performance (<a href="#bb0125" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0125">Liu et al., 2021</a>). Moreover, integrating multiple screening strategies may enhance model robustness compared with relying on a single method (<a href="#bb0235" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0235">Zeng et al., 2015</a>).</p>

Characterization of candidate biomarkers</h3>
<p id="p0215">Untargeted LC–MS/MS metabolomics combined with machine-learning analysis identified four candidate biomarkers discriminating LRW from LRT: 6-hydroxyoctanoylcarnitine, dimethylethanolamine, xanthine, and ethyl hydrogen fumarate. The heat map based on these features (Fig. S5) showed partial separation between the two groups: 6-hydroxyoctanoylcarnitine was relatively more abundant in LRT, whereas dimethylethanolamine, xanthine, and ethyl hydrogen fumarate were relatively enriched in LRW. The annotation details for these candidate biomarkers are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.</p>
<p id="p0220">Among these metabolites, 6-hydroxyoctanoylcarnitine belongs to the acylcarnitine family and is commonly linked to mitochondrial fatty-acid transport and β-oxidation–related metabolism. Its higher relative abundance in LRT may reflect cut-dependent differences in mitochondrial fatty-acid handling under the applied processing conditions, potentially influencing the pool of lipid-derived precursors available for volatile formation during curing and smoking. Medium- and long-chain acylcarnitines have been widely used as indicators associated with mitochondrial β-oxidation status and cellular energy metabolism (<a href="#bb0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0015">Dambrova et al., 2022</a>; <a href="#bb0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0135">McCann et al., 2021</a>). This observation is also broadly consistent with metabolic differences between oxidative and glycolytic muscles, as oxidative muscles typically contain more mitochondria and rely more on fatty-acid oxidation for energy supply (<a href="#bb0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0195">Tan et al., 2023</a>).</p>
<p id="p0225">In contrast, dimethylethanolamine, a choline analogue that can be linked to choline/ethanolamine metabolism and phosphatidylcholine biosynthesis (Kennedy pathway) (<a href="#bb0080" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0080">Kenny et al., 2025</a>; <a href="#bb0210" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0210">van der Veen et al., 2017</a>), was more abundant in LRW. Given that membrane phospholipids are major sources of aldehydes and ketones generated during lipid oxidation (<a href="#bb0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0025">Domínguez et al., 2019</a>), enrichment of dimethylethanolamine may indicate cut-dependent differences in membrane-lipid composition and/or oxidative stability, which could influence the availability of flavor precursors during processing.</p>
<p id="p0230">Xanthine, a downstream product of inosine monophosphate (IMP) catabolism, was also relatively higher in LRW. IMP is commonly associated with umami perception, whereas its degradation products (e.g., inosine, hypoxanthine, and xanthine) have been linked to bitterness and shifts in flavor perception. Accordingly, elevated xanthine in LRW may indicate more active nucleotide turnover and could be relevant to sensory attributes of the bacon. Integrative metabolomics–flavoromics studies have reported associations between purine metabolites and flavor characteristics in meat products (<a href="#bb0200" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0200">Tikk et al., 2006</a>; <a href="#bb0205" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0205">To et al., 2023</a>), supporting the potential sensory relevance of this observation (<a href="#bb0250" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0250">Zhao et al., 2025a</a>, <a href="#bb0255" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0255">Zhao et al., 2025b</a>). From a pathway perspective, increased xanthine in LRW may relate to cut-dependent differences in postmortem energy metabolism, particularly ATP breakdown and nucleotide turnover; such differences are often more evident in muscles with higher glycolytic activity (<a href="#bb0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0010">Chauhan and England, 2018</a>; <a href="#bb0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0150">Muroya, 2023a</a>).</p>
<p id="p0235">The fourth feature, ethyl hydrogen fumarate, was tentatively annotated based on its mass spectral characteristics and is indexed in the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB0040207) and the LIPID MAPS Structure Database (LMFA07010876). Structurally, it is a monoethyl ester derivative of fumaric acid. Fumaric acid is a detectable TCA-cycle intermediate in muscle and may vary with postmortem biochemical processes. In processed meat systems, shifts in organic-acid–related metabolism (including TCA intermediates) may contribute to metabolomic differentiation and could be associated with flavor development, potentially through changes in reaction environment (e.g., acidity) and downstream chemical transformations (<a href="#bb0215" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0215">Wojtasik-Kalinowska et al., 2023</a>; <a href="#bb0245" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0245">Zhao et al., 2024</a>).</p>
<p id="p0240">From a food chemistry perspective, the presence of ethyl hydrogen fumarate in bacon may be more plausibly explained by processing-related chemical transformations than by a specific endogenous biosynthetic route. During curing and smoking, fumarate-related organic acids may coexist with trace alcohols derived from limited microbial activity and/or processing-related pathways. Under typical processing conditions (e.g., elevated temperature, acidic environment, and reduced water activity), low-level non-enzymatic esterification between organic acids and alcohols may occur, yielding low-abundance ester derivatives. Similar esterification reactions have been considered part of the chemical evolution contributing to flavor development in cured and fermented meat products (<a href="#bb0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0055">Flores, 2018</a>; <a href="#bb0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0090">Leroy et al., 2006</a>). To our knowledge, ethyl hydrogen fumarate has been infrequently reported in food metabolomics, which may relate to its low abundance and potential instability rather than the absence of a chemically reasonable formation pathway. Accordingly, in this study, ethyl hydrogen fumarate is better interpreted as a cut-discriminatory chemical feature associated with fumarate-related organic-acid chemistry modulated by processing conditions, rather than as a direct marker of TCA cycle activity.</p>
<p id="p0245">Although these four metabolites showed good discriminatory performance in multivariate statistics and machine-learning models, their interpretation as biomarkers should consider potential confounding factors. First, inherent anatomical and physiological differences between LRW and LRT—such as muscle fiber-type composition, intramuscular fat content, connective tissue distribution, and baseline oxidative capacity—can systematically influence fatty-acid β-oxidation–related metabolism, phospholipid metabolism, and nucleotide degradation. Therefore, part of the observed differences may reflect intrinsic muscle metabolism rather than being solely induced by processing. Second, even under standardized curing and smoking conditions, cut-dependent physical properties may introduce processing-related variability. For example, differences in tissue structure and density may affect salt diffusion and water migration (<a href="#bb0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0050">Fekete et al., 2025</a>; <a href="#bb0265" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0265">Zhou et al., 2024</a>), and local heterogeneity during smoking (e.g., temperature, humidity, and smoke exposure) may also contribute to subtle variation in metabolite levels (<a href="#bb0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0040">Duma-Kocan et al., 2020</a>). Taken together, these confounding influences may contribute to the observed metabolite differences and should be considered when interpreting the results.</p>
<p id="p0250">Even though all samples were obtained from the same batch and processed using an identical protocol, several potential confounding factors were not quantitatively monitored, which may have introduced between-sample variability and consequently influenced metabolite abundances. Given that the present analyses were conducted on fully smoked pork belly bacon and pork rump bacon, the observed metabolite profiles may primarily reflect intrinsic, cut-specific metabolic characteristics, while their abundances may be further modulated under standardized processing conditions. Future studies should incorporate real-time monitoring of key processing parameters and salt diffusion dynamics, and validate these findings across independent processing batches. In addition, paired sampling before and after smoking would help disentangle inherent cut-related differences from processing-induced alterations, thereby allowing a more rigorous evaluation of the robustness and generalizability of the results.</p></section><section id="s0100"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.7. Statistical validation of candidate biomarker robustness</h3>
<p id="p0255">To further assess the robustness of the biomarker candidates, a statistical power analysis was conducted. With equal sample sizes (<em>n</em> = 18 per group), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a target power (1 − β) = 0.95, the estimated power based on the observed effect sizes for the four metabolites exceeded 0.95 (Supplementary Fig. S6). At this sample size, the minimal detectable standardized effect size (Cohen's d) at 95% power was 1.24 (Supplementary Fig. S7). Collectively, these results indicate adequate statistical sensitivity to support the robustness of the selected biomarker candidates.</p>
<p id="p0260">Although the putative annotations of these candidate biomarkers were primarily based on accurate mass and MS/MS spectral information, and their retention times and fragmentation patterns were generally consistent across samples (Supplementary Fig. S8), targeted quantification was not performed because authentic standards were unavailable for some metabolites. Therefore, these candidate biomarkers should be considered putatively annotated and warrant further confirmation. Future studies will aim to validate these candidate biomarkers using authentic standards and independent bacon batches when feasible.</p></section></section><section id="s0105"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0265">This study establishes a systematic framework for screening and assessing discriminatory candidate biomarkers for bacon authentication by integrating LC–MS/MS-based metabolomics with machine learning. The analysis identified 100 common differential metabolic features, from which four key candidate biomarkers were prioritized through statistical analysis and feature-dimension reduction. Among them, 6-hydroxyoctanoylcarnitine was relatively enriched in LRT, whereas dimethylethanolamine, xanthine, and ethyl hydrogen fumarate constituted an LRW-associated biomarker panel. The robustness and discriminative performance of these candidate biomarkers were supported by a multimodel evaluation system incorporating RF, KNN, SVM, and FNN with both forward and reverse validation. Statistical power analysis, based on the observed effect sizes, further supported the reliability of these group differences, with power exceeding 0.95 under the tested conditions. Overall, this integrated approach provides a basis for data-driven biomarker discovery and offers a practical paradigm for bacon authentication; however, the biomarker identities remain putative and warrant further confirmation using authentic standards and independent processing batches.</p></section><section id="s0110"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CRediT authorship contribution statement</h2>
