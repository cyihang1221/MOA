</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>The phytotoxic effects
of extracts from <em>Hyptis crenata</em> (<em>
<strong>Hcre</strong>
</em>), <em>Hyptis suaveolens</em> (<em>
<strong>Hsua</strong>
</em>), <em>Hyptis saxatilis</em> (<em>
<strong>Hsax</strong>
</em>), <em>Hyptis campestris</em> (<em>
<strong>Hcam</strong>
</em>), and <em>Hyptis brevipes</em> (<em>
<strong>Hbre</strong>
</em>) were evaluated against three <em>Amaranthus</em> weed species under controlled germination chamber
conditions. Extracts <em>
<strong>Hcre</strong>
</em>, <em>
<strong>Hsua</strong>
</em> and <em>
<strong>Hsax</strong>
</em> inhibited
seed germination by 85–93% relative to the control and markedly
suppressed radicle and hypocotyl growth in <em>Amaranthus viridis</em> and <em>Amaranthus hybridus</em> in Petri dish assays. At
200 ppm, <em>
<strong>Hcre</strong>
</em> and <em>
<strong>Hsax</strong>
</em> reduced radicle and hypocotyl lengths by up to 90% in both
species, comparable to the effects of commercial herbicides Sencor
and Sulfentrazone. Multivariate analyses, including PCA, PLS-DA, and
molecular networking based on LC-MS/MS data, enabled the annotation
of compounds associated with phytotoxicity. A total of 56 metabolites
were identified, including terpenoids, flavonoids, α-pyrones,
carbohydrate derivatives, and polyketides, using both an in-house
database and the GNPS platform. Terpenoids were the predominant class,
with abietane-type diterpenes as the most abundant subgroup, exhibiting
strong correlations with the observed biological activity.</p></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><hr class="headless">
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=12572983_ao5c07637_0010.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/fda0/12572983/8d12694b8e72/ao5c07637_0010.jpg" loading="lazy" id="abs1" height="217" width="680" alt="graphic file with name ao5c07637_0010.jpg"></a></p></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract3"><hr class="headless">
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=12572983_ao5c07637_0008.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/fda0/12572983/8d12694b8e72/ao5c07637_0008.jpg" loading="lazy" id="tgr1" height="217" width="680" alt="graphic file with name ao5c07637_0008.jpg"></a></p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Agrochemical development draws from diverse
sources of bioactive
compounds, ranging from the phytotoxic metabolites found in nature
to synthetically engineered molecules which are designed for targeted
activity and reduced environmental toxicity.<em>
,
</em> Traditionally,
research into the diverse array of bioactive metabolites produced
by the plants have showed a significant potential, encompassing applications
such as bioherbicides development, immune modulation, pharmacological
activities, and the enhancement of the abiotic stress tolerance.<em>
−

</em> The control of invasive plants remains a major challenge in agricultural
production. Integrated weed management,
particularly the use of bioherbicides, has emerged as a promising
strategy for sustainable agriculture, offering both ecological and
economic benefits. Bioherbicides derived
from natural sources, such as plant and microbial extracts, have demonstrated
potential for pre- and postemergence weed control by inhibiting seed
germination and plant growth.
</p>
<p>Seed
germination, a critical stage in plant development, involves
essential physiological and biochemical processes necessary for seedling
establishment. Plant extracts used as
bioherbicides can suppress germination by blocking nutrient hydrolysis
and disrupting cell division, thereby
chemically regulating weed development. These extracts are rich in
secondary metabolites that act as phytotoxins, inhibiting weed growth
by interfering with processes such as transpiration, respiration,
and photosynthesis. This has driven interest
in plant species with allelopathic potential as sources for novel
bioherbicides.
</p>
<p>The genus <em>Hyptis</em> (Lamiaceae), comprising approximately
400 species primarily distributed across tropical America, has attracted
considerable attention due to its chemical diversity. Species in this genus produce a wide array of bioactive
compounds, including flavonoids, terpenes
and steroids, phenolic acids, alkaloids, lignans, and other secondary metabolites..<em>
,
</em> Previous studies have reported the phytotoxic activity of <em>Hyptis</em> extracts and isolated metabolites. For example, extracts
from <em>Hyptis suaveolens</em> and <em>Hyptis rhomboidea</em> inhibited germination and seedling growth of several crop and weed
species, including <em>Brassica campestris</em>, <em>Raphanus
sativus L</em>., <em>Oryza sativa</em>, <em>Lactuca
sativa</em>, <em>Lolium multiflorum</em>, and <em>Echinochloa crus-galli</em>. Additionally,
suaveolic acid, an allelochemical isolated from <em>H</em>. <em>suaveolens</em>, reduced the growth of <em>L</em>. <em>sativa</em>, <em>L</em>. <em>multiflorum</em>, and <em>E</em>. <em>crus-galli</em>.
</p>
<p>Metabolomics has increasingly been applied
to investigate the phytotoxicity
of plant extracts, providing insights into their chemical composition
and enabling the identification of secondary metabolites responsible
for bioactivity. For instance, allelochemicals such as afzelin derivatives,
anthraquinones, and phenolic acids were linked to the phytotoxic activity
of three <em>Cassia</em> species against Chenopodium murale. Similarly, <em>Jatropha gossypiifolia</em> L. inhibited the germination and growth of <em>Bidens bipinnata</em> L. with activity attributed to bioactive alkaloids, phenolics, and
terpenoids. Moreover, metabolomic analyses
of <em>Dipteryx lacunifera</em> Ducke, <em>Ricinus communis</em> L., <em>Piper tuberculatum</em> Jacq., and <em>J</em>. <em>gossypiifolia</em> L. identified phenolics and terpenoids
as allelochemicals contributing to their allelopathic effects.
</p>
<p>This study extends beyond previous research
by comparatively investigating
the phytotoxic and allelopathic activities of five Hyptis species,
Hyptis crenata, H. suaveolens, Hyptis saxatilis, Hyptis campestris,
and Hyptis brevipes, against Amaranthus weeds, a globally relevant
and herbicide-resistant genus. By integrating bioassays with UPLC-qTOF-MS/MS
metabolic profiling and molecular networking via GNPS, we provide
a comparative chemical–biological analysis within Hyptis. This
integrative approach not only identifies bioactive metabolites but
also highlights interspecific variation in allelopathic potential,
offering new insights into the discovery of plant-derived bioherbicides.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results

<p>Moreover, the phytotoxic effects
of <em>Hyptis</em> extracts
have been documented against other problematic weed species, including <em>Eleusine coracana</em>,
<em>E</em>. <em>crus-galli</em>, and <em>L</em>. <em>multiflorum</em>. These results further highlight the potential
of <em>Hyptis</em> as a natural herbicide for sustainable
weed management, where allelopathic interactions disrupt key metabolic
pathways in target species, ultimately inhibiting their growth and
development.
</p>
<p>The reduction in plant
growth observed in this study demonstrates
the selective phytotoxic activity of the Hyptis extracts at 200 ppm.
This allelopathic potential against Amaranthus species is likely attributed
to bioactive secondary metabolites present in Hyptis. Plant extracts
often contain compounds capable of inhibiting germination and growth
in other species, potentially by interference with indole-3-acetic
acid biosynthesis via the tryptophan pathway, disruption of protein
synthesis, or impairing ion absorption.
</p>
<p>Several <em>Hyptis</em> species have previously been
recognized
for their phytotoxic potential and proposed as candidates for bioherbicide
development. For example, the secondary
metabolite 6-methoxy-benzoxazolin-2­(3<em>H</em>)-one, isolated
from <em>H</em>. <em>suaveolens</em>, strongly inhibits
germination in eight weed species by blocking the induction of α-amylase,
an enzyme essential for starch hydrolysis during seed germination
and early seedling growth.
</p>
<p>The
bioherbicidal potential of <em>Hyptis</em> species
is closely associated with their structurally diverse secondary metabolites,
particularly terpenes and flavonoids. Several terpenoid have been
reported, including triterpene lactones from <em>Hyptis albida</em>, labdane diterpenoids from <em>Hyptis fasciculata</em>, isopimarane diterpenes from <em>Hyptis salzmanii</em>, and tricyclic
diterpenes from <em>Hyptis dilatate</em>. In addition, flavonoids such as isoquercitrin from <em>H</em>. <em>fasciculata</em>, apigenin-<em>C</em>,<em>C</em>-diglucoside, and
kaempferol-3-<em>O</em>-rutinoside from <em>Hyptis colombiana</em>
 have been identified. Despite the chemical
diversity of the genus, terpenoids remain the most prominent class
of bioactive compounds, exhibiting a broad spectrum of biological
activities.<em>
,
</em>
</p>
<p>In summary, the increasing
demand for safe and sustainable alternatives
has driven the development of bioherbicides derived from plant extracts,
which provide effective weed control without long-term persistence
or broad toxicity. Among these, allelochemical-based
bioherbicides are particularly promising, as they combine low toxicity,
multiple modes of action, and rapid degradation, thereby minimizing
risks to nontarget organisms and environmental accumulation. Our findings, demonstrating the herbicidal potential
of Hyptis species, further support the feasibility of exploring allelochemical-inspired
molecules as leads for the development of novel bioherbicides.</p></section><section id="sec2.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Multivariate

(<strong>6</strong>) - have been reported previously. Of the compounds
identified in the chemical profile, 52% were classified as terpenoids,
as illustrated in <a href="#fig7" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>. Terpenes are widely recognized for their diverse biological
activities, including inhibition of seed germination, insecticidal and herbicidal
effects, suppression of root and shoot
growth, and induction of disease symptoms in plants.
</p>

in chemical profile,
comprising 16 compounds, of which 10 exhibited the characteristic
tricyclic scaffold of abietane diterpenoids. Abietane diterpenoids
can be classified into 14 structural categories. In this study, three distinct classes were identified:
(i) aromatic abietanes (compounds <strong>1–5</strong>), (ii) <em>para</em>-quinone abietanes (compounds <strong>6–9</strong>), and (iii) a rearranged abietane diterpenoid (compound <strong>10</strong>), there by expanding the structural diversity of abietanes detected
in <em>Hyptis</em> species.</p>
<p>Abietane-type diterpenes,
such as those previously isolated from
the roots of <em>Hyptis martiusii</em>, <em>Hyptis platanifolia</em>, and <em>Hyptis carvalho</em>, have been widely investigated
for their biological activities, including antioxidant, antibacterial, cytotoxic, antileishmanial, topoisomerase I inhibitory, and seed
germination inhibitory effects.
</p>
<p>Metabolites <strong>1–3</strong> were detected in the mass spectra
as protonated adducts [M + H]<sup>+</sup> with <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values of 331.1906 (C<sub>20</sub>H<sub>27</sub>O<sub>4</sub>), 347.1851 (C<sub>20</sub>H<sub>27</sub>O<sub>5</sub>) and
347.2218 (C<sub>21</sub>H<sub>31</sub>O<sub>4</sub>), respectively.
Abietane <strong>1</strong> was annotated as 12,16-epoxy-11,14-dihydroxy-17(15→16)-abeo-abieta-8,11,13-trien-7-one,
a diterpenoid previously reported in Hyptis crassifoli<em>a</em>. Similarly, rosmanol (<strong>2</strong>) and 11,14-dihydroxy-12-methoxyabieta-8,11,13-triene-7-one (<strong>3</strong>) have been described in Hyptis <em>dilatata</em>
 and Hyptis <em>verticillate</em>, respectively. All three metabolites
exhibited neutral losses of water and carbon monoxide [M+H–H<sub>2</sub>O–CO]<sup>+</sup> in their MS/MS fragmentation patterns,
yielding ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 285.1824 (<strong>1</strong>),
<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 301.1766 (<strong>2</strong>), and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 301.2132 (<strong>3</strong>), consistent with their structural
assignments.</p>
<p>The aromatic abietane diterpenoid 11,12,15-trihydroxy-8,11,13-abietatrien-7-one
(<strong>4</strong>) has been previously reported in <em>H</em>. <em>crassifolia</em>. This
compound was detected as a sodium adduct ion [M + Na]<sup>+</sup>,
with an observed <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> of 355.1871,
corresponding to the molecular formula C<sub>20</sub>H<sub>28</sub>O<sub>4</sub>Na. Compound <strong>5</strong>, with an observed <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> of 347.2218 and a calculated molecular
formula of C<sub>21</sub>H<sub>30</sub>O<sub>4</sub>, was the only
abietane in this study to yield a dehydrated ion [M-H<sub>2</sub>O+H]<sup>+</sup>. The base peak was detected at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 329.2062 (C<sub>21</sub>H<sub>29</sub>O<sub>3</sub>),
indicating a prominent loss of water. This compound was annotated
as 12-methoxycarnosic acid, previously described in <em>H</em>. <em>martiusii</em>.
</p>
<p>Metabolites <strong>6–9</strong> were annotated as <em>para</em>-quinone abietanes diterpenoids, characterized by carbonyl substitutions
at the C-11 and C-14 positions. Some compounds, such as <strong>8</strong>, also exhibited 6,7-unsaturation. Compound <strong>8</strong> generated protonated adducts [M + H]<sup>+</sup>, whereas
compounds <strong>6</strong>,
<strong>7</strong>, and <strong>9</strong>
 were detected as sodium adducts [M + Na]<sup>+</sup>. These
abietanes displayed limited fragmentation in their MS/MS spectra,
a feature commonly associated with the stability of the quinonoid
structure. Compound <strong>6</strong> was detected at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 383.2195 [M + Na]<sup>+</sup>, corresponding
to the molecular formula C<sub>22</sub>H<sub>32</sub>O<sub>4</sub>Na. Structurally similar to royleanone (<strong>7</strong>), it features
an ethoxy substituent at C-7 and was annotated as 7α-ethoxyroyleanone,
previously reported in <em>Hyptis camaroides</em>.
</p>
<p>Metabolites <strong>7</strong> and <strong>8</strong> were classified as
royleanone abietanes, differing in their degree of unsaturation and
hydroxylation patterns at positions C-6, C-7, and C-12. Compound <strong>7</strong>, annotated as royleanone, was observed at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 339.1938 ([M + Na]<sup>+</sup>, C<sub>20</sub>H<sub>28</sub>O<sub>3</sub>Na), whereas compound <strong>8</strong>,
identified as 6,7-dehydroroyleanone, was detected at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 315.1952 ([M + H]<sup>+</sup>, C<sub>20</sub>H<sub>27</sub>O<sub>3</sub>). Both compounds have been previously
isolated from <em>H</em>. <em>verticillate</em>.
</p>
<p>Compound <strong>9</strong>, an isomer of
compound <strong>7</strong>,
differs in the hydroxyl group position, located at C-12 (ring C) in
compound <strong>7</strong> and at C-7 (ring B) in compound <strong>9</strong>. It was detected at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 339.1929
as a sodium adduct and annotated as 7β-hydroxy-11,14-dioxoabieta-8,12-diene
(C<sub>20</sub>H<sub>28</sub>O<sub>3</sub>Na). This metabolite has
also been reported in H. martiusii.
</p>
<p>Compound <strong>10</strong> was classified as a rearranged abietane
diterpenoid, distinguished by a spiro-fused tricyclic skeleton with
a spiro linkage at C-9. It was detected
as a sodium adduct ion [M + Na]<sup>+</sup>, with an observed <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> of 341.2076 and a calculated molecular
formula of C<sub>20</sub>H<sub>30</sub>O<sub>3</sub>Na. This compound
was annotated as martiusane, a rare diterpene previously reported
in <em>H</em>. <em>martiusii</em>.
</p>
<p>Aromatic abietanes represent the most dominant class
of abietane
diterpenoids, consistent with their reported prevalence. These compounds
are characterized by an aromatic C-ring and are frequently involved
in plant chemical defense mechanisms. In this study, all annotated aromatic abietanes were hydroxylated
at the C-11 position, a structural feature that may influence their
biological activity.</p>
<p>Except for compound <strong>4</strong>, the
aromatic abietanes produced
abundant and informative MS/MS fragment ions. Several compounds exhibited
conserved fragmentation patterns, commonly involving neutral losses
of water (18 Da), carbon monoxide (28 Da), and an additional 46 Da
fragment, potentially corresponding to formic acid (HCOOH) or another
carboxyl-derived moiety. Detailed mass spectral data for all compounds
acquired in positive ionization mode are provided in <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.5c07637/suppl_file/ao5c07637_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S1</a>.</p></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p>In this study, the bioherbicidal
potential of plant extracts from <em>Hyptis</em> species was
evaluated against weed species of the <em>Amaranthus</em> genus.
The extracts exhibited significant phytotoxic
effects, evidenced by reductions in seed germination and seedling
growth, indicating the presence of potent allelochemicals. Multivariate
statistical analyses corroborated the biological data, effectively
distinguishing the most active extracts from the less effective ones.
Metabolomic profiling revealed 56 secondary metabolites, primarily
terpenoid, along with flavonoid glycosides, chalcones, and lactones.</p>
<p>These results highlight the potential of <em>Hyptis</em>-based bioherbicides as sustainable and environmentally friendly
alternatives to synthetic herbicides. Future research should focus
on isolating and characterizing the bioactive constituents, elucidating
their mechanisms of action, and evaluating their efficacy and safety
in practical applications. Specifically, field trials are needed to
assess herbicidal performance under agricultural conditions, and crop
toxicity tests should be conducted to ensure selectivity and safety
for nontarget plants.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Experimental Section</h2>
<section id="sec4.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Plant Material</h3>

do Leverger, Mato Grosso, Brazil, at coordinates S15°55′53.3″,
W055°56′18.7″; S15°50′30.1″,
W56°04′28.0″; and S15°48′17.9″,
W56°04′47.6″, respectively. <em>H</em>. <em>saxatilis</em> and <em>H</em>. <em>brevipes</em> were collected in Cuiabá, Mato Grosso, Brazil, at S15°31′55.6″,
W56°02′06.7″. All specimens were collected in May
2021. Plant specimens were identified and deposited in the Central
Herbarium of the Federal University of Mato Grosso under the following
registration numbers: <em>H</em>. <em>crenata</em> (UFMT 44471), <em>H</em>. <em>suaveolens</em> (UFMT

<p>Fresh aerial
parts of <em>H</em>. <em>crenata</em> (240 g), <em>H</em>. <em>suaveolens</em> (340 g), <em>H</em>. <em>saxatilis</em> (460 g), <em>H</em>. <em>campestris</em> (230 g), and <em>H</em>. <em>brevipes</em> (360 g) were extracted with 150 mL of ethanol using homogenizer-assisted
extraction. Extractions were performed with an Ultra-Turrax dispersing

were subsequently filtered under reduced pressure, and the extraction
procedure was repeated twice for each plant. The resulting extracts
were combined, ethanol was removed under reduced pressure, and the
residues were lyophilized and stored at −80 °C. The final
extract yields were as follows: <em>H</em>. <em>crenata</em> (<em>
<strong>Hcre</strong>
</em>, 10.080 g, 4.20%), <em>H</em>. <em>suaveolens</em> (<em>
<strong>Hsua</strong>
</em>, 3.330 g, 0.97%), <em>H</em>. <em>saxatilis</em> (<em>
<strong>Hsax</strong>
</em>, 3.083 g, 0.67%), <em>H</em>. <em>campestris</em> (<em>

Assay</h3>
<p>Commercially available <em>Amaranthus</em> seeds
were obtained from Agro Cosmos Company
(Engenheiro Coelho, Brazil). Seeds of <em>A</em>. <em>viridis</em>, <em>A</em>. <em>hybridus</em>,
and <em>A</em>. <em>lividus</em> were surface-sterilized
with sodium hypochlorite and thoroughly rinsed with distilled water.
The sterilized seeds were placed in autoclaved Petri dishes (9 cm
in diameter) lined with germination paper. Each dish was moistened
with 4 mL of distilled water, and 20 seeds were evenly distributed
per dish.</p>
<p>The control group was treated with dimethyl sulfoxide
(DMSO) alone, while the experimental groups received 200 mg/L (200
ppm) solutions of extracts <em>
<strong>Hcre</strong>
</em>, <em>
<strong>Hsua</strong>
</em>, <em>
<strong>Hsax</strong>
</em>, <em>
<strong>Hcam</strong>
</em>, and <em>
<strong>Hbre</strong>
</em>. Each treatment was performed with seven replicates, arranged in
a completely randomized design. The plates were incubated at room
temperature (25–30 °C), and germination was monitored
daily. Seeds were considering germinated when the emerging seedling
reached 2 mm in length. After 7 days, germination percentage as well
as hypocotyl and radicle lengths were recorded, and percent inhibition
was calculated.
</p>
<p>The <strong>GP</strong> was calculated as the ratio of germinated seeds
to the total number of seeds sown. The <strong>GSI</strong> was determined
according to the equation: <strong>GI</strong> = ∑(<em>Gi</em> × <em>I</em>), where <em>Gi</em> represents
the number of germinated seeds on day <em>i</em>, and <em>I</em> is the corresponding day after incubation. The <strong>MGT</strong> was calculated as <strong>MGT</strong> = ∑(<em>Gi</em> × <em>I</em>)/∑(<em>Gi</em>). The <strong>RL</strong> and <strong>HL</strong> of the germinated seedlings were measured
using a digital caliper.
</p>
<p>Data were
analyzed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS software
(version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). When significant differences
were detected (<em>p</em> ≤ 0.01 or <em>p</em> ≤ 0.05), means were compared using Tukey’s Honestly
Significant Difference test.
</p></section><section id="sec4.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">UPLC-MS/MS
Data Acquisition</h3>
<p>The crude extracts of <em>Hyptis</em> were analyzed using a Shimadzu liquid chromatograph
(Tokyo, Japan) coupled to a micrOTOF-Q II mass spectrometer (Bruker
Daltonics, Boston, MA). The system was equipped with an electrospray
ionization (ESI) source and a quadrupole time-of-flight (QTOF) analyzer.</p>
<p>Chromatographic separation was performed on a reverse-phase C18
column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA) maintained
at 35 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic
acid in ultrapure water; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and solvent
B (acetonitrile). The gradient program was as follows: 0–20
min, linear increase from 5% to 100% B; followed by a 3 min hold at
100% B. The injection volume was 1 μL of extract solution in
methanol (1 mg/mL).
</p>
<p>Mass spectra
were acquired in positive ion mode over the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> range of 50 to 1100 Da using data-dependent
acquisition (MS1 and MS2). Instrument parameters were set as follows:
nebulizer gas pressure of 4.5 bar, drying gas flow rate of 9.0 L/min,
capillary voltage of 3500 V, and ion source temperature of 220 °C.
Automated fragmentation was applied to the four most intense ions
in each spectrum, with collision-induced dissociation energies ranging
from 20 to 105 eV depending on the precursor ion. After MS/MS acquisition,
precursor ions were released for subsequent scans. Data were processed
using Compass DataAnalysis 4.3 software (Bruker Daltonics, Boston,
MA).</p></section><section id="sec4.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Molecular Networking and Metabolite Annotation using GNPS</h3>
<p>The acquired MS data were converted from. d to. mzXML format using
MZmine 2.53 software. Data processing
parameters were set according to general chromatographic characteristics:
mass detection (noise level: 1.0 × 10<sup>3</sup>), chromatogram
builder (threshold: 1.0 × 10<sup>4</sup>; <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance: 0,002), deconvolution (threshold: 5%; retention
time (RT) range: 0.02 min; minimum relative height: 15%), deisotoping
(<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance: 5 ppm; RT tolerance:
0.2 min), alignment (join aligner: <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance: 5 ppm; RT tolerance: 0.2 min; weight for <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>: 15, RT: 0.1 min), gap filling (intensity, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> and RT tolerance: 10%, 5 ppm, and 0.2
min, respectively), and annotation (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> and RT tolerance: 5 ppm and 30 min).</p>
<p>Molecular networks (MNs)
were generated using the GNPS platform (<a href="https://gnps.ucsd.edu" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gnps.ucsd.edu</a>) through
the classical molecular networking workflow. UPLC-MS/MS data were processed in positive ionization mode with
the following parameters: cosine similarity score threshold of 0.5,
minimum of six matched fragment ions, parent mass tolerance of 0.02
Da, and fragment ion tolerance of ± 0.05 Da. The maximum molecular
family size was set to 100 nodes. The resulting network spectra were
matched against GNPS spectral libraries. Visualization and network
analysis were carried out in Cytoscape version 3.9.1.

<p>UPLC–MS/MS data sets
of <em>Hyptis</em> extracts in positive ionization mode were
analyzed using multivariate statistical methods, including PCA, PLS-DA,
and Volcano Plot (VP), with MetaboAnalyst 5.0 software (Montreal,
Canada).</p>
<p>Analyses were conducted using a data matrix of 30 samples
× 1895 variables, comprising promising, nonpromising, and blank
samples based on bioherbicide assay results. For PCA and PLS-DA, the
data were sum-normalized, log-transformed, and mean-centered. Blank
samples were excluded from PLS-DA, which was validated using 10-fold
cross-validation. VP were generated using unpaired analysis, a fold-change
threshold of 2.0, comparison direction set as nonpromising versus
promising, and a <em>p</em>-value threshold of 0.1.
</p>
<p>Bioherbicide activity correlations and
VIP scores were obtained
from PLS-DA using four components, while correlations with promising
samples were further analyzed using VP. Metabolites with VIP values
greater than 1.5 and negative log2­(FC) values were selected for annotation.
Heatmaps were generated for data visualization using VIP scores, Euclidean
distance metrics, and the Ward clustering method.</p></section></section><section id="sec13"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
