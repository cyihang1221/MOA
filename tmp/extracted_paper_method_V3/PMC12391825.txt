</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ab0010"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Herbal products are widely used in healthcare due to their therapeutic potential. However, concerns persist among medicinal chemists and regulatory toxicologists regarding their safety and potential toxicity. Botanical products used in herbal formulations requires rigorous risk assessments. In this study, we applied <em>in silico</em> toxicity testing using natural flavouring complexes (NFCs) approach and Toxtree® v3.1.0 software to assess toxicological risk associated with <em>Curtisia dentata</em>, a widely used medicinal plant for treating diseases. We first conducted experimental analysis of the hexane crude extract of <em>C. dentata</em> using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) to identify its chemical constituents. A total of 124 compounds were identified from GC-MS analysis and verified using reputable databases, including PubChem, Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) database, and scientific literature. The Cramer’s toxicity class of the identified components was determined based on their congeneric groups. The identified constituents were then subjected to in silico toxicity evaluation using Toxtree® v3.1.0. Certain constituents, including aniline, 2,6-dimethoxybenzoquinone, and 1-iodododecane triggered structural alerts for genotoxic carcinogenicity and mutagenicity, while phthalic acid, 4-chloro-3-methylphenyl undecyl ester exhibited alerts for non-genotoxic carcinogenicity. While <em>C. dentata</em> has undoubted proven pharmacological effects, further studies should focus on confirming the <em>in silico</em> predictions through targeted <em>in vitro</em> and <em>in vivo</em> studies, particularly for constituents that fired multiple structural alerts.</p>
<section id="keys0005" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> <em>Curtisia dentata</em>, Chemical constituents, Toxicity, Risk assessment, Genotoxicity</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="ab0015"><h2>Graphical Abstract</h2>

</ul></section><section id="sec0005"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0035">Herbal preparations derived from botanicals are complex chemical mixtures, consisting of hundreds or even thousands of compounds from diverse chemical groups. Though medicinal plants used in herbal formulations are assumed to be potent and safe, there have been recent reports that show that some medicinal plants could have possible carcinogenic, mutagenic and other toxic effects, highlighting the need for robust toxicity assessment of botanical products <a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1">[1]</a>, <a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">[2]</a>, <a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">[3]</a>, <a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4">[4]</a>. One of the major challenges in evaluating the safety of botanical mixtures is the lack of comprehensive toxicological data for most of their constituents. Given this, regulatory scientists have explored the use of risk-based screening approaches, such as those developed for natural flavouring complexes (NFCs), to prioritize constituents for further investigation <a href="#bib5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib5">[5]</a>, <a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6">[6]</a>. These frameworks, based on chemical structure and exposure thresholds, enable prioritization of compounds that may pose higher toxicological concern. In traditional contexts such as herbal decoctions or teas, exposure to individual phytochemicals is often low, typically in the sub-milligram to low milligram range per day, which supports the applicability of a flavouring-style framework for first-tier risk prioritization <a href="#bib5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib5">[5]</a>, <a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6">[6]</a>. This approach is particularly useful for identifying constituents with structural alerts or those classified into Cramer toxicity Classes II and III, which may warrant more detailed toxicological investigation.</p>
<p id="p0040">In parallel, increasing restrictions on animal testing, especially for cosmetic and food-related products have driven the development and adoption of non-animal testing methods in toxicology. Among these, computational toxicology (<em>in silico</em> models) has emerged as a valuable alternative, offering efficient and ethically responsible approaches for chemical risk assessment, particularly when experimentally derived toxicity data are unavailable <a href="#bib5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib5">[5]</a>, <a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6">[6]</a>. One such tool, Toxtree®, employs rule-based and decision-tree algorithms to estimate the toxicological potential of chemical substances based on their molecular structure <a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7">[7]</a>. It supports the prediction of various endpoints, including genotoxicity, mutagenicity, carcinogenicity, and Cramer class assignment, using structure–activity relationship (SAR) principles <a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8">[8]</a>, <a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9">[9]</a>. Because of its open-source availability and ease of integration into regulatory workflows, the algorithms encoded in Toxtree have been widely used in chemical risk assessments, including applications under Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals (REACH), food contact materials, and other regulatory frameworks <a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7">[7]</a>, <a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">[10]</a>, <a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">[11]</a>, <a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12">[12]</a>, <a href="#bib13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib13">[13]</a>.</p>
<p id="p0045"><em>Curtisia dentata</em> (Burm.f.) C.A. Sm. is a medicinal plant widely used in Africa by the locals to manage a wide range of diseases, including stomach ailments, diarrhoea, cancer, diabetes, tuberculosis, malaria, and sexually transmitted diseases <a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">[14]</a>, <a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">[15]</a>. <em>C</em>. <em>dentata</em> is recognised for its antioxidant, antimicrobial and anti-verotoxic effects <a href="#bib16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib16">[16]</a>, radical scavenging activity <a href="#bib17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib17">[17]</a>, anticancer <a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18">[18]</a> and antifungal effect <a href="#bib19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib19">[19]</a>. The leaf and stem-bark of <em>C</em>. <em>dentata</em> contain various bioactive constituents, including lupeol, betulinic acid, ursolic acid, 2α-hydroxyursolic acid, betulinaldehyde, tetracosanol, hexadecanoic acid and <em>β</em>-sitosterol <a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20">[20]</a>, <a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21">[21]</a>, <a href="#bib22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib22">[22]</a>. While its isolated compound, lupeol, have been studied for cytotoxic effects <a href="#bib23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib23">[23]</a>, there remains limited systematic evaluation of the overall safety profile of <em>C. dentata</em> as a complex botanical mixture, particularly with respect to its genotoxic and carcinogenic potential.</p>
<p id="p0050">To address this knowledge gap, the present study undertook a combined experimental and computational approach to evaluate the safety of <em>C. dentata</em>. First, we used gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) to identify chemical constituents in the plant’s hexane stem-bark extract. These experimentally identified compounds were then subjected to <em>in silico</em> toxicological assessment using Toxtree®, with a focus on predicting Cramer toxicity classification, genotoxicity, and metabolism. This integrated strategy provides a risk-based preliminary prioritization of compounds in <em>C. dentata</em>, offering insights into potential toxicological hazards and informing the need for future <em>in vitro</em> or <em>in vivo</em> validation studies.</p></section><section id="sec0010"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="sec0015"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Preparation of <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark extract</h3>
<p id="p0055">The plant <em>C. dentata</em> is a protected species in South Africa <a href="#bib22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib22">[22]</a>, <a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24">[24]</a> and is among the most commonly traded medicinal products in Southern African plant markets <a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25">[25]</a>. Thus, <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark (<a href="#fig0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>) was obtained from the popular Faraday Traditional Medicine Market in Johannesburg, South Africa using its isiZulu and isiXhosa name, “<em>Umlahleni</em>”. The plant material was processed and prepared for solvent extraction as previously described to obtain <em>C. dentata</em> hexane crude extract <a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26">[26]</a>.</p>

<figcaption><p>Stem-bark samples of <em>Curtisia dentata</em>.</p></figcaption></figure></section><section id="sec0020"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Identification of chemicals in <em>C. dentata</em> extract</h3>
<p id="p0060">Data on the chemicals present in the <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark extract were determined using gas chromatography-mass spectrometer (GC-MS). 200 mg of hexane crude extract was dissolved in 2 ml of hexane, vortexed for 3 min, and sonicated for 20 min. Then, 0.5 ml of the concentrated supernatant was filtered into GC/LC analytical bottles. To dilute and increase the volume to 1.5 ml for GC-MS analysis, 1 ml of pure hexane was added.</p>
<p id="p0065">GC-MS analysis was performed as previously documented by van Wyk [26] using a Leco® 7890B GC system paired with a Pegasus®4D TOF-MS. Briefly, the crude extract sample was analyzed twice. Compounds were separated using a dual-column setup (main column: 32 m, 250 μm ID, 0.025 μm film; secondary: 0.79 m, 250 μm ID). A 0.2 ml splitless injection was used, with a temperature program starting at 33 °C, increasing to 180 °C at 10 °C/min, then to 220 °C at 40 °C/min. Helium (99.99 %) served as the carrier gas at 1.0 μl/min. Ionization was at 70 eV, with a scan rate of 50 scans/sec over a 60–600 MHz range. Results were reported as normalized percent peak area. Pure hexane was run as a blank control.</p>
<section id="sec0025"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2.1. Criteria for selecting chemical compounds for safety evaluation</h4>
<p id="p0070">Only chemical compounds with an 80 % or higher chromatogram similarity to the mass spectrometer's main database were evaluated. Inclusion was further restricted to compounds with available data from chemical databases, including PubChem <a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27">[27]</a>, HMDB <a href="#bib28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib28">[28]</a>, NIST <a href="#bib29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib29">[29]</a> and/or scientific literature. For constituents identified through GC-MS, publicly available hazard information from databases, such as European Chemicals Agency (ECHA, <a href="https://echa.europa.eu/information-on-chemicals" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://echa.europa.eu/information-on-chemicals</a>; accessed August 1st, 2025), EU REACH regulations (<a href="http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2006/1907/oj" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2006/1907/oj</a>; accessed August 1st, 2025), the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR, <a href="https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/</a>), the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA, <a href="https://www.epa.gov/environmental-topics/chemicals-pesticides-and-toxics-topics" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.epa.gov/environmental-topics/chemicals-pesticides-and-toxics-topics</a>, accessed August 1st, 2025), and ThermoFischer Scientific Safety Data Sheets (<a href="https://www.thermofisher.com/za/en/home/support.html" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.thermofisher.com/za/en/home/support.html</a>) was used to confirm known human health hazards, including classification for carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, reproductive toxicity, and other toxic endpoints. These data were used to contextualize <em>in silico</em> predictions and highlight compounds of concern even when structural alerts were absent.</p></section></section><section id="sec0030"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Safety assessment evaluation</h3>
<p id="p0075">A combination of <em>in silico</em> software (Toxtree – current version v3.1.0.1851) and the stepwise procedure for the safety evaluation of NFCs were used to assess the toxic hazard of the chemicals in the <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark extract. The standard procedure for the safety evaluation of NFCs, initially described <a href="#bib30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib30">[30]</a>, <a href="#bib31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib31">[31]</a>, <a href="#bib32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib32">[32]</a> and later revised by Cohen et al. <a href="#bib33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib33">[33]</a> was applied with minimal modifications. <a href="#fig0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a> depicts the workflow employed.</p>

<figcaption><p>Prioritization framework for preliminary safety assessment of chemical constituents from <em>C. dentata</em> employing NFCs and Toxtree.</p></figcaption></figure><section id="sec0035"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3.1. <em>In silico</em> prediction of toxicity for <em>C. dentata</em> chemicals</h4>
<ul id="li0010" class="list" style="list-style-type:none"><li id="u0030">
<span class="label">I.</span><div class="display-inline p" id="p0085">The potential genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, and metabolic fate of the chemical constituents identified in the extract of <em>C. dentata</em> were evaluated using Toxtree® v3.1.0.1851, a freely available toxicological prediction software that applies expert rule-based decision trees to assess structural alerts and mechanistic toxicology endpoints. We employed the following rulebases within the software:<ul id="li0015" class="list" style="list-style-type:none">
<li id="u0035">
<span class="label">i.</span><div class="display-inline p" id="p0090">Cramer rules for toxicity classification: This rulebase classifies chemicals into three toxicity classes (I–III) based on structural features and metabolic plausibility, using the <em>“</em>Cramer rules with extensions to functional groups<em>”</em> decision tree. This implementation follows the revised Cramer decision tree model, which incorporates additional functional group considerations to enhance the original methodology <a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9">[9]</a>, <a href="#bib34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib34">[34]</a>.</div>
</li>
<li id="u0040">
<span class="label">ii.</span><div class="display-inline p" id="p0095">Genotoxic and non-genotoxic carcinogenicity (Benigni/Bossa rulebase): This module identifies structural alerts associated with deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) reactivity, oxidative stress, or mitogenic activity, which may contribute to genotoxic or non-genotoxic carcinogenicity <a href="#bib35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib35">[35]</a>, <a href="#bib36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib36">[36]</a>.</div>
</li>
<li id="u0045">
<span class="label">iii.</span><div class="display-inline p" id="p0100">
<em>In vitro</em> Ames mutagenicity (<em>Salmonella typhimurium</em>) rulebase: Predicts mutagenic potential based on structural analogs known to yield positive Ames test results. This rulebase serves as a proxy for DNA-damaging potential in bacterial systems <a href="#bib35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib35">[35]</a>, <a href="#bib36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib36">[36]</a>, <a href="#bib37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib37">[37]</a>.</div>
</li>
<li id="u0050">
<span class="label">iv.</span><div class="display-inline p" id="p0105">
<em>In vivo</em> micronucleus assay rulebase: This assesses structural features known to induce chromosomal damage in rodent models <a href="#bib38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib38">[38]</a>.</div>
</li>
<li id="u0055">
<span class="label">v.</span><div class="display-inline p" id="p0110">Protein and DNA binding rulebases: These modules evaluate the potential of chemical moieties to bind covalently to proteins or nucleic acids, which are key initiating events in many adverse outcome pathways, including immunotoxicity and genotoxicity <a href="#bib39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib39">[39]</a>, <a href="#bib40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib40">[40]</a>.</div>
</li>
<li id="u0060">
<span class="label">vi.</span><div class="display-inline p" id="p0115">Cytochrome P450-mediated metabolism module: The CYP450 module predicts oxidative biotransformation by identifying molecular sites susceptible to metabolism based on expert rules and substructure recognition. Primary sites are those most likely to be metabolized due to favourable structural features, while secondary sites are less reactive or accessible. Given the limited metabolic data available for most constituents in the crude extract, the cytochrome P450-mediated drug metabolism model within the software was utilized to predict reactions catalyzed by P450 proteins (CYPs) <a href="#bib41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib41">[41]</a>, <a href="#bib42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib42">[42]</a>.</div>
</li>
</ul>
</div>
</li></ul>

<p id="p0125">Only compounds that did not trigger genotoxicity structural alerts were then assigned into Cramer’s toxicity classes based on their SMILES representations which can then be input into Toxtree® (<a href="#fig0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>). The graphical interface of PubChem (<a href="https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/</a>) was used to access the SMILES codes of the identified chemicals. In the same manner, the compounds were organized into congeneric groups based on their structural and functional similarities. Compounds with shared core scaffolds, functional groups, or biosynthetic origins, such as fatty acids, alkanes, alcohols, and phenolic derivatives were grouped together to facilitate Cramer classification and toxicity interpretation <a href="#bib33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib33">[33]</a>, <a href="#bib43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib43">[43]</a>. Each congeneric group was then assigned a toxicity class. If multiple toxicity classes were present within a group, the highest level of toxicological concern was applied to the entire group <a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9">[9]</a>, <a href="#bib34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib34">[34]</a>, <a href="#bib44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib44">[44]</a>. Following the congeneric group classification approach used for NFCs by Cohen et al. <a href="#bib33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib33">[33]</a>, three additional congeneric groups were proposed based on the chemical data obtained in this study, as outlined below. <a href="#fig0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a> depicts congeneric composition of chemicals identified in <em>C. dentata</em>.</p>
<ul id="li0020" class="list" style="list-style-type:none">
<li id="u0065">
<span class="label">i.</span><p class="display-inline" id="p0130">Terpenoids and related alcohols and esters (Group 37) were separated from Group 19 (which originally contained both aliphatic/aromatic hydrocarbons and terpenoids) to reflect their distinct structural and functional profiles.</p>
</li>
<li id="u0070">
<span class="label">ii.</span><p class="display-inline" id="p0135">Halogenated compounds (Group 38), which were not represented in the existing classification, were grouped together based on the presence of halogen atoms.</p>
</li>
<li id="u0075">
<span class="label">iii.</span><p class="display-inline" id="p0140">Others (Group 39) consists of compounds with structural features that did not fit into any of the previously defined categories, representing a diverse set of uncategorized chemicals.</p>
</li>
</ul>

Estimation of the mean percentage contribution or daily per capita intake (PCI)</h4>
<p id="p0145">Intake estimates were first calculated for each identified compound (µg/person/day). These compound-level intake values are presented in <a href="#sec0100" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>. The daily PCI for each constituent was determined using a case-by-case approach, based on the administered dosage of the herbal preparation and the peak area of the total ion current (% area) for each compound in the mixture. This method assumes equal ionization efficiency across all compounds, making the resulting PCI estimates semi-quantitative expressed in micrograms per person per day (µg/day). For this study, we assumed a traditional intake of <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark extract at a concentration of 0.01 g/ml, administered orally at a standard dose of 250 ml once or twice daily, depending on ailment severity. These parameters were used to estimate the mean % or daily PCI for each congeneric group of identified constituents. The daily PCI for individual chemicals in <em>C. dentata</em> stem bark was thus calculated as follows:</p>
<p id="p0150">Daily PCI per chemical = (0.01 x % area) x 250 (one cup full) per day</p>
<p id="p0155">OR</p>
<p id="p0160">[(0.01 x % area) x 250] x 2 (two cups full) per day</p>
<p id="p0165">It is important to note that these estimates only apply to volatile and semi-volatile compounds identified via GC-MS following hexane extraction. As hexane preferentially extracts lipophilic constituents and is not representative of traditional aqueous or ethanolic preparations, the calculated intake values may not reflect actual human exposure during typical medicinal use of <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark. These values therefore represent a preliminary, extract-specific estimate of potential exposure for risk prioritization purposes.</p></section><section id="sec0050"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3.4. Estimation of threshold of toxicological concern (TTC) levels</h4>
<p id="p0170">We assessed whether the aggregate intake of each congener group remained below the TTC threshold assigned to its toxic potency class. This group-level comparison provides a preliminary, protective screening approach in line with TTC principles applied to botanical products and natural flavouring complexes. While this framework enables prioritization in the absence of full toxicological data, it may be refined in future studies by incorporating individual Health-Based Exposure Limits (HBELs) where available.</p>
<p id="p0175">A body weight of 60 kg was assumed for an adult individual, consistent with standard practice in regulatory toxicology (e.g., ECHA), to enable conversion of TTC values from µg/kg/day to total daily intake levels. For an individual (60 kg), the given thresholds are as follows: Class I – 30.0 μg/kg/day, Class II – 9.0 μg/kg/day, and Class III – 1.5 μg/kg/day <a href="#bib45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib45">[45]</a>, <a href="#bib46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib46">[46]</a>. Importantly, we initially calculated the daily per capita intake (PCI) for each compound identified by GC-MS using the formula described in <a href="#sec0045" class="usa-link">2.3.3</a>. This produced an estimated intake in µg/person/day per constituent, which we then aggregated to derive the total daily intake per congeneric group (<a href="#sec0100" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>). These values were then compared to the appropriate TTC threshold for the group’s assigned Cramer toxicity class.</p>
<p id="p0180">For constituents that triggered structural alerts for genotoxic carcinogenicity, we recognize that these require a more conservative TTC value of 0.0025 µg/kg/day (0.15 µg/day for a 60 kg adult) <a href="#bib46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib46">[46]</a>. These compounds were flagged in <a href="#sec0100" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>. For example, aniline was flagged as genotoxic and its predicted daily intake (PCI) was compared against the 0.15 μg/day TTC, rather than the default Class III threshold. Prior to assessment, constituents were thoroughly examined to identify if any chemical falls under the TTC exclusion categories. These categories include inorganic substances, proteins, nanomaterials, radioactive substances, organosilicon compounds, metals in organic, ionic, or elemental forms, high-potency carcinogens, steroids, and substances with potential for bioaccumulation <a href="#bib45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib45">[45]</a>, <a href="#bib46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib46">[46]</a>.</p></section></section></section><section id="sec0055"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. <p id="p0200">The <em>in silico</em> prediction of the safety of <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark was assessed using Toxtree, and the structural alerts for various toxicological concerns related to the extract of <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark are shown in <a href="#tbl0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>. These include genotoxic and non-genotoxic carcinogenicity, <em>S. typhimurium</em> mutagenicity (Ames test), <em>in vivo</em> micronucleus assay in rodents, as well as protein-binding and DNA-binding alerts. The identified chemical constituents fall under toxicity classes III and II.</p>
<p id="p0205">X; alerts triggered</p></section></section><section id="sec0070"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Discussion</h2>
<p id="p0210"><em>C. dentata</em> is a medicinal plant that is commonly used as alternative therapies to treat a myriad of diseases. However, there are very little data available on the potential toxicity of the species. While several constituents of <em>C. dentata</em> have been previously reported, comprehensive safety data for most are lacking, particularly with respect to genotoxicity and metabolic fate among others. To address these gaps, we employed, in this study, the <em>in-silico</em> genotoxicity assessment using Toxtree® v3.1.0 to provide an initial, mechanistically informed screening of the chemical constituents of <em>C. dentata</em> for potential toxicological liabilities. The selected rulebases covering endpoints such as bacterial mutagenicity (Ames test), chromosomal damage (micronucleus assay), DNA-binding potential, and carcinogenicity alerts (Benigni–Bossa rules) are aligned with well-defined biological endpoints relevant to genotoxicity <a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7">[7]</a>. These algorithms are based on curated toxicophore libraries and structural rules, enabling transparent, reproducible predictions, which comply with key elements of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) principles for Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship (QSAR) model <a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">[10]</a>, <a href="#bib35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib35">[35]</a>, <a href="#bib47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib47">[47]</a>.</p>
<p id="p0215">There are very little data available on the annual volume of use of <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark in Southern Africa ethnomedicine. Here, the approach used to estimate the daily PCI of individual constituents in the chemical mixture enabled the determination of each chemical's daily PCI, followed by the calculation of the daily PCI for the corresponding congeneric groups. For each of the individual non-genotoxic constituents in the <em>C. dentata</em> chemical profile, the PCI is below the daily TTC levels for their respective toxicity classes (<a href="#sec0100" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>). The TTC values for Cramer toxicological classes I, II and III are 30 µg/kg body weight (bw) per day, 9 µg/kg bw per day, and 1.5 µg/kg bw per day, respectively <a href="#bib44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib44">[44]</a>, <a href="#bib45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib45">[45]</a>. Since all constituents in <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark are consumed simultaneously in a single oral dose, potential additive or synergistic effects cannot be ruled out. While each constituent may fall below its respective TTC threshold, the combined toxicological impact of co-exposure could pose a higher risk than predicted by individual assessments alone.</p>
<p id="p0220">The Joint Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) published monographs (<a href="https://inchem.org/pages/jecfa.html" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://inchem.org/pages/jecfa.html</a>) on related congeneric groups found in the <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark. Metabolic data for Group 19 compounds and related terpenoid hydrocarbons (Group 37) were published by the Flavour and Extract Manufacturers Association (FEMA) Expert Panel <a href="#bib48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib48">[48]</a>, while metabolic data for halogenated compounds were published by Cheeseman et al. <a href="#bib49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib49">[49]</a>. In this study, CYP-mediated metabolic sites and reactions of the identified <em>C. dentata</em> constituents were predicted. Although genotoxicity alerts often account for metabolic activation, this additional metabolic simulation was conducted to provide a complementary perspective and to explore possible bioactivation mechanisms underlying toxicity. Among the metabolic reactions catalysed by CYP enzymes for chemicals in the <em>C. dentata</em> extract are aliphatic and aromatic hydroxylation, epoxidation, oxidation, and de-alkylation (<a href="#sec0100" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>). These metabolic pathways may influence toxicological outcomes, particularly genotoxicity. For instance, epoxidation, a reaction known to generate electrophilic, potentially carcinogenic intermediates, was predicted for 2,6-dimethoxybenzoquinone, which also triggered a structural alert for genotoxic carcinogenicity <a href="#bib50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib50">[50]</a>, <a href="#bib51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib51">[51]</a>. Other genotoxic compounds, such as chloromethanesulfonyl chloride were associated with alternative predicted pathways including hydroxylation and oxidation (<a href="#tbl0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a> and <a href="#sec0100" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>). This suggests that genotoxic potential may arise through multiple biotransformation routes, highlighting the value of integrating metabolic predictions with structural alerts when prioritizing constituents in botanical risk assessment.</p>
<p id="p0225">It is generally assumed that constituents in Cramer Class I have simple chemical structures and efficient metabolism, leading to low oral toxicity. However, it is important to acknowledge that Cramer Class I classification does not guarantee safety in all cases. For instance, some constituents of <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark extract classified as Class I (<a href="#sec0100" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>), such as butyrolactone, phenol, and 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol are listed as hazardous by the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA), with butyrolactone specifically flagged as a neurotoxin and emerging public health concern <a href="#bib52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib52">[52]</a>, <a href="#bib53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib53">[53]</a>. It is important to note that the Cramer classification does not define specific hazard types (e.g., mutagenicity or neurotoxicity) but instead reflects an assumed level of toxicological concern based on structural complexity and potential potency. These classes are used to derive conservative exposure thresholds within the TTC framework <a href="#bib46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib46">[46]</a>. While Class II and III compounds are generally considered to pose greater toxicological risks under the TTC framework, the presence of ECHA-listed hazardous constituents within Class I highlights an important research gap that warrants further investigation for a more comprehensive safety assessment.</p>
<p id="p0230">Many constituents assigned to toxicological class II had protein-binding and/or DNA-binding structural alerts (<a href="#tbl0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>), however, 1-octanol, 2-butyl (2-butyloctanol) had no protein- or DNA- structural alerts but showed structural alerts for non-genotoxic carcinogenicity based on the identification of its structure as a nongenotoxic substituted n-alkylcarboxylic acid (<a href="#tbl0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). All except one constituent assigned to Class III showed either protein-binding or DNA-binding structural alerts, or both, with the identification of Michael acceptors and nucleophilic substitution type 2 (SN<sub>2</sub>) as the major causal factors (<a href="#tbl0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). Chemical constituents in the hexane crude extract of <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark assigned to Class III which are of toxicological concern include aniline, 1-iodododecane, 2,6-dimethoxybenzoquinone and ursolic aldehyde as these constituents showed structural alerts in all the software rulebases (<a href="#tbl0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). Chloromethanesulfonyl chloride fired structural alerts for genotoxic carcinogenicity, for the <em>in vivo</em> micronucleus assay in rodents, protein-binding and DNA-binding alerts. The compound phthalic acid, 4-chloro-3-methylphenyl undecyl ester showed structural alerts for nongenotoxic carcinogenicity, for the micronucleus assay in rodents, protein-binding and DNA-binding alerts, but there were no structural alerts for <em>in vitro S. typhimurium</em> mutagenicity (Ames test).</p>
<p id="p0235">Even though daily PCI for individual non-genotoxic constituents in <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark is below the daily TTC for their respective toxicity classes, the level of concern changes when intake per congeneric group, as well as intake per toxicological class for both single- and double-dose administrations are considered. For congeneric groups assigned Class I toxicity classes (Groups 1, 3, 5, 7, 14, 20 and 23), both the daily single- and double dose PCI per congeneric group are below the daily TTC value of 1800 µg/person/day (<a href="#tbl0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>S). Similarly. for congeneric groups assigned Class II toxicological classes (Groups 2, 16, 25 and 28), both the daily single- and double-dose PCI per congeneric group are below the daily TTC value of 540 µg/person/day. Congeneric groups 8, 10, 12, 17, 19, 30, 37 and 38 were assigned Class III toxicological classes. The TTC value for Class III is 90 µg/person/day <a href="#bib44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib44">[44]</a>, <a href="#bib45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib45">[45]</a>. For these congeneric groups, the daily PCI for both the single and double dosages per congeneric group are above the TTC value for Class III (<a href="#tbl0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>S).</p>
<p id="p0240">Furthermore, aniline is a Class III constituent and an organic compound classified as a suspected carcinogen <a href="#bib54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib54">[54]</a>. Certain constituents, including aniline and 2,6-dimethoxybenzoquinone, triggered structural alerts for genotoxic carcinogenicity in Toxtree. For such compounds, the standard Cramer Class TTC thresholds are not appropriate. Instead, following well-established regulatory guidance (e.g., European Food Safety Authority (EFSA)), a conservative TTC value of 0.0025 µg/kg/day (0.15 µg/day for a 60 kg adult) is recommended <a href="#bib46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib46">[46]</a>.</p>
<p id="p0245">It is also important to consider that a complex herbal mixture, such as <em>C. dentata</em> extract, contains numerous constituents, which may influence the toxicity of other components <a href="#bib55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib55">[55]</a>. During biotransformation, interactions among different components can be inhibitory, additive, or synergistic. Additionally, the concentrations and composition of constituents in a given botanical species may fluctuate based on seasonal changes, geographical region, and even within the same species growing in the same location <a href="#bib22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib22">[22]</a>. The specific time and region the material is harvested for the preparation of an ethnomedicine would therefore affect the concentrations of the respective constituents consumed. Periods of exposure (POE) is another significant factor. In countries where ethnomedicines are key to primary healthcare, long-term exposure to their chemicals raises concerns due to intermittent use throughout life.</p></section><section id="sec0075"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">5. Study limitations</h2>
<p id="p0250">One limitation of this study is the use of a hexane extract for estimating per capita intake (PCI) and conducting the subsequent risk assessment. Hexane, a non-polar solvent, selectively extracts lipophilic compounds, potentially leading to a chemical profile that does not accurately represent more commonly consumed preparations such as aqueous infusions or 30 % ethanol tinctures. Although hexane extraction may serve as a case scenario for toxicity evaluation, future studies can improve on this work by employing extraction methods that better simulate typical human exposure routes to enhance the safety assessment. It is important to note that GC-MS primarily detects volatile and thermally stable constituents, and therefore, the chemical profile presented in this study does not capture nonvolatile, thermolabile, or inorganic compounds. Potential limitations arising from compounds labeled as “unknown” due to the absence of definitive structural information, as well as from undetected constituents, are acknowledged; however, these are considered to fall within the conservative safety margins provided by the TTC-based approach. Additional techniques such as LC-MS would be required to fully characterize the broader spectrum of constituents present in <em>C. dentata</em> extract.</p></section><section id="sec0080"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">6. Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0255"><em>Curtisia dentata</em> (Burm. f.) C.A. Sm is a widely used medicinal plant in Southern African traditional medicine. This study applied <em>in silico</em> toxicological risk assessment methods, aligned with approaches used for natural flavouring complexes (NFCs), to evaluate the potential safety risks of its stem-bark extract. Although the estimated daily intake (PCI) of non-genotoxic constituents was below their respective TTC thresholds, which indicates a low level of concern, several constituents of the <em>C. dentata</em> stem-bark extract, including aniline, 2,6-dimethoxybenzoquinone, 1-iodododecane, and ursolic aldehyde activated structural alerts associated with genotoxicity, mutagenicity, and carcinogenicity. While these findings do not confirm toxicity, they suggest that <em>C. dentata</em>-based herbal formulations may contain components of toxicological concern. Moreover, limitations related to the analytical method (GC-MS), solvent system (hexane), and polar or non-volatile constituents mean the risk assessment may not be comprehensive. Based on these results, this study underscores the need for further confirmatory testing, particularly for compounds flagged by <em>in silico</em> alerts, to support a more complete toxicological profile of <em>C. dentata</em>.</p></section><section id="sec0085"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CRediT authorship contribution statement</h2>
