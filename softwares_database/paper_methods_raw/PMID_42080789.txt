
Methods
Thirteen children aged 6 to 16 years (mean = 9.5 years, 9 girls)
were surgically treated for bilateral congenital cataracts as part of
Project Prakash in India. They were examined at 5 longitudinal time
points: presurgery and 1 week, 1 month, 6 months, and 1 year
postsurgery. Eye movements were recorded with a smartphone
camera and macro lens. Pupil trajectories were extracted using
computer vision techniques, analyzed in the frequency domain, and
a nystagmus magnitude metric was defined as the mean magnitude
of horizontal (0–5 hertz [Hz]) movements. This metric was
examined longitudinally, and additional comparisons were made
with 14 separate patients examined 4 years after surgery.
Results
Nystagmus magnitude in the longitudinal group decreased
significantly (1.7-fold, P = 0.006) during the first postsurgical year.
Cross-sectional comparisons indicate stabilization by approximately
1 year postoperatively. Variability across individuals was not
explained by visual acuity, age at surgery, or eye axial length.
Binocular recordings revealed strong interocular coordination
despite persistent nystagmus.
Conclusions
Sight restoration after prolonged congenital blindness yields
significant but incomplete reductions in nystagmus, indicating
notable but circumscribed neural plasticity in the oculomotor
system beyond infancy. In addition to demonstrating the feasibility
of smartphone-based quantification of nystagmus in resource-
limited settings, these results reinforce the importance of early
visual input for calibrating eye movements and highlight important
paths for rehabilitation.
Keywords: nystagmus, congenital blindness, late sight onset, visual
development, computational analysis
Nystagmus is characterized by involuntary rhythmic movements of
the eyes.1 It can manifest in the first months of life as a result of
several conditions, such as congenital cataracts, albinism, or retinal
diseases, but may also be idiopathic. Nystagmus can also be
acquired later in life and may occur in association with neurological
disorders.1,2 Underlying nystagmus are disruptions in visual and
oculomotor systems, impairing stable visual fixation and gaze
control, resulting in oscillatory eye movements, often at frequencies
up to approximately 5 hertz (Hz).3–5
In individuals treated for early-onset blindness, such as due to
bilateral congenital cataracts, the presence of nystagmus is well-
established.6–10 In fact, the linkage between early visual
deprivation and the development of nystagmus is so pronounced
that the presence of nystagmus in an older blind child can serve as a
useful clue to infer that the child's visual deprivation had onset at or
near birth. Notwithstanding this categorical linkage, quantitative
data on the longitudinal progression of nystagmus following sight
surgery are lacking. Such data could have far-reaching implications
because longitudinal changes in nystagmus would provide evidence
of plasticity within oculomotor control systems late into childhood.
In this study, we longitudinally examined the status of nystagmus in
a unique group of children who were born blind and subsequently
gained vision through surgical intervention as part of Project
Prakash.11 Studying these children allowed us to address three
research questions. First, does the intensity of nystagmus diminish
following surgery, and if so, by when do improvements asymptote
postsurgery? Second, to what extent can interindividual differences
in nystagmus intensity be accounted for by factors such as visual
acuity, age, or ocular morphology? Finally, are the neural
generators of nystagmus in the Prakash children eye-specific or
bilateral, leading to coordinated movement trajectories of the two
eyes? Whereas nystagmus, including its infantile forms, usually
involves conjugate eye movements,12–14 there are important
exceptions. For instance, in see-saw nystagmus, often linked to
tumors or lesions, one eye elevates as the other depresses, thereby
inducing inherently disconjugate movements.15,16 Similarly, in
spasmus nutans, which presents early in life, high-frequency
oscillations vary between conjugate and disconjugate forms.17 In
addition, strabismus introduces disconjugacy even during normal
saccadic eye movements.18,19 Thus, whereas we expected the
Prakash children to exhibit predominantly conjugate eye
movements, as in classic forms of infantile nystagmus, we sought to
confirm this empirically.
In answering these questions, we also sought to address a
methodological goal. Specifically, we demonstrate here the utility of
frequency-based analyses derived from simple camera recordings
for quantifying nystagmus. Although computational methods
capable of accurately doing so exist, they typically require
expensive, specialized equipment, such as infrared eye trackers or
electronystagmography.20,21 Simple camera-based analyses,
although inadequate for some of the more detailed
characterizations, hold great promise for fieldwork or resource-
limited clinical settings in which specialized equipment is
unavailable.

Participant Information
Our longitudinal patient group consisted of 13 individuals who, as
part of Project Prakash, underwent surgery for congenital blindness
due to bilateral congenital cataracts at ages 6 to 16 (mean age =
9.5 years, 9 girls). Detailed patient data can be found in Table 1,
and a qualitative, clinical description of individuals’ nystagmus is
provided in Supplementary Table S1. Each participant was
examined at five longitudinal time points: a few days prior to
surgery (preoperative) as well as approximately 1 week, 1 month, 6
months, and 1 year postoperatively (1–4).

14 Prakash patients (mean age at the time of surgery = 12.9 years;
6 girls; see Table 2 for details and Supplementary Table S2 for

restoring surgery at least 4 years earlier, performed by the same
surgeon and based on the same surgical protocol. This group served
to assess whether the longitudinal reduction in nystagmus observed
during the first postsurgical year plateaued or continued with
extended visual experience.
