<p>What metabolic pathways and metabolites in the serum and follicular fluid are associated with peak estradiol levels and the number of mature oocytes?</p></section><section id="s2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">SUMMARY ANSWER</h3>
<p>In the serum metabolome, mostly fatty acid and amino acid pathways were associated with estradiol levels and mature oocytes while in the follicular fluid metabolome, mostly lipid, vitamin, and hormone pathways were associated with peak estradiol levels and mature oocytes.</p></section><section id="s3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">WHAT IS KNOWN ALREADY</h3>
<p>Metabolomics has identified several metabolic pathways and metabolites associated with infertility but limited data are available for ovarian stimulation outcomes.</p></section><section id="s4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">STUDY DESIGN, SIZE, DURATION</h3>
<p>A prospective cohort study of women undergoing IVF from 2009 to 2015.</p></section><section id="s5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">PARTICIPANTS/MATERIALS, SETTING, METHODS</h3>
<p>A total of 125 women undergoing a fresh IVF cycle at a fertility clinic in the Northeast United States who provided a serum and follicular fluid sample. Untargeted metabolomics profiling was conducted using liquid chromatography with high-resolution mass spectrometry in two chromatography columns (C18 and hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC)). The main ovarian stimulation outcomes were peak serum estradiol levels and number of mature oocytes. We utilized adjusted generalized linear regression models to identify significant metabolic features. Models were adjusted for age,BMI, initial infertility diagnosis, and ovarian stimulation protocol. We then conducted pathway analysis using mummichog and metabolite annotation using level-1 evidence.</p></section><section id="s6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">MAIN RESULTS AND ROLE OF CHANCE</h3>
<p>In the serum metabolome, 480 and 850 features were associated with peak estradiol levels in the C18 and HILIC columns, respectively. Additionally, 437 and 538 features were associated with mature oocytes in the C18 and HILIC columns, respectively. In the follicular fluid metabolome, 752 and 929 features were associated with peak estradiol levels in the C18 and HILIC columns, respectively, Additionally, 993 and 986 features were associated with mature oocytes in the C18 and HILIC columns, respectively. The most common pathways associated with peak estradiol included fatty acids (serum and follicular fluid), hormone (follicular fluid), and lipid pathways (follicular fluid). The most common pathways associated with the number of mature oocytes retrieved included amino acids (serum), fatty acids (serum and follicular fluid), hormone (follicular fluid), and vitamin pathways(follicular fluid). The vitamin D3 pathway had the strongest association with both ovarian stimulation outcomes in the follicularfluid. Four and nine metabolites were identified using level-1 evidence (validated identification) in the serum and follicular fluid metabolomes, respectively.</p></section><section id="s7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LIMITATIONS, REASONS FOR CAUTION</h3>
<p>Our sample was majority White and highly educated and may not be generalizable to thewider population. Additionally, residual confounding is possible and the flushing medium used in the follicular fluid could have diluted our results.</p></section><section id="s8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">WIDER IMPLICATIONS OF THE FINDINGS</h3>
<p>The pathways and metabolites identified by our study provide novel insights into the biologicalmechanisms in the serum and follicular fluid that may underlie follicular and oocyte development, which could potentially be used to improve ovarian stimulation outcomes.</p></section><section id="s9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">STUDY FUNDING/COMPETING INTEREST(S)</h3>

The authors have no competing interests to disclose.</p></section><section id="s10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">TRIAL REGISTRATION NUMBER</h3>
<p>N/A.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> follicular fluid metabolome, estradiol level, total oocyte, mature oocyte, fertilized oocytes</p></section></section><section id="sec11"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>In the USA, 15% of women 15–44 years old are estimated to be infertile, defined as not being able to conceive after 6 months or 1 year or more of unprotected sex (depending on maternal age) (<a href="#dead189-B48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B48">Thoma <em>etal.</em>, 2013</a>). While it is unclear whether the incidence of infertility is increasing, more American women are delaying pregnancy as evidenced by the rising age at first birth (<a href="#dead189-B33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B33">Mathews and Hamilton, 2016</a>). Because more women are attempting pregnancy later and age is the strongest risk factor for infertility (<a href="#dead189-B47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B47">teVelde and Pearson, 2002</a>), we would inevitably expect that more women would face issues trying to conceive and thus turn to ART. ART is one of the most common treatments for infertility. Since the first successful US live birth was achieved with ART in 1981, its use has increased (<a href="#dead189-B45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B45">Sunderam <em>etal.</em>, 2022</a>) such that now over 300 000 cycles are performed each year and over 2% of all infants born are a result of ART in the US (<a href="#dead189-B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B5">CDC, 2021</a>). While the success rate of ART has steadily improved, still only 37.2% of ART cycles result in a live birth (<a href="#dead189-B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B5">CDC, 2021</a>).</p>
<p>Among factors contributing to ART failure are both poor oocyte yield and low oocyte quality, thus limiting availability of high-quality blastocysts for transfer and cryopreservation. Additionally, estradiol levels, another common ovarian stimulation outcome, have been positively associated with higher pregnancy rates (<a href="#dead189-B25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B25">Kondapalli <em>etal.</em>, 2012</a>; <a href="#dead189-B51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B51">Wei <em>etal.</em>, 2023</a>) and live birth (<a href="#dead189-B25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B25">Kondapalli <em>etal.</em>, 2012</a>). Therefore, better understanding of the biological mechanisms that underlie response to ovarian stimulation and the increase in estradiol levels and the production of high-quality oocytes, will invariably improve IVF success rates and enhance clinical care (<a href="#dead189-B50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B50">Vaiarelli <em>etal.</em>, 2018</a>; <a href="#dead189-B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B11">Di Guardo <em>etal.</em>, 2022</a>). One method to identify underlying biological mechanisms and potential biomarkers is metabolomics. Metabolomics is the study of small molecules that are the end result of gene expression and metabolic processes. Metabolomics has been utilized extensively to identify biomarkers of reproductive diseases including different types of female subfertility with both serum and follicular fluid samples (<a href="#dead189-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B2">Baskind <em>etal.</em>, 2011</a>; <a href="#dead189-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B10">de la Barca <em>etal.</em>, 2017</a>; <a href="#dead189-B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B4">Castiglione Morelli <em>etal.</em>, 2019</a>; <a href="#dead189-B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B12">Dogan <em>etal.</em>, 2020</a>). While there is growing interest in using metabolomics to identify biomarkers for IVF success, few rigorous studies have been conducted to date (<a href="#dead189-B35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B35">McRae <em>etal.</em>, 2013</a>). In a small study investigating serum markers of ovarian response, 30 patients were classified as poor, normal, and hyper responders and researchers were able to identify ten metabolites that were capable of distinguishing between different response levels (<a href="#dead189-B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B3">Borges <em>etal.</em>, 2019</a>). Beyond this study, however, limited data are available on metabolomic markers of ovarian response and underlying mechanisms.</p>
<p>To address the current dearth of knowledge, we utilized two biological matrices, serum and follicular fluid, to identify metabolites and metabolic pathways associated with peak estradiol levels and the number of mature oocytes retrieved. Both serum and follicular fluid were used because we have previously shown that these samples have correlated but distinct metabolomes (<a href="#dead189-B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B19">Hood <em>etal.</em>, 2022b</a>). The goal of this project was to build on our previous descriptive work to identify the unique metabolic signatures in the follicular fluid and serum that are associated with ovarian stimulation outcomes. Identifying metabolites associated with an ideal response to controlled ovarian stimulation will help us better understand the pathways that contribute to and can be potentially optimized for improving oocyte quality, including possible targets for future therapeutic intervention. We hypothesized that more metabolomic features would be associated with the ovarian stimulation outcomes in the follicular fluid compared to the serum.</p></section><section id="sec12"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study participants</h3>
<p>Participants were part of the Environmental and Reproductive Health (EARTH) study (<a href="#dead189-B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B36">Messerlian <em>etal.</em>, 2018</a>). Briefly, the EARTH study was a prospective cohort study that examined how dietary and environmental factors influence fertility. Women attending the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) Fertility Center (2005–2019) for infertility evaluation or treatment were invited to participate. At enrollment, participants completed a questionnaire about their diet, environmental exposures, and reproductive health. Participants also had their height and weight measured by trained study staff to calculate BMI (kg/m<sup>2</sup>). The Institutional Review Boards at MGH and the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health approved this study. All participants provided written informed consent.</p>
<p>Two hundred women undergoing a fresh autologous ART cycle between 2005 and 2016 were randomly selected for a serum metabolomics sub-study, as previously described (<a href="#dead189-B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B15">Gaskins <em>etal.</em>, 2021</a>). Women randomly selected for this sub-study were similar in terms of demographic and ART cycle characteristics to all the eligible women (<a href="#dead189-B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B15">Gaskins <em>etal.</em>, 2021</a>). Of these 200 participants, 125 women also had a follicular fluid sample taken from the first follicle that was clear (uncontaminated with blood), which was analyzed for metabolomics. Follicles were considered dominant if they were &gt;16 mm on the last ultrasound performed the day of hCG trigger. During retrieval, fluid was aspirated in individual tubes for study purposes from three of the largest dominant follicles, in no particular order.</p></section><section id="sec14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Outcomes of controlled ovarian stimulation</h3>
<p>Women in the study underwent one of three controlled ovarian stimulation protocols on Day 3 of induced menses after completing a cycle of oral contraceptives: (i) luteal phase GnRH-agonist protocol, (ii) follicular phase GnRH-agonist/Flare protocol, or (iii)GnRH-antagonist protocol. Protocol selection was based on clinical indication with luteal-phased GnRH agonist being used for normal/high responders, follicular phase GnRH agonist/Flare for low responders, and GnRH-antagonists for normal/low responders. Among patients pre-treated with oral contraceptive pills, the GnRH antagonist protocol was started when a dominant follicle reached ≥14 mm or estradiol was &gt;1000 pg/ml. Among patients without pre-treatment with oral contraceptive pills, GnRH antagonist protocol was started when a dominant follicle reached ≥12 mm or estradiol &gt;400 pg/ml. In patients utilizing a GnRH agonist protocol, Lupron (AbbVie Inc., North Chicago, IL, USA) was started in the luteal phase of the preceding cycle and was continued until hCG trigger day. In the Flare protocol, GnRH agonist was administered at a dose of 1 mg between Days 2 and 4 of the cycle and subsequently decreased to 0.25 mg. Stimulation was started on Day 3 per routine. During gonadotropin treatment, women were monitored for serum estradiol levels (Elecsys Estradiol II reagent kit, Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA), follicle size measurements and counts, and endometrial thickness and pattern. hCG was administered intramuscularly ∼35–36 h before the scheduled egg retrieval procedure to induce oocyte maturity. All visible follicles that were accessible by the needle were aspirated. Per study protocols, the first three dominant follicles were chosen for follicular fluid collection. We investigated two markers of response to controlled ovarian stimulation: peak estradiol levels (E<sub>2</sub>) and number of mature oocytes collected. The measurement of these outcomes has been described elsewhere (<a href="#dead189-B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B14">Gaskins <em>etal.</em>, 2019</a>). In brief, peak serum E<sub>2</sub> concentration was defined as the highest level of E<sub>2</sub> preceding the oocyte retrieval and obtained on the day of hCG administration. Following oocyte retrieval, a trained embryologist classified the retrieved oocytes as either germinal vesicle, metaphase I, metaphase II, or degenerated. The number of mature oocytes was the sum of all metaphase II oocytes.</p></section><section id="sec15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Blood and follicular fluid collection</h3>
<p>During gonadotropin treatment, most commonly (70%) on Day 3 of the IVF cycle protocol, ∼6 ml of blood was collected at a routine appointment between 7 and 10 a.m. At blood draw, women were not required to be fasting. Samples allowed to clot for 20–60 min were centrifuged at 1040<em>g</em> for 20 min at room temperature and stored at −20°C at MGH until being transferred to Harvard School of Public Health to be stored at −80°C. During oocyte retrieval, a follicular fluid sample was taken from a woman’s first three follicles with a 16-G needle. Each sample was collected in a separate tube prepared with 1 ml of flushing media. Once the oocytes were removed, the follicular fluid was centrifuged at 1040<em>g</em> for 10 min at room temperature to separate the supernatant and pellet. The resulting aliquots were then stored at −80°C. To reduce variability, we used follicular fluid taken from the first large follicle collected.</p></section><section id="sec16"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolomic analysis</h3>
<p>Serum and follicular fluid supernatant samples were shipped in separate batches, on dry ice, to Emory University for metabolomics analysis. We utilized established protocols for feature extraction that has been previously used for non-fasting samples and has been described elsewhere (<a href="#dead189-B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B16">Go <em>etal.</em>, 2015</a>; <a href="#dead189-B30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B30">Liang <em>etal.</em>, 2018</a>, <a href="#dead189-B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B29">2019</a>; <a href="#dead189-B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B28">Li <em>etal.</em>, 2021</a>; <a href="#dead189-B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B19">Hood <em>etal.</em>, 2022b</a>; <a href="#dead189-B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B22">Hwang <em>etal.</em>, 2022</a>). Briefly, samples were treated with two volumes of acetonitrile and were analyzed in triplicate. Samples were randomized prior to analysis to minimize batch effects. Serum and follicular fluid samples were analyzed separately using liquid chromatography with high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS; Dionex Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano; Thermo Orbitrap Fusion). To facilitate greater feature detection and separation, we used two chromatography columns, the C18 hydrophobic chromatography column (Higgins Targa C18; 2.1 × 50 mm<sup>2</sup>; 3-µm particle size) with negative electrospray ionization (ESI) (-30kV) and the hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) column (Waters XBridge BEH Amide XP HILIC; 2.1 × 50 mm<sup>2</sup>; 2.6-µm particle size) with positive electrospray ionization (ESI) (3.5 kV). The sheath and auxiliary gases were set at 30 (arbitrary units) and 5 (arbitrary units) for the C18 column and 45 (arbitrary units) and 25 (arbitrary units) for the HILIC column, respectively. With each batch, we used two quality control samples, NIST 1950 (<a href="#dead189-B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B39">Simón-Manso <em>etal.</em>, 2013</a>) and pooled human plasma (Equitech Bio), which has been described elsewhere (<a href="#dead189-B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B16">Go <em>etal.</em>, 2015</a>; <a href="#dead189-B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B31">Liu <em>etal.</em>, 2020</a>). Briefly, pooled human plasma assisted with batch correction while NIST 1950 samples, across batches, assisted with signal intensity normalization relative to a calibrated reference. Furthermore, inclusion of NIST 1950 samples was used to improve intra- and inter-laboratory comparison. We used ProteoWizard to convert raw data files to .mzML files using apLCMS and xMSanalyzer (<a href="#dead189-B58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B58">Yu<em>etal.</em>, 2009</a>; <a href="#dead189-B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B6">Chambers <em>etal.</em>, 2012</a>; <a href="#dead189-B49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B49">Uppal <em>etal.</em>, 2013</a>). Unique features were characterized based on the mass-to-charge ratio (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em>), retention time, and ion intensity. Features that appeared in &lt;10% of samples were removed. We also removed features with a median coefficient of variation among the triplicates &gt;30% and a Pearson correlation &lt;0.7. Average intensities of the remaining features were natural log-transformed to normalize these data and allow for analysis with conventional statistical methods.</p></section><section id="sec17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h3>
<p>We utilized generalized linear models to identify features associated with each of the two outcomes of controlled ovarian stimulation,</p>

<p>In these models, <em>Y<sub>ji</sub></em> was the natural log of the intensity for feature <em>j</em> and participant <em>i</em>. Oocyte<sub><em>ik</em></sub> was woman <em>i</em>’s controlled ovarian stimulation outcome (e.g. estradiol level or mature oocytes). These models also included the woman’s BMI (BMI<sub><em>i</em></sub>), age (Age<sub><em>i</em></sub>), initial infertility diagnosis (Female<sub><em>i</em></sub>; Unexplained<sub><em>i</em></sub> vs Male), and ovarian stimulation protocol (Protocol<sub><em>i</em></sub>; luteal phase GnRH agonist vs other). <em>E<sub>ij</sub></em> denotes the residual normal error. Covariates were selected based on <em>a priori</em> knowledge and biological relevance. Separate models were used for the C18-negative and HILIC-positive columns. We identified significant features at <em>P</em>-value &lt;0.05. Given the high number of statistical tests, we also corrected these raw <em>P</em>-values for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure at two thresholds (<em>q</em>-value: &lt;0.20 and &lt;0.05). We used volcano plots todescribe the magnitude and statistical significance of the associationsbetween the metabolic features and each controlled ovarian stimulation outcome.</p></section><section id="sec18"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Pathway analysis</h3>
<p>Pathway analysis was completed using mummichog (v. 1.0.10) which has been described and validated elsewhere (<a href="#dead189-B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B27">Li <em>etal.</em>, 2013</a>). Briefly, mummichog computes biological pathways from a feature list using mass-to-charge ratio and retention time without prior metabolite identification. An adjusted <em>P</em>-value for each pathway is calculated by resampling the reference input file using a gamma distribution (<a href="#dead189-B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B27">Li <em>etal.</em>, 2013</a>). We utilized a reference file for each chromatography column (C18 and HILIC) with the file consisting of features with a raw <em>P</em>-value &lt;0.05. We examined pathways with <em>P</em>-values &lt;0.05 for any of the two outcomes and compared the significance and number of matched metabolites in each pathway. In this analysis, pathways could have the same number of overlapping features but different pathway <em>P</em>-values and pathway sizes because of the different number of significant underlying features in the reference file and the differing number of features detected initially. Heat maps were used to visually compare the pathways across each outcome and each biological matrix.</p></section><section id="sec19"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Feature annotation and confirmation</h3>
<p>For chemical annotation of significant metabolic features (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05), we tentatively matched these features to authentic standards analyzed in the same manner and with the same chromatography columns in our lab (<a href="#dead189-B44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B44">Sumner <em>etal.</em>, 2007</a>). Significant features were matched with authentic standards (level-1 evidence) (<a href="#dead189-B44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B44">Sumner <em>etal.</em>, 2007</a>) by comparing their <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>. We further screened annotated features by their retention times and those with retention times that differed by more than 30 s with the authentic standards were excluded. If features matched to multiple metabolites based on m/z and retention time, we selected those with the closest retention times. We further screened these matches on their retention time, isotope patterns, and peak quality by examining the extracted ion chromatograph to further minimize false positive matches. Only those with high-quality peaks were considered high-quality matches. We then used the Human Metabolome Database to identify the superclass and class of each matched metabolite.</p>
<section id="sec20"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Response to ovarian stimulation</h4>
<p>Since poor response and hyper response to ovarian stimulation are not an optimal outcome either, we further evaluated whether there was any evidence of a non-monotonic association between the level-1 metabolites identified and ovarian response categories defined as poor (&lt;4 mature oocytes, n = 9), normal (4–18 mature oocytes, n = 110), and high (&gt;18 mature oocytes, n = 6). We used ANOVA testing to compare the difference in mean concentrations for each metabolite by ovarian response category (α = 0.05).</p></section></section></section><section id="sec21"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="sec22"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample characteristics</h3>
<p>Women included in this sample had a mean age of 35 years (SD: 3.7) and a mean BMI of 24.0 (SD: 4.6) kg/m<sup>2</sup> (<a href="#dead189-T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). Most of the cycles occurred between 2009 and 2012 (62%; n = 77) and the most common initial infertility diagnosis was unexplained (43%; n = 54). The median peak estradiol level was 1968 pmol/l (range: 591–4051 pmol/l) and the median number of mature oocytes retrieved was 9 (range: 1–27). The Spearman correlation between peak estradiol and number of mature oocytes was 0.63.</p>

<p>Peak estradiol levels were associated with eight pathways and ten pathways in the C18 column and the HILIC column, respectively. In the C18 column, two pathways occurred in the serum metabolome and six occurred in the follicular fluid metabolome (<a href="#dead189-T2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). The two serum metabolome pathways were all fatty acid pathways (fatty acid metabolism and polyunsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis). In the follicular fluid, alanine and aspartate metabolism, androgen and estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism, C21-steroid hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, bile acid biosynthesis, prostaglandin formation from dihomo gama-linoleic acid, and vitamin B3 metabolism were associated with peak estradiol levels. In the HILIC column, six pathways occurred in the serum metabolome and six pathways occurred in the follicular fluid metabolome (<a href="#dead189-T3" class="usa-link">Table 3</a>). In the serum metabolome, alkaloid biosynthesis II, phosphatidylinositol phosphate metabolism, biopterin metabolism, and vitamin E metabolism were associated with the peak estradiol levels. In the follicular fluid metabolome, butanoate metabolism, androgen and estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism, C21-steroid hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, and vitamin D3 metabolism were associated with the peak estradiol levels. Between the two columns, androgen and estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism, C21-steroid hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, and bile acid biosynthesis were associated with peak estradiol levels in the follicular fluid metabolome while no pathways associated with peak estradiol levels overlapped in the serum metabolome. Across the two matrices, carnitine shuttle and bile acid biosynthesis were associated with peak estradiol levels in the HILIC column. No overlap was observed between the two matrices in the C18 column.</p>

In the C18 column, seven pathways occurred in the serum metabolome and six pathways occurred in the follicular fluid metabolome (<a href="#dead189-T2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). The seven metabolomic pathways associated with mature oocytes in the serum metabolome included arginine and proline metabolism, aspartate and asparagine metabolism, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, butanoate metabolism, ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, vitamin B9 metabolism, and drug metabolism. The six metabolic pathways associated with mature oocytes in the follicular fluid metabolome included fructose and mannose metabolism, polyunsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, bile acid biosynthesis, propanoate metabolism, vitamin D3 metabolism, and urea cycle/amino group metabolism. In the HILIC column, six pathways occurred in the serum metabolome and ten pathways occurred in the follicular fluid metabolome (<a href="#dead189-T3" class="usa-link">Table 3</a>). In the serum metabolome, arginine and proline metabolism, glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, butanoate metabolism, and <em>de novo</em> fatty acid biosynthesis were associated with mature oocytes. In the follicular fluid metabolome, tyrosine metabolism, heparan sulfate degradation, carnitine shuttle, androgen and estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism, C21-steriod hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, bile acid biosynthesis, vitamin A metabolism, and vitamin D3 metabolism were associated with mature oocytes. Between the two matrices, fatty acid metabolism and squalene and cholesterol biosynthesis were associated with mature oocytes in the HILIC column. <p>In general, the two outcomes shared more common pathways in the follicular fluid as compared to the serum. In the follicular fluid, five pathways, bile acid biosynthesis, carnitine shuttle, androgen and estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism, C21-steroid hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, and vitamin D3 metabolism were associated with both outcomes. In contrast, none of the pathways identified in the serum overlapped between the two outcomes.</p></section><section id="sec25"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolite identification</h3>
<p>Using level-1 evidence, we identified four unique metabolites associated with one or both outcomes from the serum metabolome (<a href="#dead189-T4" class="usa-link">Table 4</a>). Dihydroxybenzoate was negatively associated with both peak estradiol levels and the number of mature oocytes. 10-Hydroxydecanoate and omega-hydroxydodecanoic acid were negatively associated with estradiol levels. Citrate was negatively associated with the number of mature oocytes retrieved. In the follicular fluid metabolome, nine unique metabolites were associated with one or both ovarian stimulation outcomes (<a href="#dead189-T5" class="usa-link">Table 5</a>). Cholesta-5,7Dien-3Beta-ol, deoxycholate, citrulline, and arginine were all negatively associated with both peak estradiol levels and the number of mature oocytes. Bilirubin, was only negatively associated with peak estradiol levels. In contrast, Reichstein’s substance <em>S</em> and methionine, were negatively associated with the number of mature oocytes. Caffeine was the sole metabolite positively associated with both peak estradiol levels and the number of mature oocytes. Between the serum and follicular fluid metabolomes, we did not observe any overlap in the level-1 identified metabolites associated with peak estradiol levels and the number of mature oocytes. There was limited evidence of non-monotonic relationships between any of the level-1 metabolites and ovarian response categories (<a href="#f1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figs S1</a> and <a href="#f2" class="usa-link">S2</a>). None of the metabolites survived FDR correction.</p>

</div></section></section></section><section id="sec26"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p>We identified several metabolic pathways and metabolites unique to peak estradiol levels and the number of mature oocytes retrieved. When comparing the two biological matrices (serum and follicular fluid), we observed related but ultimately different pathways between the two biological fluids. In the serum metabolome, lipid pathways were associated with both peak estradiol levels and the number of mature oocytes retrieved. In addition to these pathways, amino acids were associated with the number of mature oocytes retrieved in the serum metabolome. In the follicular fluid metabolome, hormone, and lipid pathways were associated with peak estradiol levels. These same pathways were associated with the number of mature oocytes retrieved in the follicular fluid metabolome as well as vitamin pathways. The differences in these outcomes may point to different biological processes while the differences by biological matrices may indicate different potential biomarkers for the serum and follicular fluid. Lastly, among all the pathways observed, vitamin D3 was the most consistently associated with both outcomes across matrices.</p>
<p>Few studies have utilized untargeted metabolomics to investigate ovarian stimulation response and those that do focus onthenumber of oocytes retrieved rather than estradiol levels (<a href="#dead189-B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B3">Borges <em>etal.</em>, 2019</a>). However, the results of our estradiol analysis do fit in well with results from related studies. In our analysis, we observed that the major pathways associated with peak estradiol included fatty acid (serum and follicular fluid), vitamin (follicular fluid), and hormone (follicular fluid). Fatty acids in particular have been associated with a variety of IVF outcomes including, poor morphology (<a href="#dead189-B24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B24">Jungheim <em>etal.</em>, 2011</a>), cleavage rate (<a href="#dead189-B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B38">O'Gorman <em>etal.</em>, 2013</a>), embryo growth (<a href="#dead189-B54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B54">Yagi <em>etal.</em>, 2016</a>), and pregnancy status (<a href="#dead189-B57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B57">Younis <em>etal.</em>, 2012</a>). In one study of ten patients undergoing ovarian hyperstimulation, estradiol levels were associated with elevated fatty acid levels (<a href="#dead189-B46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B46">Tadmor <em>etal.</em>, 1994</a>). Estradiol may trigger an increased metabolism of fatty acids, which in turn are used by maturing oocytes and embryos for growth and development. Additionally, studies have observed that fatty acids can be predictive of IVF outcomes. For example, in a study of 15 patients undergoing IVF, increased levels of palmitic and stearic acids in serum samples were associated with lower pregnancy rates(<a href="#dead189-B57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B57">Younis <em>etal.</em>, 2012</a>). Fatty acids appear to be important in the response to ovarian stimulation and potentially oocyte development.</p>
<p>In addition to fatty acids, peak estradiol levels were associated with vitamin and hormone pathways in the follicular fluid metabolome but not the serum metabolome. We observed vitamins D3 and B3 metabolism, which both have been previously associated with various aspects of IVF and fertility (<a href="#dead189-B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B26">Lerchbaum and Obermayer-Pietsch, 2012</a>; <a href="#dead189-B1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B1">Antunes <em>etal.</em>, 2018</a>; <a href="#dead189-B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B32">Makieva <em>etal.</em>, 2021</a>; <a href="#dead189-B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B43">Song <em>etal.</em>, 2021</a>; <a href="#dead189-B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B17">Guo <em>etal.</em>, 2022</a>). Vitamin D, in particular, has been examined in relation to estradiol production (<a href="#dead189-B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B26">Lerchbaum and Obermayer-Pietsch, 2012</a>). Interestingly, one study of women undergoing IVF, observed that lower vitamin D levels in the follicular fluid were associated with higher peak estradiol serum levels and a higher number of large size (≥16 mm) follicles (<a href="#dead189-B1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B1">Antunes <em>etal.</em>, 2018</a>). In contrast, in a clinical trial comparing IVF outcomes between women receiving either a single dose of 25-hydroxyvitamin D (600 000 IU) or a placebo, hormone levels including estradiol did not differ by group (<a href="#dead189-B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B32">Makieva <em>etal.</em>, 2021</a>). However, vitamin D measured in the follicular fluid was moderately correlated (<em>r</em> = 0.45) with estradiol levels in the placebo group (<a href="#dead189-B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B32">Makieva <em>etal.</em>, 2021</a>). In the current study, we observed an association in the follicular fluid metabolome between the vitamin D3 pathway and peak estradiol levels. In general, it is possible that vitamin D is serving several purposes in the follicular fluid, where it may act as an antioxidant protecting the developing follicle from reactive oxygen species (<a href="#dead189-B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B26">Lerchbaum and Obermayer-Pietsch, 2012</a>; <a href="#dead189-B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B32">Makieva <em>etal.</em>, 2021</a>) and is therefore being consumed in redox reactions which would explain the negative association observed in previous studies. Another theory is that vitamin D does not directly influence hormones but may protect against hyperstimulation (<a href="#dead189-B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B32">Makieva <em>etal.</em>, 2021</a>); although, there is conflicting evidence for this idea. In either case, vitamin D metabolism plays an important, albeit, not fully understood, role in maintaining a positive response to ovarian stimulation. We also observed an association between estradiol levels and vitamin B3. Vitamin B3 may act as an antioxidant protecting the developing follicle from reactive oxygen species (<a href="#dead189-B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B17">Guo <em>etal.</em>, 2022</a>). In general, the role of vitamins in ovarian stimulation requires additional research. As expected, peak estradiol was also associatedwith hormone pathways in the follicular fluid, specifically, androgen, estrogen, C21-steroid hormone biosynthesis, and metabolism.</p>
<p>When we examined the number of mature oocytes retrieved, the main metabolic pathways in the serum involved amino acid metabolism while, similar to estradiol, fatty acids, vitamin, and hormone pathways dominated the follicular fluid metabolome. For the serum metabolome, a prior study of 30 patients undergoing ICSI classified women into poor responders, normal responders, and hyper responders depending on the number of oocytes retrieved (<a href="#dead189-B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B3">Borges <em>etal.</em>, 2019</a>). From this study, ten metabolites in the serum were able to distinguish between the different types of responders, with most metabolites being tentatively classified as lipids but one being classified as an amino acid (<a href="#dead189-B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B3">Borges <em>etal.</em>, 2019</a>). Additionally, in the serum metabolome, amino acids have been previously associated with gynecological disorders like PCOS (<a href="#dead189-B56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B56">Ye <em>etal.</em>, 2022</a>). In a study of 380 women, 190 with and without PCOS, plasma samples were collected to compare 50 different amino acids between the two groups (<a href="#dead189-B56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B56">Ye <em>etal.</em>, 2022</a>). From this analysis, 31 amino acids differed by PCOS status (<a href="#dead189-B56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B56">Ye <em>etal.</em>, 2022</a>). In another study examining age related changes in the follicular fluid metabolome between 15 older women (39–47 years) and 15 younger women (27–34 years) several amino acids were altered (<a href="#dead189-B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B21">Huang <em>etal.</em>, 2022</a>). When animal models are considered, specifically bovines, alanine, and glycine levels in the follicular fluid metabolome have previously been associated with poor morphology of oocytes (<a href="#dead189-B40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B40">Sinclair <em>etal.</em>, 2008</a>). In our analysis, we observed four particular amino acid pathways associated with the number mature oocytes, including arginine and proline metabolism, aspartate and asparagine metabolism, glutamate metabolism, and histidine metabolism. It may be possible that alterations of these serum amino acids, through receptor synthesis or synthesis of peptide signaling molecules, could lead to changes in the follicular fluid and ultimately influence the number of mature oocytes. However, when the follicular fluid metabolome was analyzed, we did not observe any of these same amino acid pathways. This is consistent with an earlier study of amino acids in the follicular fluid of twelve women undergoing IVF, in which no difference was observed in the amino acids by oocyte maturity (<a href="#dead189-B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B23">Jimena <em>etal.</em>, 1993</a>).</p>
<p>In the follicular fluid metabolome, we observed many lipid pathways associated with the number of mature oocytes. These findings are very similar to those observed in relation to peak estradiol levels. Lipid and more specifically fatty acids have been associated with a variety of IVF endpoints in culture media, endometrial fluid, and follicular fluid including protocol type (<a href="#dead189-B53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B53">Wen <em>etal.</em>, 2018</a>), oocyte quality (<a href="#dead189-B24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B24">Jungheim <em>etal.</em>, 2011</a>), oocyte growth (<a href="#dead189-B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B38">O'Gorman <em>etal.</em>, 2013</a>) and development (<a href="#dead189-B55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B55">Yang <em>etal.</em>, 2022</a>), embryonic growth (<a href="#dead189-B54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B54">Yagi <em>etal.</em>, 2016</a>), pregnancy status (<a href="#dead189-B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B37">Montani <em>etal.</em>, 2019</a>; <a href="#dead189-B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B34">Matorras <em>etal.</em>, 2020</a>), and reproductive aging (<a href="#dead189-B60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B60">Zhang <em>etal.</em>, 2020</a>). In a study of 70 women undergoing ICSI, follicular fluid levels of phospholipid stearic acid, alpha-linolenic acid, total n-3-polyunsaturated fatty acids, dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid, and delta-6 desaturase were negatively correlated with the number of oocytes while follicular fluid levels of oleic acid, monounsaturated fatty acids, and stearoyl-CoA desaturase and were positively correlated with the number of oocytes (<a href="#dead189-B59" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B59">Zarezadeh <em>etal.</em>, 2021</a>). In another study comparing women with PCOS with hyper response, women without PCOS but with hyper response, and a control group with a normal response, 15 metabolites, including some lipids were identified as markers of hyper response (<a href="#dead189-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B9">Cordeiro <em>etal.</em>, 2018</a>). Additionally, a study of 26 participants comparing the metabolomes of a small (8–13 mm) and large (17–22 mm) follicles demonstrated that several lipids, specifically phospholipids, had differing levels in the follicular fluid (<a href="#dead189-B55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B55">Yang <em>etal.</em>, 2022</a>), which further highlights the role of lipids in oocyte maturation. Taken together, these studies and our results, indicate that fatty acids and more broadly lipids play a key role in oocyte development.</p>
<p>In addition to fatty acids, we again observed vitamin and hormone pathways associated with the number of mature oocytes. Specifically, vitamins A and D3 were associated with mature oocytes. The role of vitamin A, and more specifically retinoic acid, in reproductive health has been known for some time (<a href="#dead189-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B8">Clagett-Dame and Knutson, 2011</a>). Extensive studies showed that vitamin A deficiency induced either by nutritional or genetic means results in implantation failure as well as defects in early embryonic growth and development (<a href="#dead189-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B8">Clagett-Dame and Knutson, 2011</a>). In a recent study of 313 follicular fluid samples, higher vitamin A (retinol) levels were associated with improved fertilization and embryo quality and development (<a href="#dead189-B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B42">Skowrońska <em>etal.</em>, 2020</a>). Vitamin D has also been associated with IVF outcomes (<a href="#dead189-B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B26">Lerchbaum and Obermayer-Pietsch, 2012</a>; <a href="#dead189-B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B7">Ciepiela <em>etal.</em>, 2018</a>); although the mechanism for these associations is not entirely understood. Finally, hormone pathways including androgen and estrogen, and C21 steroid hormone metabolism were associated with the number of mature oocytes. Identifying these associations again in relation to an ovarian stimulation outcome offers some additional validity to our results, as we would expect these hormone pathways to be associated with these outcomes. Interestingly, in the previous study of 35 women, sex steroid levels in the follicular fluid were associated with follicle size but not oocyte maturation or the ability of the oocyte to fertilize (<a href="#dead189-B52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B52">Wen <em>etal.</em>, 2010</a>). The study by Wen <em>etal.</em>, had follicles that ranged from 10 to 35 mm while our analysis typically included follicles at or above 16 mm. It is possible that the difference in follicle size between these two studies could account for why we observed several hormone pathways associated with the number of mature oocytes. Future studies are warranted to fully investigate the role and timing of hormone synthesis in follicle growth and maturation.</p>
<p>In general, we observed limited overlap between the two biological matrices (i.e. serum and follicular fluid). However, across the two ovarian stimulation outcomes, we observed a large overlap in the follicular fluid metabolomic pathways. Through our study, we have further demonstrated that while the serum and follicular fluid are related, these matrices are ultimately distinct (<a href="#dead189-B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B19">Hood <em>etal.</em>, 2022b</a>). The blood and follicular fluid are separated by the blood-follicle barrier which allows for the transport of select metabolites between the blood and follicular fluid (<a href="#dead189-B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B41">Siu and Cheng, 2012</a>). In addition, each biological fluid has a distinct biological purpose (<a href="#dead189-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B2">Baskind <em>etal.</em>, 2011</a>; <a href="#dead189-B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B13">Dumesic <em>etal.</em>, 2015</a>). The follicular fluid metabolome may have greater relevance for ovarian stimulation outcomes as it is a more targeted biological fluid while the serum circulates across the body and is involved in numerous processes. However, given the logistical challenges in collecting follicular fluid prior to oocyte retrieval, the utility of using follicular fluid biomarkers as predictors of ovarian response remains difficult.</p>
<p>Our study does have several limitations. First, the sample for this study is a majority White and highly educated and therefore cannot be generalized to the broader US population. Second, while we adjusted for several known confounders, we cannot rule out the possibility of residual confounding, which could bias our results. Additionally, because we adjusted for age, BMI, infertility diagnosis, and ovarian stimulation, metabolic associations with these parameters may have been lost. Third, there is a risk of false positives due to multiple comparisons. While we attempted to address this using different levels of FDR correction, we most often used a cut-off of raw <em>P</em>-value &lt;0.05 for input into the pathway analyses to ensure that there would be sufficient features for a meaningful interpretation. Because our study was relatively small and designed to be exploratory, we ultimately decided on less stringent criteria for statistical significance to decrease the likelihood of false negatives. Therefore, considering the elevated chance of false positive findings, our results should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger cohorts. Next, the serum samples collected were non-fasting which could have influenced our results. However, from previous analyses of these data, we examined dietary patterns and observed limited differences when these were included in our models (<a href="#dead189-B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B18">Hood <em>etal.</em>, 2022a</a>,<a href="#dead189-B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dead189-B20">c</a>). Next, each follicle fluid sample was collected with standardized 1 ml of flushing fluid, which would have diluted it. However, we observed several thousand features in the follicular fluid and observed more than those detected in the serum samples, so the chance that this dilution negatively impacted our results is low. Finally, the identification of metabolites using untargeted methods is difficult and some metabolic pathways are harder to detect than others, such as vitamin D3. While caution should be taken with these results; our results are generally in line with the literature that indicates a role of vitamin D in oocyte maturation. Our study does have several strengths, including its prospective design and our utilization of a comprehensive metabolomics workflow that has been extensively utilized, allowing us to use level-1 evidence to identify potential metabolites.</p></section><section id="sec27"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p>We investigated the association between two ovarian stimulation outcomes and the metabolomes from two distinct biological fluids. We observed that in the serum metabolome, mostly fatty acid pathways were associated with peak estradiol levels while mostly amino acid pathways, in addition to fatty acid pathways, were associated with the number of mature oocytes. In the follicular fluid metabolome, mostly lipid, vitamin, and hormone pathways were associated with both peak estradiol levels and the number of mature oocytes. While the follicular fluid metabolome had a high degree of overlap between the ovarian stimulation outcomes, the serum had none. Our results provide unique insight into the metabolites and biological pathways that might be underlying optimal response to ovarian stimulation and provide guidance for future targeted studies.</p></section><section id="sec28"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
