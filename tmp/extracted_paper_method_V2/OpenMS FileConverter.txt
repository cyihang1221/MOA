sample_description: The study utilized wastewater influent samples collected from the wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) influent of an industrial park hosting approximately 40 chemical plants. These were 24-hour consecutive composite samples. Two distinct validation datasets were constructed: (1) Validation Dataset I consisted of 53 replicate vials, each containing 100 μL of a pooled representative wastewater matrix (prepared by pooling a batch of representative wastewater samples to simulate a controlled, low-variability background) mixed with 900 μL of eluent A (LC/MS-grade methanol/water/formic acid mixture), and spiked with eight target compounds following predefined temporal intensity profiles (as shown in Fig. 1); (2) Validation Dataset II comprised 52 individual 24-h composite influent wastewater samples collected on consecutive days; each sample was centrifuged for 1 min at 2500 rpm, tenfold-diluted with Milli-Q ultra-pure water, and then spiked with five deuterated internal standards (ISs) and nine chemical standards at concentrations ranging from 8 μg/L to 350 μg/L, following five categories of temporal patterns—left-sided spills (Var237–238), wide spills (Var239–240), short-lived spills (Var241–243), right-sided spill (Var244), and episodic pattern (Var245)—as illustrated in Fig. 2. Additionally, quality control (QC) samples were prepared by spiking all 38 target compounds into eluent A to a final volume of 1 mL at five concentration levels (5–100 μg/L), with the 50 μg/L level used initially for software parameter optimization and the full range employed to assess area-concentration linearity. All samples—including QCs, validation sets, and real influent samples—were spiked with four internal standards prior to analysis and analyzed in triplicate. Prior to spiking in Dataset II, each unspiked sample was measured once to confirm the absence of the nine target compounds in the native matrix. The real influent samples were dominated by two categories of chemicals: (i) several HPLC-HRMS-detectable compounds at relatively high concentrations (requiring dilution), and (ii) a large number of compounds present at low concentrations (whose detectability is further challenged by dilution). Chemical presence and concentrations varied significantly over time due to industrial production schedules.

sample_description: The study utilized two main types of samples: (1) Training data derived from a mouse proteome dataset, digested with trypsin (Promega), and analyzed via nanoflow LC–MS/MS using a Proxeon Biosystems nano-HPLC system with a 75 µm × 15 cm column packed in-house with 3-µm C18 beads (Dr. Maisch), coupled online to an AB Sciex 4000 QTrap mass spectrometer; precursor ion selection was performed in Q1 over m/z 400–1000 Th and product ion selection in Q3 over m/z 400–1200 Th. Peptide identifications were obtained via consensus scoring across Mascot, X! Tandem, and OMSSA search engines against the SwissProt mouse database (v57.1) using a target/decoy strategy with reversed decoy sequences; carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, no variable modifications were allowed, precursor mass tolerance was 0.8 Da, and fragment mass tolerance was 0.5 Da; false discovery rate was controlled at q-value ≤ 0.01; for model training, only peptides with no missed cleavages were retained, and the top 1,000 highest-confidence peptides (ranked by q-value) were selected—those with the highest total ion current (TIC) when multiple spectra matched the same sequence and charge state—and additionally, 1,000 undetected theoretical peptides (from proteins with high sequence coverage) served as negative examples for proteotypicity modeling. (2) Experimental test data consisted of the UPS1 protein mixture (Sigma Aldrich, Cat. No. U6133), containing 48 distinct proteins, similarly trypsin-digested and analyzed under identical instrumental conditions; transitions were computationally calculated and scheduled for all 48 proteins. Furthermore, pre-existing experimentally validated transition information was incorporated from the SRMAtlas repository (http://www.srmatlas.org), exemplified using human α-lactalbumin (UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot: P00709, LALBA_HUMAN).
