</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="s1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Background</h3>
<p>High-resolution metabolomics (HRM) is an innovative tool to study challenging infectious diseases like leprosy, where the pathogen cannot be grown with standard methods. Here, we use HRM to better understand associations between disease manifestations, nutrition, and host metabolism.</p></section><section id="s2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>From 2018 to 2019, adults with leprosy and controls were recruited in Minas Gerais, Brazil. Plasma metabolites were detected using an established HRM workflow and characterized by accurate mass, mass to charge ratio m/z and retention time. The <em>mummichog</em> informatics package compared metabolic pathways between cases and controls and between multibacillary (MB) and paucibacillary (PB) leprosy. Additionally, select individual metabolites were quantified and compared.</p></section><section id="s3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>Thirty-nine cases (62% MB and 38% PB) and 25 controls were enrolled. We found differences (<em>P</em> &lt; .05) in several metabolic pathways, including fatty acid metabolism, carnitine shuttle, retinol, vitamin D3, and C-21 steroid metabolism, between cases and controls with lower retinol and associated metabolites in cases. Between MB and PB, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, tryptophan, and cortisol were all found to be lower in MB (<em>P</em> &lt; .05).</p></section><section id="s4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h3>
<p>Metabolites associated with several nutrient-related metabolic pathways appeared differentially regulated in leprosy, especially MB versus PB. This pilot study demonstrates the metabolic interdependency of these pathways, which may play a role in the pathophysiology of disease.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> carnitine, energy metabolism, fatty acids, Hansen's disease, high-resolution metabolomics, leprosy, <em>Mycobacterium leprae</em>, metabolism, nutrition, retinol, tryptophan, vitamin D</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><hr class="headless">
<p>Results of our untargeted high-resolution metabolomics study demonstrated differentially regulated metabolic pathways that differed significantly across cases of leprosy and controls with vitamin D, retinol, and fatty acid metabolites featuring prominently, supporting potential roles in the immune response to infection.</p></section><hr class="headless">
<p>Leprosy, or Hansen's disease, a disabling infection of nerves and skin caused by <em>Mycobacterium leprae</em> and <em>Mycobacterium lepromatosis</em>, remains a public health issue in endemic areas [<a href="#jiad410-B1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B1">1</a>]. Before the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, over 200 000 new cases were reported globally annually, with pockets of highly endemic disease in many areas such as India, Brazil, and Indonesia [<a href="#jiad410-B1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B1">1</a>]. Furthermore, a chronic armadillo reservoir is implicated in the steady number of individuals diagnosed with Hansen's disease in the southern United States, presenting additional challenges and potential routes of transmission [<a href="#jiad410-B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B2">2</a>]. The inability to grow <em>M. leprae</em> in vitro and the long incubation period make it difficult to study host-pathogen interactions that may influence the course of infection [<a href="#jiad410-B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B3">3</a>]. This not only hinders identification of biomarkers with potential to aid in disease diagnosis, but also limits our understanding of the factors that drive progression to clinically evident disease.</p>
<p>Host metabolism shapes the immune response to infectious diseases, and performing high-resolution metabolomics (HRM) on biologic samples of persons with leprosy provides a mechanism to better understand immune responses to the bacillus. HRM captures thousands of small molecules including those derived from endogenous metabolism, the diet, the microbiome, the environment, medications, and microbial pathogens [<a href="#jiad410-B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B4">4–6</a>]. As an intracellular pathogen, <em>M. leprae</em> utilizes host metabolism to replicate [<a href="#jiad410-B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B7">7</a>]. In particular, <em>M. leprae</em> alters the metabolism of Schwann cells and macrophages, leading to accumulation of fatty acids in organelles called lipid droplets [<a href="#jiad410-B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B7">7</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B8">8</a>]. The accumulation of these droplets, as seen in lepromatous leprosy, can interfere with the immune response leading to impaired T-cell responses and propagation of infection [<a href="#jiad410-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B8">8</a>]. This is due to upregulation of prostaglandins (PGE2), interleukin 10 (IL-10), and other polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) that can promote T-regulator (Treg) cells [<a href="#jiad410-B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B7">7</a>]. The sparse metabolomic data published to date have shown an increase in PUFAs in serum and skin from patients with higher versus lower bacillary loads, respectively [<a href="#jiad410-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B9">9</a>].</p>
<p>Other important metabolic pathways involved in the immune response to mycobacterial infections involve amino acid and micronutrient metabolism like tryptophan and vitamin D3, respectively [<a href="#jiad410-B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B7">7</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B10">10</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B11">11</a>]. Tryptophan metabolism can be associated with either proinflammatory or anti-inflammatory responses to pathogens [<a href="#jiad410-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B10">10</a>]. Recent work in tuberculosis and leprosy have shown that upregulation of indoleamine 2,3 dioxygenase (IDO-1) in response to either infection can lead to catabolism of tryptophan into kynurenines, which modulates effector T-cell responses by inducing production of Tregs via activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor and impairing Th17 responses [<a href="#jiad410-B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B7">7</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B10">10</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B12">12</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B13">13</a>]. Vitamin D3 has many roles in the immunologic response to <em>M. leprae</em> including downregulating select toll-like receptors (TLRs) in human monocytes, leading to reduced binding of <em>M. leprae</em> and consequent reduced infection of the monocyte [<a href="#jiad410-B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B11">11</a>]. Also, vitamin D3 regulates the expression of genes in the prostaglandin pathway, causing less expression of PGE2, which reduces the fatty acid accumulation that can negatively impact T-cell immunity [<a href="#jiad410-B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B11">11</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B14">14</a>].</p>
<p>Despite the potential ability of HRM to identify new biomarkers of disease [<a href="#jiad410-B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B15">15</a>] and novel insights into host-pathogen interactions [<a href="#jiad410-B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B6">6</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B16">16–18</a>], data on metabolomic responses to leprosy are limited. The potential of plasma HRM to gain important mechanistic insights into disease pathophysiology is particularly great for <em>M. leprae</em> due to challenges studying the infection and complexity of disease presentations [<a href="#jiad410-B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B3">3</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B19">19</a>]. The goal of this pilot study was to broadly characterize systemic plasma metabolic differences in patients with leprosy versus asymptomatic controls in the same community, as well as individuals with multibacillary (MB) and paucibacillary (PB) disease. We sought to improve our understanding of metabolic differences that may impact the host immune response and nutritional metabolism in these patients.</p>
<section id="jiad410-s0"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">METHODS</h2>
<section id="jiad410-s0.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study Design and Population</h3>
<p>We performed HRM on plasma samples from a cross-sectional study of persons with leprosy (n = 39), asymptomatic household contacts (n = 17), and healthy community controls (n = 8). Participants were recruited from Belo Horizonte and Governador Valadares, 2 urban centers in the southeastern state of Minas Gerais, Brazil, from 2 state leprosy reference clinics, Hospital Eduardo de Menezes in Belo Horizonte and CREDEN-PES (Centro de Referência em Doenças Endêmicas e Programs Especiais) in Governador Valadares. Governador Valadares has a high annual new case detection rate of leprosy, found to be 28.4/100 000 in 2019 [<a href="#jiad410-B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B20">20</a>].</p></section><section id="jiad410-s0.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data Collection</h3>
<p>Between June 2018 and December 2019, participants aged 18 years and older were recruited at the 2 dermatologic clinics. One 16 year old, an individual with PB leprosy, was also recruited at CREDEN-PES. A case of leprosy was defined as an individual with newly diagnosed leprosy by a dermatologist, either by clinical diagnosis, skin biopsy, skin smear/bacillary index, or combination of these. Bacillary index was determined by microscopic examination of skin scrapings from the ears and elbows of all cases. Those with bacteria detected were positive and those without bacteria were deemed bacillary negative. Ridley-Jopling criteria on histopathology was used to differentiate the different clinical forms of leprosy in those who received a biopsy: indeterminate, tuberculoid, borderline tuberculoid, borderline borderline, borderline lepromatous, and lepromatous [<a href="#jiad410-B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B21">21</a>]. If a biopsy was not done, then the dermatologist classified the disease as indeterminate, tuberculoid, borderline, or lepromatous per the Madrid classification [<a href="#jiad410-B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B22">22</a>]. MB was defined as per World Health Organization guidelines as ≥ 5 lesions and as PB for less than 5 lesions and negative bacillary index [<a href="#jiad410-B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B23">23</a>]. Participants were recruited at their dermatologist visit and were eligible if they had not started multidrug therapy. Controls were recruited from the Hospital Eduardo de Menezes Dermatology clinic and were classified as household contacts (healthy individuals who accompanied cases) or healthy controls. The healthy controls were recruited from the dermatologic clinic and were eligible if they presented with a noninfectious, nonautoimmune, minor skin ailment. Household contacts were examined for leprosy before enrollment. Pregnant women were excluded.</p>
<p>After informed consent, participants were administered a questionnaire on clinical, demographic, and household information. Blood samples were drawn and centrifuged to separate plasma. Plasma samples were then immediately frozen and stored at −80°C until they were shipped on dry ice to the Clinical Biomarkers Laboratory at Emory University, Atlanta, GA.</p></section><section id="jiad410-s0.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">High-Resolution Metabolomics</h3>
<p>Deidentified plasma samples were randomized by laboratory personnel who were blinded to the study data. Thawed plasma (65 μL) was treated with 130 μL acetonitrile (2:1, v/v) containing an internal isotopic standard mixture (3.5 μL/sample), as previously described [<a href="#jiad410-B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B5">5</a>]. The internal standard mix for quality control consisted of 14 stable isotopic chemicals covering a broad range of small molecules [<a href="#jiad410-B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B5">5</a>]. Samples were mixed and placed on ice for 30 minutes before centrifugation to remove protein and then the supernatant was transferred to low-volume autosampler vials maintained at 4°C. They were then analyzed in triplicate using a Q Exactive HF Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Dual hydrophilic interaction and C18 chromatography was used (TARGA 2.1 × 10 cm; Higgins Analytical) with a formic acid/acetonitrile gradient in positive and negative electrospray ionization mode respectively over a scan range of 85 to 1275 m/z and stored as Raw files [<a href="#jiad410-B24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B24">24</a>]. Data were extracted and aligned using apLCMS [<a href="#jiad410-B25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B25">25</a>] and xMSanalyzer [<a href="#jiad410-B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B26">26</a>] with each feature defined by specific m/z value, retention time, and integrated ion intensity [<a href="#jiad410-B24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B24">24</a>]. Three technical replicates were performed for each plasma sample and median summarized [<a href="#jiad410-B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B27">27</a>]. Liquid chromatography was performed in triplicates of each sample for rigor of measurement. Data were filtered before downstream analysis to exclude m/z features having a median coefficient of variation within technical replicates ≥75%, and only samples with Pearson correlation within technical replicates ≥ 0.7 were used for downstream analysis [<a href="#jiad410-B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B28">28</a>].</p>
<p>Selected metabolites were then identified and quantified using ion dissociation methods (MS/MS) relative to authentic reference standards [<a href="#jiad410-B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B29">29</a>]. These metabolites were chosen from the pathway enrichment analyses to compare specific metabolites of interests within the significant pathways. In addition, given the influence of tryptophan metabolism on immunity for both tuberculosis and leprosy, median concentrations of tryptophan were determined and compared across cases (MB vs PB) [<a href="#jiad410-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B10">10</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B12">12</a>].</p></section><section id="jiad410-s0.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical Analysis</h3>
<p>Metabolite intensity values and concentrations were compared using R statistical software (R Foundation, version 3.5.0). Metabolite intensity values and concentrations were log<sub>2</sub>-transformed and compared between groups using a linear regression model controlling for age and sex. In the untargeted analysis, a false discovery rate (q &lt; 0.2) was used to correct for multiple hypothesis testing and select metabolites for downstream analysis [<a href="#jiad410-B30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B30">30</a>]. Row-normalized heat maps with 2-way hierarchical clustering were constructed with these intensity results to demonstrate clustering across individual samples and metabolites. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was performed using <em>mummichog</em>, a Python-based informatics tool that leverages the organization of metabolic networks to predict functional changes in metabolic pathway activity [<a href="#jiad410-B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B28">28</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B31">31</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B32">32</a>]. <em>P</em> values less than .05 were considered statistically significance for targeted metabolites and in the metabolic pathway analysis.</p></section><section id="jiad410-s0.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study Approval</h3>
<p>This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Emory University (Atlanta, GA), Universidade Federal de Juiz de Fora (Juiz de Fora, Brazil), Hospital Eduardo de Menezes (Belo Horizonte, Brazil), and Faculdade da Saúde e Écologia Humana (Vespasiano, Brazil). All subjects provided written informed consent, and in the case of the 16 year old, a parent provided written informed consent and the participant assented.</p></section></section><section id="jiad410-s1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS</h2>
<section id="jiad410-s1.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Clinical and Demographic Characteristics</h3>
<p>Sixty-four individuals from Minas Gerais, Brazil, were enrolled in the study with 39 newly diagnosed cases of leprosy and 25 healthy controls. The majority of participants were adults, with one 16.5-year-old individual. About half of cases were female while 84% of controls were female (<a href="#jiad410-T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). Of the cases, 24 (62%) were MB and of the asymptomatic controls, 17 (68%) were household contacts of leprosy cases. <a href="#jiad410-T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a> shows demographic data and the breakdown of clinical leprosy types from indeterminate to lepromatous, with tuberculoid being the most frequent at 12 cases (31%).</p>

<p>Using plasma HRM, we detected 9912 mass/charge (m/z) features in negative ionization mode and 10 705 in positive ionization mode. Those features that were detected in at least 50% of samples were included for downstream analysis. <a href="#jiad410-F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a> shows heat maps of positively and negatively charged metabolites with clear clustering by group (case vs control). To overcome possible misclassification of MB cases, <a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Materials</a> show comparisons across bacillary index negative versus bacillary index positive. Significant overlap of statistically significant pathways was noted when cases were classified in this manner compared to the operational classification of MB versus PB (<a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figure 1</a>).</p>

<p>With <em>mummichog</em> pathway analyses, multiple metabolic pathways were found to be statistically significantly different between leprosy cases and controls. Metabolic pathways associated with prostaglandins, linoleate, arachidonic acid (AA), and glycophospholipid metabolism, as well as fatty acid synthesis and activation were statistically significantly different (<em>P</em> &lt; .05) in cases compared to healthy controls (<a href="#jiad410-F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>) as were several micronutrients: vitamin A (retinol), vitamin C, vitamin D3, and vitamin E. Within the retinol pathway, retinol, 9-retinal, and 4-hydroxy-retinyl acetate were decreased in cases versus controls (<a href="#jiad410-F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>). Several AA downstream metabolites were lower in cases compared to controls, including prostaglandin G2 (PGG2), leukotriene A4, thromboxane A2, and 8,9-dihydroxyeicosatrienoic acid (8,9-DHET) (<a href="#jiad410-F4" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>). Lastly, carnitine shuttle metabolism differed between cases and controls (<a href="#jiad410-F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>).</p>

<p>This exploratory HRM study is the first to simultaneously show differences across a host of metabolic pathways in leprosy, from fatty acids to energy metabolism, micronutrients, and tryptophan, all pathways at the intersection of metabolism and immunity. We found that multiple metabolic pathways differentiated leprosy cases from controls, indicating leprosy disease state may be a driver of metabolic differences relevant to disease pathophysiology.</p>
<p>Differences in fatty acid and eicosanoid metabolism figured prominently between controls and cases as well as between PB and MB disease (<a href="#jiad410-F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a> and <a href="#jiad410-F5" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>). Intracellular accumulation of lipids like AA in infected cells is a hallmark of MB disease, with foamy macrophages noted in histopathological samples [<a href="#jiad410-B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B7">7</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B8">8</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B34">34</a>]. Prior studies have shown the increase in exogenous lipid uptake as well pathways producing de novo fatty acids and phospholipids in infected cells, especially those at the lepromatous end of the spectrum [<a href="#jiad410-B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B9">9</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B35">35</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B36">36</a>]. We found that cases had lower median concentrations of several AA metabolites than controls when compared directly to each other (<a href="#jiad410-F4" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>). PGG2, as the direct byproduct of oxidation of AA, is a precursor of PGE2 and thromboxane A2 [<a href="#jiad410-B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B37">37</a>] while 8,9-DHET is a product of AA metabolism through a different mechanism, the CYP450 epoyxgenase pathway [<a href="#jiad410-B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B38">38</a>]. PGE2 is known to suppress Th1 responses and induce Tregs, and 8,9-DHET, less studied in infectious diseases, appears to have predominant anti-inflammatory properties [<a href="#jiad410-B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B37">37</a>]. Thromboxane A2 is another proinflammatory metabolite in the AA/cyclooxygenase (COX) pathway [<a href="#jiad410-B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B39">39</a>] and leukotriene A4 is a precursor of the proinflammatory leukotriene B4 [<a href="#jiad410-B40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B40">40</a>]. While our data does show a spread of concentrations across both cases and controls, the consistently lower median concentrations of these AA metabolites support the role of fatty acid metabolism in the response to infection, potentially showing a shift to a more anti-inflammatory response in cases.</p>
<p>Intricately tied to fatty acid metabolism is the carnitine shuttle, which transports fatty acids across the mitochondrial membrane, to be then used in the citric acid cycle as a source of energy [<a href="#jiad410-B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B41">41</a>]. In our study, metabolites in the carnitine shuttle showed statistically significant differences in both cases versus controls and MB versus PB (<a href="#jiad410-F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a> and <a href="#jiad410-F5" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>), with a carnitine shuttle metabolite lower in MB versus PB. Inhibition of mitochondrial activity may be a mechanism that <em>M. leprae</em> uses to subvert host immunity [<a href="#jiad410-B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B42">42</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B43">43</a>], with potential downregulation of the carnitine pathway in MB.</p>
<p>A variety of changes in micronutrient-related pathways were observed between both cases versus controls and MB versus PB (<a href="#jiad410-F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a> and <a href="#jiad410-F5" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>). In particular, retinol concentrations and some metabolites were lower in cases versus controls. While rarely studied, limited observational studies have shown an association between vitamin A deficiency and leprosy [<a href="#jiad410-B44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B44">44</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B45">45</a>]. Given retinol's important role in immunity with deficiency associated with a suppressed Th1 response, it is possible that vitamin A deficiency could put an individual at risk of leprosy. Alternatively, retinol metabolism may differ in cases versus controls, leading to different concentrations.</p>
<p>As with retinol, the difference in vitamin D metabolite (1-α-24,25-trihydroxyvitamin D3) concentrations in PB versus MB suggests either differential metabolism of vitamin D3 in MB versus PB or potentially different baseline concentrations of vitamin D3 that could affect susceptibility to leprosy or MB disease. Several studies have shown that vitamin D deficiency is common in leprosy, especially MB disease [<a href="#jiad410-B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B11">11</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B14">14</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B46">46</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B47">47</a>]. Because vitamin D and vitamin D receptors interact with several aspects of the immune response, findings suggest the potential importance of vitamin D metabolism in differentiating infected versus healthy states. More mechanistic investigation and further exploration of these metabolic signatures are critical to better understanding these differences, especially as poor nutrition has been repeatedly associated with leprosy risk [<a href="#jiad410-B48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B48">48</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B49">49</a>].</p>
<p>Targeted analyses revealed differences in tryptophan and cortisol metabolism between persons with MB versus PB disease with median concentrations of tryptophan and cortisol significantly lower in persons with MB versus PB disease (<a href="#jiad410-F6" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a><em><a href="#jiad410-F6" class="usa-link">A</a></em>). Recent studies of dendritic cells and macrophages have shown increased expression of IDO-1 triggered by <em>M. leprae</em> in lepromatous lesions [<a href="#jiad410-B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B7">7</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B10">10</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B13">13</a>]. With increased IDO-1 expression, tryptophan is broken down to kynurenines that then act as aryl hydrocarbon receptor ligands. Aryl hydrocarbon receptors are involved in a host of immune responses including the upregulation of Treg cells that limit effector T-cell responses [<a href="#jiad410-B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B10">10</a>]. Findings from TB metabolomics have demonstrated metabolic differences between the kynurenine to tryptophan ratios in active and latent tuberculosis that reversed after treatment [<a href="#jiad410-B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B12">12</a>]. Our findings of lower tryptophan in MB compared to PB may support these metabolic pathways and further study of plasma concentrations of tryptophan and kynurenines in cases versus controls is warranted. Likewise, cortisol and corticosterone were found to be lower in MB versus PB, supporting differential steroid metabolism across the spectrum of disease. Discrepant results have been found in the literature in regard to cortisol concentrations, which may be due to methodology, choice of controls, or disparate settings. Dabi et al found higher cortisol levels in MB cases than controls while we found MB cases to have lower levels than PB cases, which was more concordant with Bansal et al [<a href="#jiad410-B33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B33">33</a>, <a href="#jiad410-B50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="jiad410-B50">50</a>].</p>
<p>Limitations of this study include the small sample size, which limited the power to compare metabolic differences between groups. Furthermore, the cross-sectional and observational study design prevents us from making cause-and-effect conclusions about metabolic associations observed in the study groups. A false discovery rate of 0.2 was used to adjust for multiple comparisons given the small sample size and concern for making a type 2 error by using a more stringent q-value. While this may have increased the chance of a type 1 error, overall, our findings are not only consistent with some of the published literature, but the exploratory results also allow for further study with improved power to detect these differences. While there are factors that we could not control for in this small study, including dietary impacts, medications, and other environmental exposures, the fact that all participants were from the same state of Brazil may mitigate these factors. Lastly, combining household contacts with the other controls may have limited our ability to differentiate cases versus controls if some of the household contacts were latently infected. However, overall, the metabolic signatures did not cluster in cases versus controls (<a href="#jiad410-F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>).</p>
<p>In summary, this preliminary study described several nutrient-related metabolic differences across healthy controls, MB, and PB leprosy, especially for fatty acids, energy metabolism, micronutrients, and tryptophan metabolism. Understanding metabolic differences in persons with leprosy and asymptomatic persons with evidence of prior exposure (such as anti-phenoglycolipid-1 [PGL-1] or anti-leprosy IDRI diagnostic-1 [LID-1] positivity) may enhance our ability to understand the pathophysiology of disease and, in the future, identify potential signatures that differentiate active and latent disease. Further prospective well-controlled and well-powered studies using HRM in individuals susceptible to leprosy will be useful to identify metabolites or profiles that could predict progression of this ancient disease.</p></section><section id="jiad410-s3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Data</h2>
