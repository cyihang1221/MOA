</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Background: <em>Athenaea fasciculata</em>, a Brazilian native species from the Solanaceae family, is recognized as a promising source of bioactive withanolides, particularly Aurelianolide A and B, which exhibit significant antitumoral activities. Despite its potential, research on the chemical constituents of this species remains limited. This study aimed to dereplicate extracts and partitions of <em>A. fasciculata</em> to streamline the discovery of bioactive withanolides. Methods: Using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS), various extracts—including <em>n</em>-hexane, methanol, and ethanol—were analyzed, and their mass spectrometry data were processed through the GNPS platform for the generation of molecular networking. The results indicated that crude extracts displayed comparable cytotoxicity against Jurkat cells, by treatment at 150 µg/mL, while alcoholic extracts achieved approximately 80% inhibition of K562 cells and K562-Lucena 1 at the same concentration. Notably, the dichloromethane partition exhibited the highest cytotoxicity across leukemia cell lines, particularly against Jurkat cells (IC<sub>50</sub> = 14.34 µg/mL). A total of 22 compounds were annotated by manual inspection and different libraries, with six of them demonstrating significant cytotoxic effects. Conclusions: This research underscores the therapeutic potential of <em>A. fasciculata</em> and highlights the effectiveness of integrating advanced analytical methods in drug discovery, paving the way for further exploration of its bioactive compounds.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> <em>Aureliana fasciculata</em>, GNPS, cancer, Solanaceae, withanolides</p></section></section><section id="sec1-molecules-29-04357"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>Natural products have long been acknowledged in pharmaceutical research as a promising reservoir for discovering novel therapeutic agents in the management of diverse diseases, including cancer [<a href="#B1-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-molecules-29-04357">1</a>]. Noteworthy examples of cancer drugs include vincristine, podophyllotoxin derivatives, and paclitaxel (Taxol) and ongoing investigations are exploring numerous other natural compounds, thus promoting a continuous exploration of natural products as potential anticancer agents [<a href="#B2-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2-molecules-29-04357">2</a>].</p>
<p>Among the natural products, withanolide class compounds stand out as promising anticancer agents, besides having other valuable reported pharmacological properties. Until now, more than 650 withanolides have been described, with most of these from members of the Solanaceae family. Withanolide metabolites are steroidal triterpene lactones composed of an ergostane-type skeleton comprising 28 carbon atoms, with either C-26 and C-22 or C-26 and C-23 oxidized to form a δ-lactone ring (type I) or a γ-lactone ring (type II), respectively. These rings are attached to an intact or rearranged ergostane scaffold. Present in many derivatives, the oxygenation at C-1 generates 1-oxo-vitanolides such as withaferin A, the first withanolide to be discovered (<a href="#molecules-29-04357-f001" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>) [<a href="#B3-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3-molecules-29-04357">3</a>,<a href="#B4-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4-molecules-29-04357">4</a>]. </p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="molecules-29-04357-f001"><h3 class="obj_head">Figure 1.</h3>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=11434275_molecules-29-04357-g001.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/30c2/11434275/068a42a7e0a3/molecules-29-04357-g001.jpg" loading="lazy" height="215" width="733" alt="Figure 1"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/molecules-29-04357-f001/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>Chemical structures of type I and II withanolides and withaferin A.</p></figcaption></figure><p><em>Athenaea fasciculata</em>, previously referred to as <em>Aureliana fasciculata</em>, is a native Brazilian species that had its name re-established as the correct taxon name to <em>Athenaea</em>, belonging to the Solanaceae family [<a href="#B5-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5-molecules-29-04357">5</a>]. Phytochemical studies on the isolation of secondary metabolites from this species are sparse in the literature; however, two major type I withanolides, aurelianolides A and B (<a href="#molecules-29-04357-f002" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>), have been isolated from its dichloromethane partition [<a href="#B6-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6-molecules-29-04357">6</a>].</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="molecules-29-04357-f002"><h3 class="obj_head">Figure 2.</h3>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=11434275_molecules-29-04357-g002.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/30c2/11434275/b635c5412b5f/molecules-29-04357-g002.jpg" loading="lazy" height="331" width="735" alt="Figure 2"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/molecules-29-04357-f002/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>Aurelianolides A (<strong>1</strong>) and B (<strong>2</strong>).</p></figcaption></figure><p>In the last few years, some studies have assessed the pharmacological potential of withanolide compounds with promising results. Using a panel of leukemia cell lines, it was demonstrated that aurelianolide A displayed interesting cytotoxic activity against MOLT-4 cells (IC<sub>50</sub> = 1.17 μM), while aurelianolide B showed a stronger effect on Jurkat cells (IC<sub>50</sub> = 2.25 μM). Caspase activation was suggested as the key to apoptosis induction in Jurkat and K562-Lucena 1 cell lines, without necrosis induction [<a href="#B7-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-molecules-29-04357">7</a>]. Besides their anticancer activity, these compounds have also demonstrated significant toxic effects against tropical neglected disease parasites. Their potential leishmanicidal effects against promastigotes of <em>L. amazonensis</em> were evaluated as IC<sub>50</sub> 7.61 μM and 7.94 μM, while against the intracellular amastigotes, they displayed IC<sub>50</sub> values of 2.25 M and 6.43 M. In accordance with in silico predictions, aurelianolides A and B have good bioavailability via the oral route, showing a similar effect to miltefosine, the reference drug, and presenting low toxicity [<a href="#B8-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8-molecules-29-04357">8</a>]. In another study, these two compounds showed EC<sub>50</sub> values of 4.6 ± 1.3 μM and 1.6 ± 0.4 μM, respectively, against intracellular forms of <em>Trypanosoma cruzi</em>. Their ADMET parameters in silico revealed comparable results to benznidazole (Bz), the reference drug, while avoiding mutagenicity and hepatotoxicity associated with Bz [<a href="#B9-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-molecules-29-04357">9</a>]. </p>
<p>Over the last decades, different areas of Chemistry and Pharmacology have sought to understand the specific functions of natural products, as well as their potential use as therapeutic agents. Technological advances in analytical platforms combined with increased data processing capacity expanded the view of the metabolic functions of plants and their roles as alternative sources of chemically diverse bioactive metabolites. However, the unambiguous annotation and/or elucidation of “the entire” set of metabolites present in a biological system is still considered a challenging task in analytical terms. Currently, there is no single analytical technique able to measure the wide diversity of metabolites in a single experiment. Thus, orthogonal analytical techniques are needed to overcome individual deficiencies [<a href="#B10-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10-molecules-29-04357">10</a>]. The application of ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with electrospray with tandem (UHPLC-ESI-MS/MS) became an effective tool to rapidly characterize plant-extracted chemical constituents [<a href="#B11-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11-molecules-29-04357">11</a>]. Additionally, the integration of this technique with the Global Natural Product Social (GNPS) platform has streamlined the processing and interpretation of extensive MS2 spectral datasets, particularly in complex matrices of plant extracts and their partitions. This combination allows a comprehensive study of chemical profiles and facilitates bioactivity-based molecular networking, offering a viable and efficient methodology for natural product research [<a href="#B12-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12-molecules-29-04357">12</a>,<a href="#B13-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13-molecules-29-04357">13</a>]. Therefore, the present study represents the first systematic investigation of <em>Athenaea fasciculata</em> extracts and partitions to determine its phytochemical profile using UHPLC-MS/MS and molecular networking techniques with a focus on correlating the withanolides content with cytotoxicity against leukemia cells.</p></section><section id="sec2-molecules-29-04357"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Results and Discussion</h2>

However, AFFAc and AFFBu partitions exhibited comparatively lower cytotoxicity, while the AFFAq, with IC<sub>50</sub> &gt; 1000 µg/mL, had no relevant activity against all tested cell lines (<a href="#molecules-29-04357-t001" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>; <a href="#molecules-29-04357-f004" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>). </p>
<p>Our findings indicate that the AFFD partition is the most relevant bioactive sample, suggesting the presence of antitumoral compounds. In fact, in our previous study, two compounds named Aurelianolides A and B were isolated and characterized as major constituents of the AFFD partition and showed relevant anticancer activity against leukemic cells, mainly Jurkat, K-562, and K-562 Lucena cells, promoting caspase 3/7 activation, and apoptosis in Jurkat and K-562 Lucena 1 cells [<a href="#B7-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-molecules-29-04357">7</a>]. In the current study, the nonpolar AFFPH partition showed some cytotoxic activity, which can be attributed to the presence of other relevant active compounds; on the other hand, the AFFAc, AFFBu, and AFFAq partitions, mainly composed of phenolic-derivative compounds such as flavonoids and phenolic acids, had lower or no effects on the human leukemic cells tested.</p></section><section id="sec2dot2-molecules-29-04357"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Ultra-High-Performance Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry Analysis </h3>
<p>Three extracts of <em>A. fasciculata</em>, along with four partitions from its ethanolic extract and final aqueous residue, were each analyzed in triplicate (24 samples in total). Analysis was conducted using ultra-liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) with electrospray ionization (ESI) as the ionization source, operating in both positive and negative ionization modes. As expected, the methanolic and ethanolic extracts exhibited better ionization compared to the hexane extract, both in the negative mode (<a href="#molecules-29-04357-f005" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>). In both ionization modes, the base peak chromatograms of the methanolic and ethanolic extracts shared a similar profile, with some variations observed in peak intensities. </p>

Chemometric Analyses Using PLS Regression Model</h3>
<p>A Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression analysis model was constructed to correlate the LC-MS data with the cytotoxic activity of the extracts and partitions of <em>A. fasciculate</em>. The PLS regression utilized the LC-MS dataset (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> ions and Retention Time—RT) of 24 samples as independent variables (X), while the dependent variable (Y) reflected cytotoxic activities against the three leukemic cell lines. This algorithm integrates principal component analysis (PCA) and linear regression to identify latent variables optimizing the correlation between predictors and biological activity.</p>
<p>For the independent variables (X) dataset, a low-level data fusion strategy was employed. This involved processing spectral data separately from mass spectrometry analysis using ESI sources in positive and negative ionization modes, normalizing them, concatenating, and forming an independent data matrix. The resulting low-level data matrix, comprising 24 rows (samples) and 211 columns (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> ions and Retention Time), including 114 (M + H)<sup>+</sup> and 97 (M − H)<sup>−</sup>, underwent autoscaling. The dependent variables (Y) dataset comprised one column representing the potential multitarget activity of the samples. The samples were considered multitarget cytotoxic if they exhibited cytotoxic potential (IC<sub>50</sub> ≤ 100) against at least two investigated leukemia cell lines. Conversely, if IC<sub>50</sub> ≤ 100 was determined against only one target or against none of the targets, the samples were classified as non-multitarget cytotoxic. Multitarget cytotoxic extracts or partitions were designated as [<a href="#B1-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-molecules-29-04357">1</a>], while non-multitarget extracts were denoted as [−1].</p>
<p>The multitarget PLS model (<a href="#molecules-29-04357-f007" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>) achieved an explained variance of 88.5% with two factors and 99.6% with all factors (seven). The model demonstrated an R<sup>2</sup> of 0.97 and a Q<sup>2</sup> of 0.93, indicating a reliable fit; furthermore, it exhibited low RMSE values, with the RMSECV (validation error) at 0.28, slightly higher than the RMSEC (calibration error) at 0.17.</p>

Annotated Compounds for the Predicted Ions in the Multitarget Model Using Molecular Networking Analyses</h3>
<p>Molecular networking facilitates the analysis of mass spectrometry data by employing algorithms to systematically organize compounds into clusters within the same class based on their mass fragmentation similarities. Following this, dereplication is executed by comparing them with public databases or in-house libraries to annotate known metabolites [<a href="#B14-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14-molecules-29-04357">14</a>]. GNPS is an open-access web platform for constructing molecular networks from raw mass spectrometry data. It facilitates the organization, processing, and annotation of fragments. Additionally, both raw and processed data can be stored and shared [<a href="#B15-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15-molecules-29-04357">15</a>,<a href="#B16-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16-molecules-29-04357">16</a>]. </p>
<p>Based on this procedure, the 13 ions predicted in the negative ionization mode and the 11 ions predicted in the positive ionization mode were annotated as shown in <a href="#molecules-29-04357-t002" class="usa-link">Table 2</a> and <a href="#molecules-29-04357-t003" class="usa-link">Table 3</a>. However, two of them were annotated in both positive and negative modes, namely <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 508.9 [M − H]<sup>−</sup> and 510.9 [M + H]<sup>+</sup>, corresponding to compound <strong>8</strong> (physaminilide H), and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 510.9 [M − H]<sup>−</sup> and 512.9 [M + H]<sup>+</sup>, corresponding to compound <strong>9</strong> (aurelianolide B).</p>

<p>Many studies related to the structure–activity relationship (SAR) for this class of compounds suggest the antitumoral activity of withanolides is closely related to their structural features. Some key structural aspects that contribute to their bioactivity are summarized in <a href="#molecules-29-04357-f010" class="usa-link">Figure 10</a>. The presence of a lactone ring at C-22 and C-26 is pivotal for cytotoxic activity, facilitating interactions with target proteins and cellular membranes. Concerning 2-hydroxyl groups and acylation, the removal of hydroxyl groups at specific positions (C-4, C-19, and C-27) and their acylation can enhance bioavailability. This modification affects the ability of withanolides to interact with cellular targets and induce apoptosis in cancer cells. Furthermore, double bonds within the steroidal nucleus, particularly at C-1 and C-2, as well as C-5 and C-6, are correlated with increased antitumoral activity due to enhanced oxidative stress induction in tumor cells. Additionally, epoxide rings at positions like C-5 and C-6 or C-22 and C-23 contribute to the molecule’s reactivity, facilitating the formation of covalent bonds with biological molecules and thereby disrupting cancer cell proliferation and survival [<a href="#B37-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B37-molecules-29-04357">37</a>,<a href="#B38-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38-molecules-29-04357">38</a>]. </p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="molecules-29-04357-f010"><h4 class="obj_head">Figure 10.</h4>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/molecules-29-04357-f010/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>Key structural features associated with increased antitumoral activity. <strong>1</strong>—Lactone ring at C-22 and C-26. <strong>2</strong>—Acylation groups at C-4, C-19, and C-27. <strong>3</strong>—Double bonds at C-1 and C-2 as well as at C-5 and C-6. <strong>4</strong>—Epoxide groups at C-5 and C-6 or C-22 and C-23.</p></figcaption></figure><p>The importance of α, β-unsaturated ketone in ring A, 5,6-epoxy group, and a nine-carbon side chain substituted at C-17, featuring an α, β-unsaturated-δ-lactone is considered crucial to the cytotoxicity of this compound class [<a href="#B37-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B37-molecules-29-04357">37</a>,<a href="#B38-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38-molecules-29-04357">38</a>]. All five known compounds more cytotoxic (<strong>2</strong>, <strong>3</strong>, <strong>7</strong>, <strong>8</strong> and <strong>9</strong>) have the lactone ring and the α, β-unsaturated ketone in ring A, and only physaminilide H (<strong>8</strong>) and aurelianolide B (<strong>9</strong>) lack the epoxide ring. However, despite the absence of the 5,6-epoxy group, the cytotoxicity of physaminilide H (<strong>8</strong>) has already been reported in A375 human melanoma cells [<a href="#B23-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23-molecules-29-04357">23</a>], and the cytotoxicity of aurelianolide B (<strong>9</strong>) has been reported in leukemic cells [<a href="#B7-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-molecules-29-04357">7</a>]. The role of the 5,6-epoxy group can be observed by comparing the in vitro cytotoxicity of aurelianolides A (<strong>7</strong>) and B (<strong>9</strong>) against three leukemia cell lines. The influence of the epoxy group present in aurelianolide A (<strong>7</strong>) on molecule reactivity can be evidenced by the lower IC<sub>50</sub> values of 2.62 µM, 6.47 µM, and 1.41 µM across the three leukemia cell lines of K562, K562-Lucena 1, and Jurkat, respectively. Comparing with aurelianolide B (<strong>9</strong>), with no epoxy group on C5-C6, showed IC<sub>50</sub> values of 5.07 µM, 16.23 µM, and 2.25 µM for K562, K562-Lucena 1, and Jurkat cells, respectively. Thus, the presence of epoxy groups doubled the cytotoxic effect [<a href="#B7-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-molecules-29-04357">7</a>]. The 16-deoxyphiladelphicalactone C (<strong>2</strong>) molecule showed cytotoxic activity against a panel of MM cells [<a href="#B39-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B39-molecules-29-04357">39</a>]; however, it had no antiproliferative properties against LNCaP, ACHN, UO-31, M14, and SK-MEL-28 cells [<a href="#B18-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18-molecules-29-04357">18</a>]. Withaneomexolide A (<strong>3</strong>) did not show cytotoxicity against MDAMB-231 and MCF-7 cancer cells [<a href="#B19-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19-molecules-29-04357">19</a>]. </p>
<p>Among the other compounds with minor cytotoxic activity, only physagulide (<strong>16</strong>) has all three functional groups highlighted as relevant for the cytotoxicity of the withanolides. Nevertheless, it did not show growth inhibitory activity in three human cancer cell lines: human hepatoma (HepG2), human breast carcinoma (MCF-7), and human osteosarcoma (MG-63) cells [<a href="#B30-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30-molecules-29-04357">30</a>]. Some compounds such as virginol A (<strong>1</strong>), 20S,22R,24S,25S,26R/S)-15α-acetoxy-5,6β:22,26: diepoxy-24-methoxy-4β,25,26trihydroxyergost-2-en-1-one (<strong>4</strong>), physapubescin H (<strong>6</strong>), capsisteroid G (<strong>18</strong>), and capsisteroid D (<strong>19</strong>) lack an α, β-unsaturated-δ-lactone. Among these compounds, virginol A (<strong>1</strong>) has not yet been tested in tumor cells, while (20S,22R,24S,25S,26R/S)-15α-acetoxy-5,6β:22,26:diepoxy-24-methoxy-4β,25,26trihydroxyergost-2-en-1-one (<strong>4</strong>) has potent growth inhibitory effects against four human renal cell carcinoma (RCC) cell lines: 786-O, A-498, Caki-2, and ACHN [<a href="#B32-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32-molecules-29-04357">32</a>]. Physapubescin H (<strong>6</strong>) has shown cytotoxicity against prostate cancer cells (C4-2B and 22Rvl), renal carcinoma cells (786-O, A-498, Caki-2, and ACHN), and melanoma cells (A375 and A375-S2) [<a href="#B22-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22-molecules-29-04357">22</a>]. Capsisteroid G (18) had a moderate antiproliferative effect against erythroleukemia (K562), human promyelocytic leukemia (HL-60), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Molt-4), and liver and bile duct carcinoma (HuCCT1) cells [<a href="#B31-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31-molecules-29-04357">31</a>], while capsisteroid D (<strong>19</strong>) had no cytotoxicity against erythro myeloblastoid leukemia (K562), human acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Molt-4), and human promyelocytic leukemia (HL-60) cell lines [<a href="#B32-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32-molecules-29-04357">32</a>]. (4S,20S,22R)-4-Hydroxy-1-oxo-witha-2,5,16,24-tetraenolide (<strong>5</strong>) has shown a moderate cytotoxic activity contra HeLa, A-549, and MCF-7 [<a href="#B21-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21-molecules-29-04357">21</a>], while the cytotoxicity of withaperuvin N (<strong>12</strong>) and physanicandrolide B (<strong>15</strong>) have not been studied. Baimantuoluoline R (<strong>13</strong>) has moderate cytotoxicity against SGC-7901 [<a href="#B27-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27-molecules-29-04357">27</a>], and physaminilide B (<strong>14</strong>) exhibited significant antiproliferative activity against melanoma A375 [<a href="#B28-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28-molecules-29-04357">28</a>]. The molecules with the most different structures in this group are nicanlode C (<strong>10</strong>) and phenowithanolide (<strong>11</strong>). The first was not cytotoxic to HL-60, SMMC-7721, A-549, MCF-7, and SW480 [<a href="#B24-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B24-molecules-29-04357">24</a>], and phenowithanolide (<strong>11</strong>) did not have cytotoxic activity against human lung carcinoma cells (A549) and human colorectal adenocarcinoma cells (DLD-1) [<a href="#B25-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B25-molecules-29-04357">25</a>].</p></section></section><section id="sec3-molecules-29-04357"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec3dot1-molecules-29-04357"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Plant Material</h3>
<p>The species <em>Athenaea fasciculata</em> (Vell.) I.M.C. Rodrigues &amp; Stehmann was collected in the city of Simão Pereira (MG) and identified by the botanist Dr. Rita de Cassia Almeida-Lafetá (UFRJ), who deposited an exsiccate in the Herbarium of UFRJ, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, under number 40829. This study was registered in the National Genetic Heritage and Associated Traditional Knowledge Management System (SisGen) under the number AB5D582.</p></section><section id="sec3dot2-molecules-29-04357"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. A. fasciculata Sample Preparation (Extract and Partition)</h3>
<p>Fresh leaves of <em>A. fasciculata</em> were dried in an oven with air circulating at 40 °C for 48 h. After drying, the material (450.0 g) was reduced to small fragments in a knife mill. Part of the powder material (250 g) was extracted by dynamic maceration at room temperature, first with <em>n</em>-hexane for 48 h, followed by ethanol for 48 h. In addition, in a similar procedure, another 250 g of plant material was extracted with <em>n</em>-hexane for 48 h, followed by methanol for 48 h. Each extract was filtered and separately concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator with a heating bath R-210 Buchi (Flawil, Switzerland) equipped with a V-700 Buchi vacuum pump (Flawil, Switzerland), resulting in 11.35 g (<em>n</em>-hexane), 23.15 g (ethanol), and 35.10 g (methanol) crude extracts. The dry ethanolic extract obtained was then resuspended in MeOH/H<sub>2</sub>O (3:7) to obtain the liquid–liquid partitioning in increasing order of polarity with <em>n</em>-hexane, dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, and butanol (500 mL each). The solvents were evaporated under reduced pressure, yielding the following partitions: <em>n</em>-hexane (4.89 g), AFFD (dichloromethane, 4.10 g), AFFAc (ethyl acetate, 2.90 g), AFFBu (butanol, 6.75 g), and AFFAq (aqueous residue, 2.15 g). All solvents used were of spectroscopic grade and were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany.</p></section><section id="sec3dot3-molecules-29-04357"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3. Cytotoxicity Assays and Statistical Analysis</h3>
<p>The cytotoxicities of the extracts and partitions of <em>A. fasciculata</em> were assessed at various concentrations ranging from 0.015 µg/mL to 150 µg/mL against K562, K562-Lucena 1, and Jurkat human leukemia cell lines. Human myelogenous K562 (ATCC CCL-243), K562-Lucena 1 (BCRJ 0127; a multidrug-resistant subline of K562), and acute T cell leukemia Jurkat (ATCC TIB182) were kindly provided by Dr Juliana Echevarria-Lima (Paulo Góes Institute of Microbiology; UFRJ, Brazil).</p>
<p>Twenty-four hours before treatment with the samples, 100 μL of the cell suspension (5 × 10<sup>3</sup> cells/mL) was added to 96-well plates and maintained in a 5% CO<sub>2</sub> atmosphere at 37 °C. Treatment with the aurelianolides was performed as mentioned above, with each concentration in triplicate. The negative control consisted of cells incubated with vehicle (0.25% of cell culture-grade DMSO in medium; SIGMA), while staurosporine (1 µM) was used as the positive control. After 48 h, 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT; SIGMA) was added to the wells and the plates maintained in a CO<sub>2</sub> incubator for 4 h. Following this incubation period, the plates were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for ten minutes, and the supernatant was aspirated. Formazan crystals, formed by cellular activity, were solubilized with DMSO (SIGMA) [<a href="#B7-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-molecules-29-04357">7</a>]. </p>
<p>Absorbance was measured at 540 nm wavelength, using a microplate reader (Synergy H1M2F; Biotek, Shoreline, WA, USA). The IC<sub>50</sub> values were determined using GraphPad Prism version 5 (San Diego, CA, USA).</p></section><section id="sec3dot4-molecules-29-04357"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.4. Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry Analysis</h3>
<p>The samples, at a concentration of 2 μg·mL<sup>−1</sup>, underwent analysis utilizing a UHPLC Dionex Ultimate 3000 system (ThermoFischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) connected to an LCQfleet mass spectrometer (ThermoFischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A Supelcosil LC-18 (5 μm) column was utilized for chromatography. The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B). The flow rate was maintained at 0.30 mL.min<sup>−1</sup> and the elution gradient programming was as follows: 0–0.6 min (10% B), 0.6–2.8 min (10–40% B), 2.8–3.3 min (40% B), 3.3–5.6 min (40–50% B), 5.6–6.1 min (50% B), 6.1–7.8 min (50–60% B), 7.8–8.3 min (60% B), 8.3–9.2 min (60–80% B), 9.2–10.6 min (80% B), 10.6–12 min (80–10% B), and 12–15.3 min (10% B). </p>
<p>The mass spectrometer, equipped with an electrospray ionization source (ESI) and an ion trap analyzer, was operated in positive and negative ionization modes. Mass spectra were acquired within the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> range of 100–1000. Collision-induced dissociation (CID) was performed with a normalized collision energy of 35 eV, using high-purity helium (He) as the collision gas. MS/MS fragmentation (TANDEM) of the three most intense ions was performed with counts above 100 per scan. Each analysis was conducted in triplicate, resulting in a total of 24 analyses.</p></section><section id="sec3dot5-molecules-29-04357"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.5. Data Processing and Chemometric Analysis</h3>
<p>The LC-MS data were converted to the mzML format using the Proteowizard—MSconvert version 3.02 tool. Subsequently, MZmine v. 2.53 software was employed for data processing, including mass detection, mass chromatogram construction, wavelet deconvolution, isotopic removal, and data alignment. The processed chemical data (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> ions and retention time), along with the biological data (IC<sub>50</sub>), were exported to UnscramblerX software version 10.4 for analysis. The objective was to predict potential cytotoxic compounds against the three leukemia cell lines. Chemometric analyses were conducted using low-level data fusion (concatenated ESI [+/−] data) and a Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression model. In the PLS regression, samples were categorized into two groups (binary values) based on average IC<sub>50</sub> values: cytotoxic samples (IC<sub>50</sub> ≤ 100 μg·mL<sup>−1</sup>—[<a href="#B1-molecules-29-04357" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-molecules-29-04357">1</a>]) and non-cytotoxic samples (IC<sub>50</sub> &gt; 100 μg−mL<sup>−1</sup>—[−1]). The NIPALS algorithm was employed to construct the PLS model, along with the Leave-One-Out cross-validation method.</p></section><section id="sec3dot6-molecules-29-04357"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.6. Molecular Networking and Annotation of Compounds</h3>
<p>The data obtained from MZmine were uploaded to the open-source Global Natural Products Social (GNPS) online platform, along with the converted files (.mzML) and metadata table. These files were utilized to construct molecular networks and dereplicate the predicted compounds using a Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression model. In addition to GNPS annotation, a custom database was employed. The analyses adhered to the parameters recommended in the GNPS documentation for unity resolution data. The data generated by GNPS were then downloaded and processed in Cytoscape 3.9.1 to extract the results of the molecular networking analysis.</p></section></section><section id="sec4-molecules-29-04357"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Conclusions</h2>
<p>Currently, a substantial number of withanolides have been isolated and characterized. These compounds have attracted significant attention due to their promising potential as antitumoral agents, owing to their ability to target multiple pathways related to their complex structural features. Therefore, understanding the SAR of withanolides is essential for the development of new and more effective anticancer therapies, as well as for the creation of novel analogs with enhanced efficacy and reduced toxicity. In this study, UHPLC-MS/MS, in combination with bioactivity-based molecular networking (GNPS), provided a comprehensive and rapid methodology for the identification of 22 known withanolides derived from <em>Athenaea fasciculata</em>. This approach facilitated the correlation of ions associated with the most cytotoxic compounds, present in the dichloromethane partition of the ethanolic extract of <em>A. fasciculata</em>, across various leukemia cell lines such as Jurkat, K562, and K562-Lucena 1. Furthermore, an integrated, practical, and targeted data processing strategy was employed alongside an in-house database, enabling the rapid profiling of these constituents for the first time. The withanolides identified in this study warrant further isolation for in vitro or in vivo activity screening. Ongoing research into <em>A. fasciculata</em> continues to reveal its potential as a powerful source of new natural alternative compounds in the fight against cancer and holds promise for future drug research and development.</p></section><section id="app1-molecules-29-04357" class="app"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Materials</h2>
