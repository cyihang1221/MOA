</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ABS1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="P3">The <em>Aesculus</em> genus has several species, most notably <em>Aesculus hippocastanum</em>, with reported cytotoxic and venotonic effects. The angiogenic effect from <em>A. hippocastanum</em> is of interest given the current limitations of anti-angiogenic therapeutics. <em>Aesculus glabra</em> (Willdenow), known as the Ohio Buckeye Tree is a species native to North America with reported medicinal use by the Native Americans. Previous phytochemical studies have focused on the seed and leaf contents of the <em>glabra</em> species, with most of them reporting cytotoxic activity. In this study, we assessed preliminary anti-angiogenic activity and toxicity of isolated compounds from the bark of <em>A. glabra</em> utilizing a zebrafish (<em>Danio rerio</em>) model. Procyanidin A2 and epicatechin, two pure isolates, were tested using zebrafish and gave an anti-angiogenic response, suggesting an underlying mechanism involved in vascular development.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> <em>Aesculus</em>, Ohio Buckeye Tree, Angiogenesis, Zebrafish, GNPS, LC-MS</p></section></section><section id="S1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="P4">Angiogenesis, the formation of blood vessels from pre-existing vessels, is an important process in the progression of many diseases, including vascular disorders and cancer. In cancer, angiogenesis is a necessary process for tumor growth and the metastatic spread of cancer tissue, thus making the inhibition of vessel formation a target for cancer therapy (<a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">Nishida et al., 2006</a>; <a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">Serbedzija et al., 1999</a>). Most anti-angiogenic agents that are approved for cancer treatment target the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which is considered the main angiogenesis promoter (<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">Lopes-Coelho et al., 2021</a>). In most cases, current anti-angiogenic therapies have been ineffective due to resistance from adaptive and compensatory mechanisms; hence, the need for innovative and robust research on anti-angiogenic therapeutics (<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">Lopes-Coelho et al., 2021</a>).</p>
<p id="P5">Natural products are promising agents that have made progress in drug development of angiogenic agents (<a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">Li et al., 2021</a>; <a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">Yu et al., 2017</a>). Phytochemical studies have found isolates from the <em>Aesculus</em> genus that possess a variety of biological activities including anti-angiogenic properties (<a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">Yu et al., 2017</a>). <em>Aesculus hippocastanum</em> is one of the most common <em>Aesculus</em> species used in folk and traditional medicine. Aescin (β-escin) has been reported as the major active component from the seeds of <em>A. hippocastanum</em> with anti-angiogenic and venotonic properties (<a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">Zhang et al. 2010</a>; <a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">Sirtori, 2001</a>). To date, most research has focused on the Eurasian <em>Aesculus</em> species. Research on other species in the <em>Aesculus</em> genus is limited and might be necessary to confirm chemotaxonomic and therapeutic value of this widely used botanical and to understand the chemical profile of the genus.</p>
<p id="P6"><em>Aesculus glabra</em> Willd, commonly known as the Ohio Buckeye Tree, is an understudied species with a recent report of cytotoxic compounds isolated from the seeds (<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">Yuan et al., 2012</a>). <em>A. glabra</em> is native to the Midwest region of the United States and is the state tree of Ohio. It was traditionally used by the Native American`s as a medicinal remedy to soothe aches/pain and to stun fish (<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">Harris, 2003</a>). To date, phytochemical research of <em>A. glabra</em> has focused on studying the seed and leaf contents of the species with no research dedicated to the contents of the bark (<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">Yuan et al., 2012</a>; <a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">Aurada et al., 1984</a>; <a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">Oszmiański et al., 2015</a>). In this study, we analyzed components from the bark of <em>A. glabra</em> and assessed preliminary anti-angiogenic activity and potential toxicity of the isolates in a zebrafish (<em>Danio rerio</em>) model. Phytochemical analysis of the bark extracts resulted in the isolation of seven compounds from different chemical classes including: procyanidin A2 (<strong>1</strong>), epicatechin (<strong>2</strong>), aesculin 6-rutinoside (<strong>3</strong>), isoscopoletin-β-D-glucoside (<strong>4</strong>), 1-octadecanol (<strong>5</strong>), eriocaffeate (<strong>6</strong>), and hexacosyl-(E)- ferulate (<strong>7</strong>). It also resulted in the identification of coumarins, flavonoid glycosides, flavans, fatty acids and their conjugates, and carbohydrate groups including coumarin glucosides through LC/MS analysis. Procyanidin A2 and epicatechin, two isolates identified in this study revealed to have an anti-angiogenic effect on the vasculature of zebrafish embryos.</p></section><section id="S2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Experimental</h2>
<section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. General Experimental Procedures</h3>
<p id="P7">Analytical grade chemical solvents used for extraction and column chromatography were acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Column chromatography was performed using silica-gel (particle size 40 x 63 µm, 230–400 mesh or 250–125 µm 70–230 mesh) from Sorbent Technologies, (Norcross, GA, USA). Analytical TLC was performed on precoated silica gel TLC plates (aluminum-backed, thickness 200 µm, UV254 fluorescent, Sorbent Technologies). Visualization of the plates was carried out via UV light or vanillin detection reagent (1% vanillin in ethanol mixed with an equal portion of 3% perchloric acid in water). Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) deuterated solvents used for spectroscopy experiments, include chloroform-<em>d</em> (CDCl<sub>3</sub>), methanol-<em>d</em><sub>4</sub> (CD<sub>3</sub>OD), dimethyl sulfoxide-<em>d</em><sub>6</sub> (DMSO-<em>d</em><sub>6</sub>) (Fisher Scientific) and pyridine-<em>d</em><sub>5</sub> from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, Missouri, USA). Structure elucidation of isolates was performed using spectroscopic techniques such as Bruker AVIII400 NMR instrument for 1D- and 2D NMR. Thermo Q-Exactive Orbitrap with Vanquish-H UHPLC (LC-ESI-HRMS) instrument in the positive, and negative ion mode for analysis of isolated compounds. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra of isolated compounds were recorded on the JASCO model J810 polarimeter in MeOH.</p></section><section id="S4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Plant Material</h3>
<p id="P8">Branches of <em>A. glabra</em> were collected from Chadwick Arboretum and Learning Gardens (Columbus, OH) and identified by Anna Williamson. A voucher specimen (OS-374304) was deposited at The Ohio State University Herbarium (Columbus, OH).</p></section><section id="S5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Extraction and Isolation</h3>
<p id="P9">A total of 630 g of bark material was obtained by removing bark from the collected branches. The air-dried and ground bark material of <em>A. glabra</em> (630 g) was extracted with MeOH, DCM and hexane for three days (3 X 5L per solvent) and dried <em>in vacuo</em> to afford MeOH (59.7 g), DCM (5.8 g), and hexane (1.2 g) extracts. The DCM extract was chosen to perform further isolation due to better analytical TLC resolution and separation, as no anti-proliferative activity was observed when tested against a panel of cancer cell lines. The DCM extract (5.8 g) was then fractionated by silica gel column chromatography (70–230 mesh) using a solvent gradient of hexane- EtOAc (100:0 to 0:100, v/v), to afford 10 subfractions (F1-F10), which were grouped according to TLC profiles. Fraction F2 (266 mg) was fractionated on a silica gel column (230–400 mesh) using a solvent gradient of hexane-EtOAc (100:0 to 0:100, v/v), to afford 96 sub-fractions, of which subfraction 21 (30 mg) was further separated using preparative TLC with mobile phase hexane- EtOAc (55:45, v/v) to yield compound <strong>5</strong> (5.0 mg). Fraction F3 (225 mg) was fractionated on a silica gel column (230–400 mesh) using a solvent gradient of hexane-EtOAc (100:0 to 0:100, v/v), to afford 127 subfractions, of which subfraction 9 (11.3 mg) was analyzed by preparative TLC with mobile phase hexane-EtOAc (8:2, v/v) to yield compound <strong>7</strong> (5.0 mg) and subfraction 45 (26 mg) was separated on a Sephadex LH-20 column using a solvent gradient of MeOH-CHCl<sub>3</sub>(100:0 to 50:50, v/v), to yield compound <strong>6</strong> (6.0 mg). Similarly, fractions F5 (523 mg) and F6 (179 mg) were each further fractionated on a Sephadex LH-20 column with a solvent gradient of MeOH-CHCl<sub>3</sub>(100:0 to 50:50, v/v) to provide subfractions 29 and 24, and yielded compounds <strong>2</strong> (29.0 mg) and <strong>1</strong> (10.0 mg), respectively. Fraction F7 (100 mg) was chromatographed on a Sephadex LH-20 column with a solvent gradient of MeOH-CHCl<sub>3</sub> (100:0 to 50:50, v/v) to yield 40 subfractions, of which subfraction 16 was evaluated using prep. TLC with mobile phase DCM-MeOH (8:2, v/v) to yield compounds <strong>3</strong> (3.4 mg) and <strong>4</strong> (1.3 mg). The purity of isolated compounds <strong>1–7</strong> was confirmed by analytical TLC profiling under UV light and vanillin solution, HPLC, and NMR.</p></section><section id="S6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. LC-MS Analysis of the MeOH extract from the bark of A. glabra</h3>
<p id="P10">The MeOH extract was analyzed using an UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system, coupled to a Q-Exactive plus hybrid quadrupole Orbitrap spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). An HSS T3 column (1.0 × 100 mm, 3.5 µm, Waters, MA, USA) was used to analyze the samples with an eluent mixture containing water 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (B) as the mobile phase. A gradient program was used as follows: 0 min, 5% B; 20 min, 95% B; 25 min, 95% B. The flow rate was 200 μL/min. The column temperature was 35 °C and the injection volume was 5μL.</p>
<p id="P11">The MS parameters were set as follows: negative ion mode; spray voltage: − 4.5 kV; sheath gas flow rate: 30 arb; auxiliary gas: 8 arb; capillary temperature: 320 °C; S-lens RF level: 45 V; scan mode: Full Scan (resolution 70,000 FWHM) and dd-MS2 (resolution 17,500 FWHM); scan range: <em>m/z</em> 100–1000. Normalized collision energy (NCE) was set at 30%. The top 5 abundant ions in each scan cycle were selected as precursor ions to obtain their MS/MS spectra. Data were processed using Xcalibur™ 4.1 software (Thermo Fisher).</p></section><section id="S7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Featured Based Molecular networking (FBMN) and Network Annotation Propagation (NAP) analysis</h3>
<p id="P12">The mass spectrometry data were processed using MZmine 3 (see <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary data</a>), and the resulting data were exported to GNPS for FBMN analysis (<a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">Nothias et al., 2020</a>). To filter the data, ions from solvent blank samples and all MS/MS fragment ions within ± 17 Da of the precursor <em>m/z</em> were removed. MS/MS spectra were window filtered by selecting only the top six fragment ions in the ± 50 Da window throughout the spectrum. The precursor ion mass tolerance was set to 0.5 Da, and the MS/MS fragment ion tolerance was set to 0.1 Da.</p>
<p id="P13">A network was created, and the edges were filtered based on their cosine score, keeping only those with a score greater than 0.65 and more than 6 matched peaks. Edges between two nodes were retained only if each node was in the other’s top 10 most similar nodes. The maximum molecular family size was limited to 100, and edges with the lowest scores were removed until the size was below this threshold. The spectra were searched against GNPS spectral libraries, with matches required to have a score above 0.6 and at least 4 matched peaks. Additional edges were included as well.</p>
<p id="P14">The MN job can be accessed at: <a href="http://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=93339048ced94c5d86f92e0b415b5057" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=93339048ced94c5d86f92e0b415b5057</a>. Subsequently, the molecular network was annotated using the Network Annotation Propagation (NAP) workflow on the GNPS website. The top 10 candidates were considered for consensus re-ranking, and candidates were searched within a 5 ppm window in COCONUT, GNPS, SuperNatural, and an i<em>n-house</em> DNP database. [M - H]<sup>−</sup> and [M + HCOO]<sup>−</sup> adducts were expected for the precursor ion mass. The NAP job can be accessed at: <a href="https://proteomics2.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=532d8289dc8f432c9ea92352fdb5f344" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://proteomics2.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=532d8289dc8f432c9ea92352fdb5f344</a>.</p>
<p id="P15">The <em>in-silico</em> structure annotations tool Network Annotation Propagation (NAP) [30] was utilized to incorporate information into the molecular network (MN) using the GNPS MolNetEnhancer workflow. The ClassyFire chemical ontology was used to perform chemical class annotations. The MN was then visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.1.</p></section><section id="S8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Cell Culture</h3>
<p id="P16">Cancer cells HT-1376 (bladder), MDA-T32 (thyroid), Pancreatic (HPAC), prostate (DU-145), cervical (HeLa), breast (MDA-MB-231), and myeloid leukemia (BDCM) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA. Cells were cultured in Eaglès Minimum Essential medium (EMEM), Roswell Park Memorial Institute’s medium (RPMI-1640), or Dulbeccòs modified Eaglès medium (DMEM) containing 5 % or 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic from Gibco (Rockville, MD, USA). HPAC cells were cultured in 1:1 mixture of Dulbeccós modified Eaglés medium and Hamś F12 medium (DMEM: F12) containing 1.2 g/L sodium bicarbonate, 2.5 mM L-glutamine, 15 mM HEPES and 0.5 mM sodium pyruvate. In the case of DMEM: F12, 0.002 mg/ml insulin, 0.005 mg/ml transferrin, 40 ng/ml hydrocortisone, 10 ng/ml epidermal growth factor were added. The HeLa and DU-145 cells were cultured with Dulbecco’s Modification of Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) containing 4.5 g/L glucose, L-glutamine and sodium pyruvate. The cells were grown as a monolayer in T75 tissue culture flasks and kept at 37 °C and in an atmosphere with 5% CO2.</p></section><section id="S9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.7. Antiproliferative Assays</h3>
<p id="P17">The antiproliferative activity of compounds and extracts were determined on human cancer cells lines, using the sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay as previously reported (<a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">Anaya-Eugenio et al., 2019</a>). Cells were seeded on a 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 10<sup>4</sup> cells/mL. The compounds were added at different concentrations (0.16 to 20 μg/ml) to the plates. Each cell suspension was incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO<sub>2</sub> for 72 h. After this, cells were fixed with 20 % cold trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and then washed with tap water to be dried at room temperature. Cells were stained with sulforhodamine B (0.4%), and unbound dye was washed with 1% acetic acid. After drying bound dye was solubilized with Tris-base (10 mM), and absorbance was read at 492 nm using a FLUOstar Optima plate reader (BMG Labtech Inc., Durham, NC, USA). Paclitaxel was used as positive control.</p></section><section id="S10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.8. Toxicity Assessment</h3>
<p id="P18">A zebrafish (<em>Danio rerio</em>) model (embryos 24 h post-fertilization, hpf) was used to evaluate the developmental toxicity of selected compounds based on chemical class and on respective yield. This assay was performed following a previously reported procedure (<a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">Anaya-Eugenio et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">Mirtallo Ezzone et al., 2022</a>). Embryos were provided by the Department of Neuroscience, The Ohio State University (OSU). All protocols and procedures were approved by The Ohio State University`s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (OSU IACUC protocol number 2014A00000006 entitled “Vertebrate Model for Natural Product Drug Discovery”; PI: Esperanza J. Carcache de Blanco). At 24 hpf, embryos were exposed to procyanidin A2, epicatechin, cycloheximide, or vehicle (1% DMSO in water) at 50 µM for 24 and 48 hours. Zebrafish embryos were observed with a Axiovert 40 CFL Zeiss microscope (magnifier Zeiss CP-Achromat 5×/0.12) and pictures of treated zebrafish were taken using a ProRes C10 plus camera at 24 h and 48 h.</p></section><section id="S11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.9. Angiogenesis Assessment</h3>
<p id="P19">A zebrafish model (<em>Danio rerio</em>) was used to evaluate anti-angiogenic potential of compounds isolated in sufficient quantities from different chemical classes/categories. Healthy fertilized embryos at 48 hpf were treated with VEGF (Fisher Scientific) for 30 min and then treated with selected compounds at three doses (10, 20, and 40 µg/mL), DMSO (1%) or the control methyl rocaglate (0.25 µg/mL). After 24 h of treatment, embryos were returned to normal water system and were visually observed for viability, morphological defects, and blood circulation under a Axiovert 40 CFL Zeiss microscope (magnifier Zeiss CP-Achromat 5×/0.12). At 72 hpf, embryos were euthanized and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C overnight. Oil Red O staining was carried out to visualize intersegmental vessels and zebrafish vasculature under an Axiovert 40 CFL Zeiss microscope (magnifier Zeiss CP-Achromat 5×/0.12). Pictures were taken using a ProRes C10 plus camera.</p></section><section id="S12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.10. Oil Red O Staining</h3>
<p id="P20">Oil Red O staining was performed according to a modified protocol (<a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">Chen et al., 2015</a>). Embryos were fixed overnight at 4°C with 4% paraformaldehyde fixation. Then, embryos were preincubated with 60% isopropanol for 30 min and stained with fresh 0.5% (w/v) Oil Red for 3h. Samples were then washed three times with 60% isopropanol and observed under an Axiovert 40 CFL Zeiss microscope (magnifier Zeiss CP-Achromat 5×/0.12), pictures were taken using a ProRes C10 plus camera.</p></section></section><section id="S13"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results and Discussion</h2>
<section id="S14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Isolated Compounds</h3>
<p id="P21">The DCM extract of the bark of <em>Aesculus glabra</em> Willd was subjected to column chromatography and preparative TLC, which resulted in the isolation of the following seven compounds (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>), procyanidin A2 (<strong>1</strong>), epicatechin (<strong>2</strong>), aesculin 6-rutinoside (<strong>3)</strong>, isoscopoletin-β-D-glucoside (<strong>4</strong>), 1-octadecanol (<strong>5</strong>), eriocaffeate (<strong>6</strong>) and hexacosyl-(E)-ferulate (<strong>7</strong>). The structures of the reported compounds were identified by comparison of their spectroscopic data with those reported in the literature (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figures S1</a>−<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">S24</a>, <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Material</a>). Compounds <strong>1, 2</strong>, and <strong>4</strong> have been isolated previously from plants of the genus <em>Aesculus (</em><a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">Zhang et al. 2010</a>; <a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">Owczarek et al., 2021a</a>; <a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">Owczarek et al., 2021b</a>; <a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">Green et al., 2021</a>). The isolation of compounds <strong>3</strong> and <strong>5–7</strong> from <em>Aesculus glabra</em> is reported for the first time in this species. In addition, TLC analysis revealed that these compounds were present in the DCM extract prepared from the dry material.</p>

<p id="P23">Compound <strong>2</strong> was obtained as yellow powder. Compound <strong>2</strong> was identified as epicatechin (<strong>2</strong>) by comparing its <sup>1</sup>H and <sup>13</sup>C NMR data (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S7</a>−<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">S8</a>), and ECD (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S-10</a>) with those previously described (<a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">Barrett et al., 1979</a>; <a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">Lv et al., 2015</a>).</p>
<p id="P24">Compound <strong>3</strong> was obtained as green amorphous powder. The <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum displayed the signals at δ<sub>H</sub> 7.74 (d, <em>J</em>= 9.2 Hz, H-4, 1 H) and δ<sub>H</sub> 5.92 (d, <em>J</em>= 9.2 Hz, H-3, H-1), two aromatic singlets at δ<sub>H</sub> 7.12 (s, H-5, 1 H), 6.50 (s, H-8, 1 H), and the signals for the protons of two hexose sugars. A rhamnose could be assumed from the occurrence of a CH<sub>3</sub> doublet at δ<sub>H</sub> 1.24 (<em>J</em>= 6.3 Hz) for the methyl group (C-6’’) and two anomeric protons appeared as a doublet at δ<sub>H</sub> 4.75 (<em>J</em>= 1.7 Hz), and a doublet at δ<sub>H</sub> 4.70 (<em>J</em>= 7.5 Hz). The <sup>13</sup>C-NMR spectrum of <strong>3</strong> showed typical carbon signals at δ<sub>C</sub> 165.8 (C-2), 154.8 (C-9), 148.5 (C-6), 147.1 (C-4), 147.1 (C-7), 114.4 (C-5), 108.1 (C-10), 107.0 (C-3), 105.7 (C-8), indicating that its structure contains a coumarin. In the HMBC (Heteronuclear Multiple Bond Correlation) NMR spectrum, the rhamnose anomeric proton (H-1’’) showed correlation with C-6’ of glucose, to establish the point of attachment between rhamnose and glucose to form the rutinose skeleton. The most relevant HMBC correlation was H-1’ (δ<sub>H</sub> 4.70) with C-6 (δ<sub>C</sub> 148.5), the attachment of this aglycone part at C-6 between H-1’ of the glucose. Although <strong>3</strong> is a known metabolite, spectroscopic and spectrometric data are not available. Compound <strong>3</strong> was identified as aesculin 6-rutinoside (<strong>3</strong>), by comparing its <sup>1</sup>H and <sup>13</sup>C NMR data with the reported data for similar compounds (<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">Kim et al., 2016</a>; <a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">Sarker et al., 1995</a>). Based on the analysis of compound <strong>3</strong>, 2D NMR data (HSQC and HMBC experiments), the assignments of several carbon atoms in <strong>3</strong> should be revised as indicated.</p>
<p id="P25">Compound <strong>4</strong> was isolated as a white amorphous powder. The HRESIMS displayed a molecular peak at <em>m/z</em> 399.0939 [M + HCOO]<sup>‒</sup> indicated the empirical formula C<sub>16</sub>H<sub>18</sub>O<sub>9</sub>. The <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum of <strong>4</strong> displayed to identify the signals that correspond to α-pyrone ring at δ<sub>H</sub> 7.86 (d, <em>J</em>= 9.5 Hz, H-4, 1 H) and at δ<sub>H</sub> 6.25 (d, <em>J</em>= 9.5 Hz, H-3, 1 H) and two aromatic singlets at δ<sub>H</sub> 7.34 (s, H-5, 1 H) and at δ<sub>H</sub> 7.04 (s, H-8, 1 H) which correspond to the benzene ring. The signals observed at δ<sub>H</sub> 4.90 (d, <em>J</em>= 7.2 Hz, H-1’, 1 H), 3.69 (d, <em>J</em>= 7.0 Hz, H-2’, 1 H), 3.69–3.65 (m, H-4’, 1 H), 3.63 (dd, <em>J</em>= 11.9, 2.2 Hz, H-6’a, 1 H), 3.42 (m, H-5’, 1 H), 3.23 (dd, <em>J</em>= 6.1, 2.2 Hz, 6’b, 1 H), 3.14 (d, <em>J</em>= 9.2 Hz, H-3’, 1 H) confirmed the presence of a glucose moiety. Also, the presence of a singlet at δ<sub>H</sub> 3.82 (s, OCH<sub>3</sub>, 3 H), revealed that <strong>4</strong> has a methoxyl group. The <sup>13</sup>C-NMR spectrum of <strong>4</strong> showed carbon signals at δ<sub>C</sub> 160.5 (C-2), 152.8 (C-7), 150.0 (C-9), 144.4 (C-4), 143.4 (C-6), 113.1 (C-5), 112.8 (C-3), 111.3 (C-10), 100.4 (C-8) that correspond to the coumarin nucleus. The HMBC NMR spectrum showed that the glucose anomeric proton H-1’ (δ<sub>H</sub> 4.90) had a correlation with C-6 (δ<sub>C</sub> 143.4) and OCH<sub>3</sub> (δ<sub>H</sub> 3.82) with C-7 (δ<sub>C</sub> 152.8). All these spectroscopic data allowed us to confirm that <strong>4</strong> is a glycosylated coumarin known as isoscopoletin -β-D-glucoside (<strong>4</strong>) (<a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29">Tsukamoto et al., 1985</a>).</p>
<p id="P26">Compound <strong>5</strong> was obtained as white amorphous powder. Its APCI-MS displayed a molecular ion peak at <em>m/z</em> 271.3001 [M + H]<sup>+</sup> (calc. 270.2923 for C<sub>18</sub>H<sub>38</sub>O) corresponding to the molecular formula C<sub>18</sub>H<sub>38</sub>O in conjunction with NMR data. The <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum of <strong>5</strong> displayed to identify the signals that correspond methylene attached to a hydroxyl at δ<sub>H</sub> 3.64 (t, <em>J</em>= 6.6 Hz, H-1), and signals at δ<sub>H</sub> 1.56 (m, H-2), 1.32 (m, H-3), 1.26 (m, H-4-17), and 0.88 (t, <em>J</em>= 6.9 Hz, H-18) that correspond to the aliphatic chain. The <sup>13</sup>C-NMR spectrum of <strong>5</strong> showed carbon signals at δ<sub>C</sub> 63.3 (C-1), that confirmed the presence of an alcohol attached to C-1, and other set at δ<sub>C</sub> 33.0 (C-2), 32.1 (C-16), 29.9 (C-6-C-14), 29.8 (C-5), 29.6 (C-4), 29.5 (C-15), 25.9 (C-3), 22.9 (C-17), and 14.2 (C-18) that correspond to the aliphatic chain. Compound <strong>5</strong> was identified as 1-octadecanol (<strong>5</strong>) by comparing its <sup>1</sup>H and <sup>13</sup>C NMR data (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S17</a>−<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">S18</a>) (<a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">Zhang et al., 2010</a>].</p>
<p id="P27">Compound <strong>6</strong> was obtained as white amorphous powder. Its APCI-MS displayed a molecular ion peak at <em>m/z</em> 517.4247 [M + H]<sup>+</sup> (calc. 516.4179 for C<sub>33</sub>H<sub>56</sub>O<sub>4</sub>) corresponding to the molecular formula C<sub>33</sub>H<sub>56</sub>O<sub>4</sub> in conjunction with NMR data. The <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum of <strong>6</strong> displayed two main sets of signals including a set of resonances at δ<sub>H</sub> 8.05 (d, <em>J</em>= 15.8 Hz, H-β, 1 H), 7.66 (d, <em>J</em>= 1.8 Hz, H-2, 1 H), 7.25 (d, <em>J</em>= 8.2 Hz, H-5, H-6, overlapped, 2 H), 6.69 (d, <em>J</em>= 15.9 Hz, H-α, 1 H) assignable to protons of an aromatic ring with an <em>α</em>,<em>β</em>-unsaturated ketone, and the other set at δ<sub>H</sub> 4.31 (t, <em>J</em>= 6.7 Hz, H-1’, 2 H), 1.70 (p, <em>J</em>= 6.5 Hz, H-2’, 2 H), 1.32 (brs, H-3’-H-23’, 42 H), 0.88 (t, <em>J</em>= 6.6 Hz, H-24’, 3 H) suggestive for the presence of a fatty acid moiety on the structure of <strong>6</strong>. The presence of two hydroxyl groups was also shown by a broad signal at δ<sub>H</sub> 4.95. The <sup>13</sup>C-NMR spectrum of <strong>6</strong> had typical carbon signals at δ<sub>C</sub> 168.0 (CO), 151.0 (C-4), 148.2 (C-3), 146.2 (C-β), 127.4 (C-1), 122.5 (C-6), 117.2 (C-5), 116.3 (C-2), 115.6 (C-α), indicating that its structure contains a caffeoyl basic skeleton. Further <sup>13</sup>C-NMR signals observed at 64.9 (C-1’), 32.6 (C-22’), 30.5–30.0 (C-4’-C-21’), 29.6 (C-2’), 26.8 (C-3’), 23.4 (C-23’), 14.7 (C-24’) confirmed the presence of an alcohol moiety in <strong>6</strong>. All the <sup>1</sup>H and <sup>13</sup>C data of <strong>6</strong> were fully assigned based on HSQC, HMBC, and <sup>1</sup>H <sup>1</sup>H COSY experiments and compound <strong>6</strong> was characterized as eriocaffeate (<strong>6</strong>) (<a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">Tabakam et al., 2021</a>).</p>
<p id="P28">Compound <strong>7</strong> was obtained as white amorphous powder. The APCI-MS analysis of <strong>7</strong> showed the [M + H]<sup>+</sup> ion at <em>m/z</em> 559.4713, suggesting the molecular weight of 558.4648 and solving for C<sub>36</sub>H<sub>62</sub>O<sub>4</sub> which was consistent with the structure of hexacosyl-(<em>E</em>)-ferulate (<strong>7</strong>). The <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectrum showed signals characteristics for ferulic acid moiety at δ<sub>H</sub> 7.61 (d, <em>J</em>= 15.9 Hz, H-β, 1 H), 7.07 (dd, <em>J</em>= 8.2, 1.9 Hz, H-6, 1 H), 7.03 (d, <em>J</em>= 1.9 Hz, H-2, 1 H), 6.91 (d, <em>J</em>= 8.1 Hz, H-5, 1 H), 6.30 (d, <em>J</em>= 15.9 Hz, H-α, 1 H), 5.84 (brs, OH), 3.93 (s, 3-OCH<sub>3</sub>). Also, the <sup>13</sup>C-NMR spectrum of <strong>7</strong> displayed signals characteristics for ferulic acid moiety at δ<sub>C</sub> 167.2 (CO), 147.7 (C-4), 146.6 (C-3), 144.5 (C-β), 126.9 (C-1), 122.9 (C-6), 115.6 (C-α), 114.5 (C-5), 109.2 (C-2), 55.8 (3-OCH<sub>3</sub>). The hexacosyl unit displayed signals at δ<sub>H</sub> 4.18 (td, <em>J</em>= 6.7, 2.0 Hz, H-1’), 1.69 (m, H-2’), 1.56 (m, H-3’), 1.26 (brs, H-4’-H-25’), 0.88 (t, <em>J</em>= 6.6 Hz, H-26’). The <sup>13</sup>C-NMR spectrum of <strong>7</strong> showed at δ<sub>C</sub> 64.5 (C-1’), 31.8 (C-24’), 29.5 (C-5’-C-22’), 29.3 (C-4’, C-23’), 29. 2 (C-2’), 25.6 (C-3’), 22.5 (C-25’), 13.9 (C-26’) confirmed the hexacosyl unit. On the basis of this evidence, the NMR spectra of <strong>7</strong> (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figures S22</a>−<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">S24</a>) provided enough information to identify hexacosyl-(<em>E</em>)-ferulate (<strong>7</strong>) (<a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">Delazar et al., 2003</a>).</p>
<p id="P29">In the present study, the isolated compounds from the bark of <em>A. glabra</em> align with previously reported chemical constituents of the <em>Aesculus</em> genus. The flavanone derivative epicatechin has previously been identified in <em>A. Californica, A. hippocastanum, and A. turbinata (</em><a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">Kubo and Bai-Ping, 1992</a>; <a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">Morimoto et al., 1987</a>; <a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">Ogawa et al., 2008</a>). Similarly, procyanidin A2 and other proanthocyanidins have been reported from the seeds of <em>A. hippocastanum</em> and <em>A. turbinata</em> (<a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">Morimoto et al., 1987</a>; <a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">Ogawa et al., 2008</a>). Epicatechin and procyanidin A2 are constituents also found in the bark of <em>A. hippocastanum</em> and have previously been explored for antioxidant effects and have shown efficacy in vascular disorders (<a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">Owczarek et al., 2021b</a>). Both classes of compounds have been of research interest with catechins being investigated as antioxidants for anticancer and cardiovascular-protective properties (<a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">Tang et al., 2019</a>). Previously reported properties of catechins and procyanidins confirm the need for further investigation of these compounds to verify anti-angiogenic properties of individual isolates and of the <em>A. glabra</em> species.</p></section><section id="S15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. LC-MS Analysis of the MeOH extract from the bark of <em>A. glabra</em>
</h3>
<p id="P30">To comprehensively explore the chemical diversity of <em>A. glabra</em>, a Featured-Based Molecular Network (FBMN) was constructed from the acquired LC-MS/MS data. In addition to using the MN library search (MSI level 2 annotations), the Network Annotation Propagation workflow (NAP) was used, which allows for improved annotation levels (MSI level 3 annotations) by combining spectral library search and <em>in silico</em> fragmentation matching. The results of the <em>in silico</em> structure prediction were integrated into the molecular network to retrieve the most abundant chemical classes.</p>
<p id="P31">The FBMN analysis yielded 643 nodes, of which 360 were connected in clusters of at least two nodes, and only 112 were putatively annotated by comparing the spectral data with the GNPS library. Mono-, di- and trisaccharide’s and other carbohydrates make up most of the putative annotations. After applying the <em>in silico</em> tool NAP to the dataset, different molecular families were identified and only the five larger chemical classes are shown in <a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>. These include coumarins (e.g., isoscopoletin: <em>m/z</em> 191.0342, [M-H]<sup>−</sup>), flavonoid glycosides (e.g., quercitrin: <em>m/z</em> 447.0933, [M-H]<sup>−</sup>), flavans (e.g., epicatechin: <em>m/z</em> 289.0718, [M-H]<sup>−</sup>), fatty acids and their conjugates, and a large group of carbohydrates and their conjugates, including some coumarin glucosides (e.g., aesculin: <em>m/z</em> 339.0722, [M-H]<sup>−</sup> and fraxin: <em>m/z</em> 369.0827, [M-H]<sup>−</sup>). Manual inspection of the MS<sup>2</sup> data and comparison with data available in the literature allowed the annotation of additional specialized metabolites characteristic of the genus <em>Aesculus</em> including aesculin rutinoside, isoscopolin (isoscopoletin glucoside), scopoletin rutinoside, fraxetin and procyanidin A2 (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>, <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">supplementary data</a>).</p>

Angiogenesis Assessment</h3>
<p id="P34">The zebrafish angiogenesis assessment was carried out to investigate the impact of the selected secondary metabolites in the development of the vascular system of the zebrafish model. With treatment of angiogenic inhibitors, zebrafish will experience changes such as tail bending, elimination of abnormal waste residues, abnormal elongation of the yolk sac, hemorrhage, and abnormal vasculature (<a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">Serbedzija et al., 1999</a>). The angiogenesis assessment determined characteristics that indicate a vessel mechanism involved with the treatment of epicatechin and procyanidin A2 (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>). Before Oil Red O vessel staining, images were taken of embryos at 72 hpf (after 24 h with treatment), yolk enlargement was seen with epicatechin and procyanidin A2, with the largest visual defect coming from procyanidin A2 treatment (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>). The epicatechin treatment showed small abnormalities in the pericardial and yolk region at all tested doses while procyanidin A2 showed significant abnormalities in those regions. The abnormalities observed are evident of a disruption in blood circulation, disrupting or inhibiting the process of angiogenesis in contrast to the untreated vehicle control (DMSO).</p>

Conclusion</h2>
<p id="P37">The present work explored the chemical components of <em>A. glabra,</em> an understudied species of the <em>Aesculus</em> genus. Our results showed that the chemical constituents are in accord with previous isolated compounds from the <em>Aesculus</em> genus, confirming the chemical constituents of this species. We explored the toxicity and preliminary anti-angiogenic studies in the zebrafish model and concluded that epicatechin and procyanidin A2 might be involved in a mechanism that interferes with microvascular growth, restoration, vascular aging, and endothelial cell senescence, which in turn may have a significant impact on normal vascular function. Compound 6 also presented moderate cytotoxic activity in HeLa and MDA-MB231 cancer cell lines. Other isolates identified through isolation methods and LC/MS analysis belong to diverse chemical classes including coumarins, flavonoid glycosides, flavans, and fatty acids. These chemical classes have been seen across the <em>Aesculus</em> genus and may play a role in the reported folk and traditional medicinal use of several species in the <em>Aesculus</em> genus. Our results confirm that the <em>Aesculus</em> genus is a species that could benefit from future studies to confirm chemotaxonomic and therapeutic potential. Our findings also highlight the need for future studies to understand the underlying mechanism involved in procyanidin A2 effect and its therapeutic potential.</p></section><section id="SM1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
