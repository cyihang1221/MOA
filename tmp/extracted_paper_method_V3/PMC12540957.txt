<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Background</h3>
<p id="Par1">Gliomas, especially IDH‑wildtype astrocytomas, remain lethal despite multimodal therapy. Collagen type XXII alpha-1 (COL22A1) exhibits oncogenic activity in other cancers but remains underexplored in brain tumors. We asked whether its expression, cellular origin, and spatial context add prognostic and translational value in diffuse glioma.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p id="Par2">We reprocessed TCGA/CGGA RNA‑seq to transcripts‑per‑million and re‑annotated tumours under the WHO 2021 taxonomy. Differential expression, Kaplan–Meier, and multivariable Cox models assessed survival associations; gene‑set enrichment profiled pathway context. Cell‑type sources were mapped by deconvolution and single‑cell datasets (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE131928" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE131928</a>, <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE89567" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE89567</a>), and spatial distribution was examined using Ivy GAP and 10 × Visium. Protein abundance was validated by quantitative immunohistochemistry in 75 surgical specimens, with orthogonal proteomic corroboration from the TCGA Proteomic Data Commons.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p id="Par3">COL22A1 mRNA was up‑regulated in glioma and increased with grade. High expression predicted shorter overall survival and remained an independent hazard factor after adjusting for age, grade, Karnofsky score, radiotherapy, and temozolomide. Transcriptome deconvolution and Single‑cell analyses localized COL22A1 predominantly to mesenchymal-like tumour subpopulations with negligible signal in lymphoid, reactive-glial, or myeloid compartments. Spatial transcriptomics showed marked enrichment in peri‑necrotic and microvascular proliferation niches. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) linked COL22A1‑high tumours to epithelial–mesenchymal transition, cell-cycle, and inflammatory (IL6/JAK-STAT3, TNF–NFκB) programmes, consistent with a proliferative and hypoxic phenotype. Immunohistochemistry confirmed higher protein abundance in tumours versus normal brain, a stepwise increase with grade, and a trend association with Ki‑67; mass‑spectrometry data corroborated increased protein abundance and adverse survival at high expression. A parsimonious nomogram combining COL22A1 with clinical covariates improved the prediction of 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h3>
<p id="Par4">Across bulk, single‑cell, and spatial layers, COL22A1 integrates molecular, cellular, and microenvironmental hallmarks of glioma aggressiveness. Its tumour‑centric expression, peri‑necrotic localisation, and independent prognostic value support COL22A1 as a robust biomarker and a tractable candidate for imaging or therapeutic strategies in treatment‑refractory disease.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Graphical Abstract</h3>

<p id="Par22">Primary tumors of the brain—especially gliomas—comprise a substantial fraction of central nervous system malignancies worldwide. Recent epidemiological surveys reveal that glioblastoma (GBM) is not only the most commonly diagnosed but also the deadliest form of diffuse glioma, and diffuse gliomas together represent roughly two‑thirds of all malignant primary brain tumors [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>]. Classification systems for glioma have advanced significantly over the past decades. In the 2007 World Health Organization (WHO) framework, grading of astrocytomas, oligodendrogliomas, and GBMs into II, III, or IV depended purely on histopathological criteria—cellular atypia, mitotic activity, microvascular proliferation, and necrosis [<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>]. A significant shift occurred in 2016 when molecular determinants were incorporated, most notably IDH1/2 mutations [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>] and 1p/19q codeletion [<a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>], thereby delineating IDH-mutant astrocytomas, 1p/19q-codeleted oligodendrogliomas, and IDH-wildtype astrocytomas, also known as GBMs [<a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>]. The 2021 WHO update tightened this molecular lens further, recognising TERT‑promoter mutation [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>], EGFR amplification [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>], combined chromosome 7 amplification/10 deletion [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>], and MGMT‑promoter methylation in IDH‑wild‑type astrocytoma [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>], and biallelic CDKN2A/B loss in IDH‑mutant astrocytoma [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>]. Such refinements greatly facilitate precision diagnostics and personalised treatment planning. Current standard therapy for high‑grade glioma entails maximal safe resection followed by radiotherapy plus temozolomide (TMZ) chemotherapy [<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>]. Although this regimen raises median GBM survival to about 14.6 months[<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>], relapse is frequent. MGMT‑promoter methylation modulates TMZ efficacy [<a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>], and quiescent stem‑like cell populations identified by Professor Luis F. Parada and colleagues can evade cytotoxic therapies, fuelling recurrence [<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>]. Extensive efforts over recent decades have evaluated inhibitors against RTKs, PI3K, and VEGF; however, most clinical trials have underperformed expectations [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>]. Hence, uncovering new actionable targets remains essential [<a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>, <a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>]. Bioinformatic portals—including GEPIA [<a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>], UCSC Xena [<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>], and cBioPortal [<a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>]—enable large-scale gene-expression interrogation; however, many still rely on older tumor labels or RPKM normalization, which introduces bias in biomarker discovery. To overcome these hurdles, we re-extracted raw RNA-seq data from TCGA and CGGA, converted the expression to transcripts-per-million (TPM), re-annotated the samples using the 2021 WHO criteria [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>], and applied multivariate prognostic modeling. From this pipeline and an extensive literature review, we prioritised COL22A1, which encodes collagen type XXII—a fibril‑associated collagen located at specialised tissue junctions and basement membranes [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. It contributes to the structural extracellular matrix (ECM) of connective tissues [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>]. In zebrafish models, COL22A1 is expressed by perivascular stromal cells and is required for vascular basement membrane stability—loss of col22a1 leads to leaky, dilated cranial vessels and intracranial hemorrhages [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. In this study, we reprocessed TCGA/CGGA RNA‑seq to TPM under the WHO 2021 taxonomy and integrating single‑cell and spatial transcriptomics with clinical tissue validation. This study (i) identifies COL22A1 as an independent prognostic marker in glioma, (ii) validated using immunohistochemistry in 75 clinical tissues to delineate COL22A1’s prognostic and therapeutic relevance in glioma, (iii) maps COL22A1 to mesenchymal‑like tumour cells with negligible immune/glial expression, and (iv) demonstrates peri‑necrotic and microvascular enrichment across Ivy GAP and 10 × Visium. We further present a clinically applicable nomogram that improves 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival prediction, thereby positioning COL22A1 as a biologically coherent biomarker with translational potential.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data collection for bioinformatic analyses</h3>
<p id="Par23">Five independent datasets were harnessed to evaluate COL22A1 expression and clinical impact.</p>
<ol class="list" style="list-style-type:decimal">
<li><p id="Par24">Primary TCGA Bioinformatic Cohort: Transcriptomic (mRNA) data and clinical covariates (sex, age, histology, grade, overall survival, vital status) for 690 gliomas were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (<a href="https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/</a>). Cases originally annotated under legacy criteria were re‑classified per the 2021 WHO guidelines [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>] into IDH‑wild‑type, IDH‑mutant astrocytoma, and oligodendroglioma clusters.</p></li>
<li><p id="Par25">Proteomic Data Commons (TCGA PDC): Protein-level corroboration utilized 110 mass-spectrometry profiles (100 GBM, 10 normal brain) plus clinical metadata (retrieved December 2024; <a href="https://proteomic.datacommons.cancer.gov/pdc/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://proteomic.datacommons.cancer.gov/pdc/</a>).</p></li>
<li><p id="Par26">TCGA and CGGA Immune/Heterogeneity Cohorts: To probe tumour heterogeneity and immune infiltration, bulk RNA‑seq from TCGA and the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (http:// <a href="http://www.cgga.org.cn/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.cgga.org.cn/</a>, accessed December 2024) were analysed.</p></li>
<li><p id="Par27">Ivy Glioblastoma Atlas Project: COL22A1 regional expression was examined in 270 samples across five anatomical niches (leading edge, infiltrating border, cellular tumor, microvascular proliferation, and pseudopalisading necrosis, defined either by histology or gene expression) from 10 patients (<a href="https://glioblastoma.alleninstitute.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://glioblastoma.alleninstitute.org/</a>).</p></li>
<li><p id="Par28">Single-Cell RNA Sequencing: Two GEO datasets were utilized: IDH-wildtype glioma (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE131928" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE131928</a>) and IDH-mutant glioma (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE89567" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE89567</a>), both accessed in December 2024.</p></li>
</ol></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Transcriptome, proteomic, and single‑cell processing</h3>
<p id="Par29">All analyses were carried out in R 4.1.0 (<a href="http://www.r-project.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.r-project.org</a>). Firstly, we converted FPKM (Reads Per Kilobase per Million) values to TPM (Transcripts Per Million) for precise sample comparison. Differential expression was determined via ggpubr and limma with FDR-adjusted p &lt; 0.05. Survival associations were assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves and multivariate Cox proportional-hazards models. For the single‑cell analysis, we analysed author‑curated, analysis‑ready objects in BioTuring BBrowser [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>] for IDH‑wildtype glioma (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE131928" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE131928</a>) and IDH‑mutant glioma (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE89567" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE89567</a>). Batch correction and normalisation follow the original studies’ pipelines embedded in these objects; we retained those settings unmodified and document dataset identifiers, version/build dates, and provenance in Supplementary Methods. Reproducibility steps: Download and open <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE131928" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE131928</a> and <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE89567" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE89567</a> through the BBrowser list → Analysis view → Gene expression overlay: “COL22A1” (ENSG00000173785) → Group by default setting: cell type (Tumour, myeloid, T, and reactive glia) → using default log‑normalised COL22A1 expression for Statistics calculation by group.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Gene‑set enrichment analysis (GSEA)</h3>
<p id="Par30">GSEA (<a href="https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp</a>, accessed December 2024) compared high-versus low‑COL22A1 tumours (median split) using hallmark gene sets (h.all.v2024.1.Hs. symbols.gmt). Other parameters remained at their default values [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>].</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Twelve‑cell‑state and CIBERSORT deconvolution</h3>
<p id="Par31">Two orthogonal strategies inferred cell-type composition. The twelve‑cell‑state framework of Verhaak et al. [<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>] identifies 12 principal cell states in bulk tumours, whereas CIBERSORT (<a href="https://cibersort.stanford.edu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://cibersort.stanford.edu/</a>) estimates the relative abundance of 22 immune subsets [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>]. Transcriptomic profiles from the TCGA, CGGA, and Ivy cohorts were input, retaining samples with a deconvolution p-value &lt; 0.05. Spearman correlations were calculated between COL22A1 expression and each cell population.</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Tissue microarray and immunohistochemistry</h3>
<p id="Par32">A human glioma microarray (GL1001a; Biomax, <a href="https://www.biomax.us/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.biomax.us/</a>) containing 68 glioma specimens (7 Grade 1, 43 Grade 2, 9 Grade 3, 9 Grade 4) plus 7 normal‑brain controls (core diameter 1.5 mm; thickness 5 µm) was examined. All patient material was obtained with informed Consent. Sections were processed on a Ventana Benchmark® XT automated stainer. Following de-paraffinization, antigen retrieval was performed in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.2) under pressure (125°C, 30 min), followed by three 5-min PBS washes. Slides were loaded onto the autostainer according to the vendor's protocol. Primary antibodies: COL22A1 (Abcam #ab121846), Ki‑67 (Abcam #ab15580), and p53 (Cell Signaling #2524). Detection utilised the Roche OptiView DAB IHC kit. Specificity was verified with matched positive and negative controls.</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunohistochemical scoring</h3>
<p id="Par33">FFPE TMA cores were stained on a Ventana Benchmark XT with DAB chromogen and digitised at 10 × . Using Fiji/ImageJ (<a href="https://imagej.net/software/fiji/downloads" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://imagej.net/software/fiji/downloads</a>), custom macros were used to quantify the total tissue area and cells exhibiting a specific chromogen signal [<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>–<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>]. Firstly, images (TIFF) were processed in Fiji/ImageJ using Colour Deconvolution (vectors = H DAB) to isolate the DAB channel (Colour_2; Supplementary_3). The DAB image was converted to 8‑bit (white background = 255; black foreground = 0). Positivity was defined on the grayscale histogram as 0–120, then partitioned into fixed intensity bins applied uniformly across all slides: 0–40 (3 + , strong), 41–80 (2 + , moderate), 81–120 (1 + , weak). For each bin, binary masks were generated (Analyze Particles, size ≥ 5 px) to quantify the stained area. A whole‑tissue mask (0–240) provided the denominator. Batch processing and mask export used the macro “3 level DAB” (Supplementary_4), which outputs a per‑core Summary.csv containing areas for 3 + , 2 + , 1 + , and whole tissue. We calculated an Immunoscore equivalent Immunoscore as: Immunoscore = (1 × % of weak area) + (2 × % of moderate area) + (3 × % of strong area), yielding a 0–300 range. The resulting Immunoscores were compared with clinical variables via one‑way ANOVA.</p></section><section id="Sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Spatial transcriptomics</h3>
<p id="Par34">A publicly available 10 × Genomics Visium section from an adult GBM (<a href="https://www.10xgenomics.com/datasets/human-glioblastoma-whole-transcriptome-analysis-1-standard-1-2-0" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.10xgenomics.com/datasets/human-glioblastoma-whole-transcriptome-analysis-1-standard-1-2-0</a>) comprising 3468 capture spots was interrogated. Log2‑scaled COL22A1 expression was visualised across the necrotic core, peri‑necrotic rim, cellular tumour, infiltrative border, and leading edge.</p></section><section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis—survival modelling, multiplicity, and cut‑off policy</h3>
<p id="Par35">Overall survival was analysed using Cox proportional‑hazards models in R (v4.1.0; survival package). The pre‑specified multivariable model included COL22A1 as a continuous, z‑scored predictor together with age, sex, WHO grade, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), radiotherapy (RT), and temozolomide (TMZ): Surv(time, status) ~ COL22A1_z + age + sex + WHO_grade + KPS + RT + TMZ. Continuous covariates were centred and scaled. Potential collinearity was examined using variance‑inflation diagnostics and correlation matrices; diagnostics did not indicate concerning collinearity, so no variables were removed. The proportional‑hazards assumption was evaluated with Schoenfeld residuals (with visual inspection of log–log plots); no material violations were detected. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals and two‑sided P values are reported. For discovery-stage analyses (collagen-gene family screens and MSigDB Hallmark gene-set enrichment), P values were adjusted using Benjamini–Hochberg FDR (q &lt; 0.05). The COL22A1 term in the multivariable Cox model constituted a primary, pre‑specified hypothesis and was not subjected to additional multiplicity correction. To avoid threshold‑selection bias, inferential models treated COL22A1 continuously; Kaplan–Meier plots using a median split are provided solely for visualisation, with alternative quantile displays yielding concordant patterns.</p></section></section><section id="Sec11"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="Sec12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Result 1: COL22A1 up‑regulation identified in glioblastoma and lower‑grade glioma</h3>
<p id="Par36">Analysis of pan‑cancer RNA‑seq data revealed a consistent up‑regulation of COL22A1 transcripts in malignant tissues relative to their non‑neoplastic counterparts. Across 9,162 primary tumors and 731 normal from The Cancer Genome Atlas (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>A), COL22A1 abundance was significantly elevated in 15 of 34 evaluable cancer types (Wilcoxon rank‑sum test, p &lt; 0.05), including glioma (GBM and LGG), while only one cancer type, Uterine Corpus Endometrial Carcinoma (UCEC) showed significant reverse correlation (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>B). Furthermore, in this dataset, the more advanced cancer (metastatic melanoma, annotated as SKCM-Metastasis) exhibited higher COL22A1 expression than the primary tumor (annotated as SKCM-Primary, Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>B). These data indicate that COL22A1 upregulation is a pervasive event in oncogenesis across various tissue contexts, as well as at higher stages of the disease, suggesting a conserved pro-tumorigenic function and underscoring its potential value as a biomarker or therapeutic target in human cancers, including glioma.</p>

<p id="Par42">Gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed on the 50 cancer hallmark collections after median stratification of COL22A1 expression within each TCGA glioma subtype. In IDH‑wild‑type astrocytoma, high COL22A1 was associated with the enrichment of 14 gene-sets encompassing cell‑cycle progression (E2F_TARGETS, G2M_CHECKPOINT, MITOTIC_SPINDLE), metabolic stress (HYPOXIA, OXIDATIVE_PHOSPHORYLATION, BILE_ACID_METABOLISM, PEROXISOME), and aggressive stromal signaling (notably EPITHELIAL_MESENCHYMAL_TRANSITION, IL6_JAK_STAT3_SIGNALING, and TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB), as demonstrated in the (Fig. <a href="#Fig7" class="usa-link">7</a>A–C). Collectively, the data indicate that heightened COL22A1 expression reconfigures glioma transcriptional wiring toward EMT, checkpoint activation, and inflammatory signaling; these alterations are most extensive in IDH-wildtype tumors, partially conserved in IDH-mutant astrocytoma, and largely absent in Oligodendroglioma, aligning with the gene’s subtype-specific prognostic impact.</p>

For the representative patient plotted (KPS = 80, age = 60, grade IV, chemotherapy-exposed, radiotherapy-treated, COL22A1‑high), the model assigns 421 points, translating into 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities of 52.1%, 2.0%, and 0.09%, respectively.</p>

<section id="Sec25"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Integrated data and WHO 2021 reclassification with multi‑omic datasets and clinical validation</h3>
<p id="Par48">By integrating multi-cohort transcriptomic data with the WHO 2021 glioma taxonomy [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>], we analyzed molecularly defined entities—IDH-wildtype astrocytoma, IDH-mutant astrocytoma, and Oligodendroglioma—independently. Unlike earlier studies that relied on histology or pre-2021 criteria [<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>, <a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>], this design enabled a finer resolution of COL22A1 biology. We confirmed that COL22A1 expression is markedly higher in IDH‑wildtype diffuse astrocytoma than in IDH‑mutant tumors, corroborating reports that link COL22A1 to IDH status and glioblastoma (IDH‑wildtype) aggressiveness [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>]. Crucially, COL22A1 remained a strong predictor of poor outcome after stratification by the 2021 WHO grade, indicating that its prognostic power is not an artifact of legacy grading systems. Consistent associations across public datasets and our cohort reinforce COL22A1 as a robust biomarker for aggressive diffuse glioma, echoing its identification as a hub risk gene in radiogenomic analyses [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>].</p></section><section id="Sec26"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">COL22A1 links with ECM organization and mesenchymal transition signaling</h3>
<p id="Par49">COL22A1 encodes the fibril‑associated collagen XXII, enriched at specialized basement membranes [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. Through GLQGER and GFKGER motifs, it binds to integrin α2β1, anchoring cells to the extracellular matrix and activating pro-migratory signaling [<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>]. Although gliomas lack classical epithelium, they adopt mesenchymal‑like states during progression. Our GSEA confirmed enrichment of EMT signatures in COL22A1‑high tumors (Fig. <a href="#Fig7" class="usa-link">7</a>). In primary IDH‑wildtype glioblastoma, nuclear p‑STAT3(Y705) is frequent and associates with shorter survival, supporting our IL6/JAK–STAT3 signal [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>]. NF‑κB/RelB activity marks mesenchymal GBM and correlates with adverse prognosis, aligning with the TNF–NFκB axis highlighted by GSEA [<a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>]. EMT master regulators (for example, SNAI1/ZEB1) promote invasion and aggressive behaviour in human GBM, consistent with the EMT programme in COL22A1‑high tumours [<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a>]. Consistent with our EMT/adhesion findings, isocucurbitacin B suppresses glioma via a CAV1‑mediated anoikis program, highlighting therapeutically leveragable adhesion‑dependent survival pathways [<a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, COL22A1 overexpression (partly via promoter hypomethylation) drives invasion and EMT marker shifts; silencing reverses these effects [<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>]. Analogously, COL22A1 knockdown in glioma cells diminishes migration and invasion [<a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>]. Thus, COL22A1 functions within a mesenchymal transcriptional network that fosters tumor invasiveness. A COL22A1‑centered nomogram showed potential to improve risk stratification in precision neuro‑oncology.</p></section><section id="Sec27"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">COL22A1 links with fibroblast features around perinecrotic and driver of vascular remodeling</h3>
<p id="Par50">Angiogenesis is a defining feature of glioblastoma, and COL22A1 appears integral to this process. GBM‑derived extracellular matrix induces COL22A1 in brain endothelial cells and correlates with poorer survival [<a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a>]. Zebrafish studies show that loss of COL22A1 compromises vessel integrity, while a human E736D mutation fails to rescue the phenotype [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>], implying a vessel-stabilizing role that tumors may exploit during aberrant neovascularization. In spatial transcriptomic studies of GBM, COL22A1 is indeed elevated in perinecrotic (hypoxic) tumor regions—known hotspots for neoangiogenesis and immunosuppression [<a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>]. Consistent with this, recent studies identified COL22A1 as an angiogenesis-associated gene in high-grade gliomas [<a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>]. High COL22A1 expression correlates with enriched endothelial/stromal signatures and upregulation of immune checkpoints (e.g. PD-1, PD-L1) [<a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>], implying roles in promoting vascularization and immune evasion. Functionally, COL22A1 silencing in glioma cells impaired their proliferation and invasion [<a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>]. Moreover, collagenous ECM accumulates around necrotic and perivascular niches in GBM [<a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a>], which can hinder immune cell infiltration (via receptors like LAIR1) [<a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>], further supporting a link between COL22A1-rich regions, angiogenesis, and immune escape. Recent MRI radiogenomic modeling captures GBM transcriptional heterogeneity, supporting the feasibility of imaging surrogates to detect COL22A1‑defined mesenchymal, peri‑necrotic states [<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a>].</p></section><section id="Sec28"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immune micro‑environment: tumor‑centric COL22A1 with context‑dependent immune correlates</h3>
<p id="Par51">Bulk deconvolution revealed modest positive correlations between COL22A1 and fibroblast‑like signatures in IDH‑wildtype tumors (TCGA r = 0.222; CGGA r = 0.160), whereas associations in IDH‑mutant groups were weak (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>). Fine‑grained CIBERSORT suggested variable links to activated T‑cell subsets (Table <a href="#Tab2" class="usa-link">2</a>), but these did not replicate across cohorts, likely reflecting statistical noise in necrosis‑rich GBM tissue. To delineate cell‑of‑origin, we leveraged large single‑cell cohorts: IDH‑wildtype GBM (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE131928" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE131928</a>, 28 patients) and IDH‑mutant glioma (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE89567" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE89567</a>, 10 tumours); across both, COL22A1 localised to malignant/mesenchymal‑like cells with negligible signal in lymphoid/myeloid populations, indicating tumour‑cell derivation [<a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a>]. At the tissue scale, collagen‑rich perivascular/peri‑necrotic niches—prominent in recurrent GBM—are linked to local immune suppression, consistent with our spatial maps [<a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a>]. Stromal pericyte‑like cells contribute substantially to fibrillar collagen/fibronectin deposition in GBM, providing a plausible source for the COL22A1‑enriched core [<a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a>]. Mechanistically, collagen engages the inhibitory receptor LAIR1 on glioma‑infiltrating immune cells and is enriched precisely where these cells accumulate, supporting an indirect, matrix‑mediated immunosuppression model rather than direct COL22A1 expression by immune lineages [<a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>]. Discrepancies with Yan et al. [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>]—who reported stronger “immune‑hot” associations—may stem from their radiogenomic model capturing spatial co‑localization of immune infiltrates with COL22A1‑rich, angiogenic cores rather than true expression within immune cells. Overall, COL22A1 is tumor‑centric; its immune impact is indirect, mediated through ECM remodeling that shapes immune‑cell distribution. Spatially resolved studies are warranted to dissect this interplay. Moreover, activation of the isoliquiritigenin–circ0030018/miR‑1236–HER2 pathway promotes glioma progression, concordant with the cytokine‑EMT circuits enriched in COL22A1‑high tumors [<a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">39</a>]. Clinically, COL22A1 is best viewed as a prognostic, risk‑stratifying marker that coherently captures a mesenchymal, peri‑necrotic niche rather than as a proven modifier of treatment response. In practice, COL22A1‑high status could inform follow‑up intensity, eligibility or stratification in trials testing ECM/integrin‑pathway or anti‑angiogenic strategies, and imaging workflows aimed at ECM‑rich tumour cores. Formal evaluation of predictive utility—i.e., whether COL22A1 alters benefit from RT/TMZ—will require prospective designs with predefined interaction testing.</p></section><section id="Sec29"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Translational outlook</h3>
<p id="Par52">Current data support COL22A1 as a prognostic, tumour‑centric marker that localises to peri‑necrotic, mesenchymal niches. In practice, COL22A1‑high status could (i) guide trial enrichment/stratification for agents that modulate ECM/integrin/FAK or angiogenic signalling; (ii) inform combination hypotheses (e.g., pairing ECM‑directed approaches with standard RT/TMZ or anti‑angiogenic backbones) to be tested prospectively; and (iii) enable imaging‑based phenotyping of ECM‑rich cores as a companion readout. Translation should proceed in staged steps: analytical validity of an IHC or RNA assay with a pre‑specified threshold; retrospective interaction testing in treated cohorts; and, ultimately, prospective biomarker‑stratified or enrichment designs with predefined treatment‑by‑biomarker analyses. Until such studies are completed, we present COL22A1 as prognostic and trial‑enabling, rather than as a proven modifier of treatment sensitivity.</p></section></section><section id="Sec30"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Limitations</h2>
<p id="Par53">This work was not powered or specifically designed to estimate treatment‑by‑biomarker interactions; accordingly, we refrain from inferring differential sensitivity to standard therapy and present COL22A1 as prognostic.</p></section><section id="Sec31"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="Par54">In summary, our discussion integrates new and prior evidence to illuminate the multifaceted role of COL22A1 in glioma. COL22A1 consistently emerges as a marker of high-grade, IDH-wildtype gliomas and poor patient outcomes, corroborated by radiogenomic, bioinformatic, and experimental studies [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>]. We extend these findings by leveraging the 2021 WHO reclassification, single-cell and spatial technologies, and direct tissue validation to demonstrate that COL22A1 is produced by mesenchymal-like tumor cells concentrated in peri-necrotic, hypoxic niches, where it may support angiogenesis and immune escape. This holistic understanding suggests that COL22A1 underpins a pro-tumor matrix milieu and could be explored as a therapeutic target or imaging biomarker of the aggressive “core” compartment of glioblastoma. Taken together, these data position COL22A1 as a biologically coherent, clinically usable prognostic biomarker and a candidate for trial enrichment or imaging applications, with its predictive role for standard therapy to be clarified in future studies.</p></section><section id="Sec32"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h2>
