</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ABS1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="S1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction:</h3>
<p id="P1">Dereplication, an approach to sidestep the efforts involved in the isolation of known compounds, is generally accepted as being the first stage of novel discoveries in natural product research. It is based on metabolite profiling analysis of complex natural extracts.</p></section><section id="S2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objective:</h3>
<p id="P2">To present the application of LipidXplorer for automatic targeted dereplication of phenolics in plant crude extracts based on direct infusion high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry data.</p></section><section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Material and Methods:</h3>
<p id="P3">LipidXplorer uses a user-defined molecular fragmentation query language (MFQL) to search for specific characteristic fragmentation patterns in large data sets and highlight the corresponding metabolites. To this end, MFQL files were written to dereplicate common phenolics occurring in plant extracts. Complementary MFQL files were used for validation purposes.</p></section><section id="S4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results:</h3>
<p id="P4">New MFQL files with molecular formula restrictions for common classes of phenolic natural products were generated for the metabolite profiling of different representative crude plant extracts. This method was evaluated against an open-source software for mass-spectrometry data processing (MZMine®) and against manual annotation based on published data.</p></section><section id="S5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion:</h3>
<p id="P5">The targeted LipidXplorer method implemented using common phenolic fragmentation patterns, was found to be able to annotate more phenolics than MZMine® that is based on automated queries on the available databases. Additionally, screening for ascarosides, natural products with unrelated structures to plant phenolics collected from the nematode <em>Caenorhabditis elegans</em>, demonstrated the specificity of this method by cross-testing both groups of chemicals in both plants and nematodes.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> dereplication, DI-ESI-MS, LipidXplorer, phenolic natural products</p></section></section><section id="S6"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1 |. INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p id="P6">Direct infusion electrospray ionisation mass spectrometry (DI-ESI-MS) based dereplication allows a rapid and qualitative visualisation of the sample constituents in complex mixture in only a few minutes. But the sheer volume of data produced by such state of the art technology creates difficulties for the manual or automated annotation of groups of compounds of interest. To facilitate annotation, software and computational toolboxes have been developed and aid the interpretation of such convoluted data. MS2Analyser,<sup><a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">1</a></sup> MzMine,<sup><a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">2</a></sup> and XCMS,<sup><a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a></sup> LipidBlast,<sup><a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a></sup> LipidProfiler<sup><a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a></sup> are some popular examples. However, such approaches all rely on a well-defined library<sup><a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a>–<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a></sup> and the natural product chemistry community still lacks a comprehensive open access database. Progress has recently been made to implement <em>in silico</em> tandem mass spectrometry (MS2) libraries to improve the process.<sup><a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">9</a>–<a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a></sup> Such approaches, however, are limited to classical dereplication only and cannot annotate unknown compounds or even classify them since those would not be part of any experimental database.</p>
<p id="P7">LipidXplorer is an open source Python language software kit to analyse MS-based lipidomic acquired data.<sup><a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a></sup> This software is based on the declarative molecular fragmentation query language (MFQL), which allows users to input search parameters for their specific goals. Originally it was designed to support shotgun lipidomics profiling of multiple samples through the organisation of the acquired data into a flat-file database (the Masterscan file; *.sc), followed by filtering with selected user-defined MFQL files (*.mfql).<sup><a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a></sup> The output file (MFQL files) includes the identification of the precursors, their fragments and abundance for subsequent quantification. The most important feature of LipidXplorer as an exploratory tool is the flexibility of programming of the MFQL files for either a given metabolite or a class of compounds sharing a similar fragmentation pattern. It is possible to submit a batch of samples to LipidXplorer to screen all samples and even obtain quantitative results depending on the experimental design.<sup><a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a></sup></p>
<p id="P8">In this paper, a general method is described where LipidXplorer fragmentation pattern queries were designated for automatic dereplication of phenolics in medicinal plants using electrospray high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry data. Phenolic compounds were chosen since they are widely distributed in plants and other organisms. Their importance is closely related to their antioxidant properties. Many well-known biological activities such as antimicrobial, antitumor, and cardiovascular benefits are associated with the regular ingestion of phenolics.<sup><a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">12</a>–<a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a></sup></p>
<p id="P9">MFQL files with molecular structure restrictions were created for the main classes of phenolics and used for dereplication. The method was tested on well-studied plants extracts. In addition, the same strategy was applied to screen unrelated extracts from nematodes, which are expected to have fewer phenolic compounds, to test the specificity of the suggested method. MFQL files for ascarosides,<sup><a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">15</a>,<a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">16</a></sup> a major family of pheromones in nematodes that are not found in plants, were also created.</p></section><section id="S7"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2 |. <p id="P11">Dried leaf material (30 mg) of each sample was ground and extracted with 80% methanol (1.5 mL) at room temperature using sonication. After three cycles of 8 min each, the extraction mixtures were centrifuged for 10 min at 10000×<em>g</em> at 4°C. The supernatants were filtered and concentrated under vacuum. This procedure was repeated twice for each sample for maximum recovery, and the final solution was concentrated to dryness. The original sample of hesperidin (C<sub>28</sub>H<sub>34</sub>O<sub>15</sub>) was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, MO, USA) and the solution was prepared at 80% methanol (1 mg/mL) at room temperature using sonication. After three cycles of 8 min each, the extraction mixtures were centrifuged for 10 min at 10000×<em>g</em> at 4°C, and the supernatants were kept for analysis after resuspension in 50% methanol (1 mL).</p>
<p id="P12">The <em>Caenorhabditis elegans</em> samples were produced as described elsewhere.<sup><a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">17</a>,<a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">18</a></sup> A single L1 animal was placed in a 10 cm nematode growth media (NGM)<sup><a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">17</a></sup> seeded plate and left for 9 days to grow and reproduce. Worms were then washed off the plate using M9 Buffer<sup><a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">17</a></sup> and transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube. To remove bacteria left over in the plate, worms were centrifuged for 2 min at 2000 rpm and washed three times using the buffer solution. Then, 15000 young larvae were suspended in 2 mL of S-complete,<sup><a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">17</a></sup> placed in a 50 mL Erlenmeyer flask and fed 250 μL of <em>Escherichia coli</em>. The cultures were then allowed to shake at 22 °C until they reached adult stage (~55 h). To achieve a synchronous population of worms, eggs were collected by treating adult worms with a bleach solution. Eggs were then hatched at 22 °C in the buffer. After 24 h, L1 larvae were washed and counted. An aliquot containing 30000 worms was placed in a 50 mL Erlenmeyer flask with S-complete (4 mL),<sup><a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">17</a></sup> fed 400 μL of <em>Escherichia coli</em>, and allowed to shake at 22°C until they reached young adult stage (~42 h). Once the worms reached young adult stage, they were pelleted by centrifugation. The supernatant was collected, centrifuged, and immediately frozen at −80 °C. A sucrose gradient was used to separate the worms from the residual bacteria in the culture before collection of the worm samples. All samples were washed and immediately frozen at −80 °C. The supernatant was then centrifuged at high speed, lyophilised, and resuspended in 80% methanol (1 mL).<sup><a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">18</a></sup> The worm bodies were homogenised in a FastPrep-24 MP using 80% methanol and a lysing matrix and centrifuged. The supernatant was collected and then dried using a speedvac. Residues were resuspended in 50% methanol (1 mL) for analysis.<sup><a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">18</a></sup></p></section><section id="S10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3 |. Electrospray mass spectrometry analysis</h3>
<p id="P13">Full-scan ESI-MS analysis were performed using an Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer (Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA), at a resolution of 400000 at <em>m/z</em> 200, equipped with a nano-electrospray interface EASY-Spray™ (ThermoFischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples were infused directly into the source at a flow rate of 0.5 μL/min. The ion transfer tube temperature was set at 325°C. Data were acquired in negative mode in the range <em>m/z</em> 150–1500 and the capillary voltage was set to 1.5 kV. Since phenolics are acidic compounds, they are more sensitive to negative mode ionisation. MS2 data were acquired using collision induced dissociation (CID) as activation type at 35% of collision energy (value optimised manually) at a resolution of 120000 at <em>m/z</em> 200. Untargeted mass isolation occurred in the Quad-rupole section and subsequent fragments detection were done in the Orbitrap section (level 2; MS<sup><a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">2</a></sup>). Analysis time was 2 min (per sample), controlled using Xcalibur software (ThermoFischer Scientific). Data were acquired as automatic data dependent acquisition mode top scan using the 30 most intense precursors as priority; peaks selected three times were added to an exclusion list for 30 s. Each spectrum was exported as *.mzML files in order to be read by LipidXplorer (and as *.mzXML for the Molecular Network constructions) using the software msConvert (ProteoWizard; available at <a href="http://proteowizard.sourceforge.net/tools.shtml" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">proteowizard.sourceforge.net/tools.shtml</a>).</p></section><section id="S11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4 |. LipidXplorer parameters</h3>
<p id="P14">LipidXplorer 1.2.5 (32 bits) was used in this study. Data were imported as *.mzML files. Parameters for import were as follow: Selection window: 0.5 Da; Time range: 0–120 s; <em>m/z</em> range: 150–1500; Resolution: 560000 (140000 for MS2) at <em>m/z</em> 200; Tolerance: 10 ppm (20 ppm for MS2); Threshold: 100 (200 for MS2); Resolution gradient: −865 (−280 for MS2). Isotopic correction MS1 and MS2 was switched on for the identification process. The threshold parameter was adjusted to get the main signals as 1 × 10<sup>3</sup>, directly from the acquired MS1 spectra.</p>
<p id="P15">MFQL files were written based on the structural formula for each group of compounds and their hydroxylated, methoxylated and glycosylated derivatives as described in the <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supporting Information</a>. Briefly, for each case, a skeleton with various degrees of O-substitution with hydroxy and methoxy groups, hexoses, pentoses and deoxyhexoses, from mono to tetrasaccharides, phenyl and phenylpropanoyl groups were considered. The parameters were chosen following recommendations from Herzog <em>et al</em>.<sup><a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a>–<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a></sup> MFQL files are available as <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supporting Information online</a>.</p></section><section id="S12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5 |. MFQL descriptions</h3>
<p id="P16">All MFQL files are available at the LipidXplorer wiki site at <a href="https://wiki.mpi-cbg.de/wiki/lipidx/index.php/Main_Page" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://wiki.mpi-cbg.de/wiki/lipidx/index.php/Main_Page</a>. Note that LipidXplorer can deliver quantitative results if the appropriate parameters and sample requirements are applied. Reliable quantification results require a validated internal standard, but such standards are difficult to obtain for natural products and therefore no quantification was done in this study. Details about creating the MFQL files are described in the <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supporting Information</a>.</p></section><section id="S13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6 |. Then all the filters (phenolics and ascarosides) to all plant and nematode samples were applied.</p>

Test for efficiency of the restrictions</h3>
<p id="P20">To test the efficiency of the restrictions proposed Compass MolWeight to Formula (Bruker Daltonik, Bremen, Germany) software was to determine the possible molecular formulae within 10 ppm of tolerance for some selected exact mass. The possible molecular formula for each <em>m/z</em> value was calculated with no restriction and added the number of carbon, oxygen and nitrogen atoms stepwise; those restrictions were extracted from the *.mfql files (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supporting Information</a>).</p></section><section id="S15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.8 |. Script for automation of MFQL creation and visualisation of the results</h3>
<p id="P21">To facilitate the writing of MFQL files an automated perl script called parse_cdxml.pl (available at <a href="https://github.com/rhilt13/MFQLAutomation" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://github.com/rhilt13/MFQLAutomation</a>) that can create these MFQL files from a more common type CDXML file from ChemOffice (Cambridgesoft) was designed; such a script creates only a MS1 type of query. This script allows the users to submit the parameters through a command line and also provide a *.cdxml file containing the molecules they are interested in. The script gets the information from the CDXML file inputs and formats it into a MFQL format. The result is a file ready to be used in LipidXplorer. With this script, automated generation of MFQL files could be performed more efficiently.</p>
<p id="P22">Once LipidXplorer is used, it produces a csv file that includes all the results. There are columns for masses, error, name of the precursors and fragments along with other information. A second perl script was designed, called get_struct.pl (available at <a href="https://github.com/rhilt13/MFQLAutomation" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://github.com/rhilt13/MFQLAutomation</a>) to parse those files, to pull structures for the previously listed precursors and fragments from CDXML files, perform calculations of the number of methoxy groups, hydroxy groups and double bonds that are present in each of the precursors and fragments, and compile all of this information into a textbox along with the structure into a.cdxml file. Automated extraction of the structure and related information improves analysis efficiency.</p></section></section><section id="S16"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3 |. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION</h2>
<p id="P23">Based on literature data and experimental fragmentation patterns of phenolics, MFQL files were written for the annotation of shikimic acid (C7), quinic acid (C7), benzoic acid (PhC6C1) and phenylpropanoid acid derivatives (PhC6C3), caffeoyl shikimic and dicaffeoyl shikimic acid derivatives (CSC6C3C7), caffeoyl quinic and dicaffeoyl quinic acid derivatives (CQC6C3C7), flavonoids (FlC6C3C6), and catechin derivatives (CatechC6C3C6). For example, PhC6C3 are known to exhibit neutral losses of 44 for CO<sub>2</sub> from the side chain, and generally glycosylated derivatives exhibit neutral losses of 162, 146 and 132 for hexose, deoxyhexose and pentose, respectively.</p>
<p id="P24">All the molecular filtering tools were tested to survey a series of data dependent analysis (DDA) MS2 on selected samples of medicinal plants with well characterised composition. The same filters were applied to extracts from the nematode <em>Caenorhabditis elegans</em> to assess the specificity of the method. Detailed information on the generation the MFQL files are found in the <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supporting Information</a> and the terminology used is based on previously published LipidXplorer papers.<sup><a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a>–<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a>,<a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a></sup></p>
<p id="P25">All extracts and standards were analysed by DI-ESI-MS in negative ion mode under DDA mode to select the 30 most intense peaks for further fragmentation during 2 min of direct infusion. The acquired data yielded more than 350 MS2 spectra associated with up to 8400 features for a single extract.</p>
<p id="P26">The approach used in this study followed two steps. As a first step (step 1; <a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>) the MS2 data were converted from proprietary into mzML format with MSConvert (Proteowizard) and then imported into LipidXplorer using the parameters described earlier. Those parameters were chosen based on the experimental data acquired. Secondly (step 2 <a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>), MFQL files were firstly edited manually and written following expected molecular formula ranges and MS fragmentation patterns for each class and subclass of compounds. Subclasses were intended to be subdivisions of each class designed not to make the molecular formula range excessively broad, which would produce an incongruent number of false positive results. The details of step 2 are presented in the <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supporting Information</a>. Briefly, the MFQL file is separated into four sections: (1) Define; (2) Identify; (3) SuchThat; and (4) Report. In the Define section (line 6, <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>), a description of the expected molecular range, double bond ratio (DBR) and the mode of acquisition are made explicit for a given label that would represent the class/subclass of compound to be detected. In the Identify section (line 10, <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>), it is indicated where each defined label is to be found; MS1 for the survey spectra and MS2 for the fragmentograms. The SuchThat section (line 16, <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>) is a filter for false positive occurrences of the defined molecular ranges to be found; for example, for a flavonoid aglycone the number of additional carbon atoms should not the greater than the number of oxygen atoms since O-methylation only was considered and the representation of such a restriction is as follows: ‘pr2GlyFlC6C3C6OMe.chemsc [O] – 12 +2 &gt;= pr2GlyFlC6C3C6OMe.chemsc [C] – 26’. The Report section (line 25, <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>) is where the output parameters are described; here one can print as much information as needed to aid in structure elucidation.</p>

It is impossible to identify the exact position of the hydroxy and the methoxy substituents and the nature of the glycosides on the aglycone using the data acquired by a simple direct infusion MS1 scan.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/T8/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section><p id="P33">To evaluate these results, this method was compared with the results obtained from MZmine, which is based on formal database queries, using the sample prepared from leaves of <em>Coffea arabica</em>. HMDB, Kegg compound database, LipidMaps database and PlantCyc database were used in online database searches and 10 ppm as “M/Z tolerance”. MZMine identified 69 compounds, from which 35 were nitrogen, phosphorus, chloride, fluoride and bromide free, 26 were phenolic derivatives and five were related to the classes for which this method was targeted (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>). These last five compounds were: 3,4,5-trimethoxycinnamic acid, epicatechin, 3-<em>O</em>-<em>p</em>-coumaroylquinic acid, 3-<em>O</em>-feruloylquinic acid, 2-<em>O</em>-galloylsucrose and 1,3-dicaffeoylquinic acid; in addition to quinic acid. These five compounds were successfully detected by the LipidXplorer approach, together with more than 100 other compounds at a threshold of 0.1%.</p>

Therefore, every compound identified by MZMine was detected. Thus, the method has the capability to match every identification done by the current software because it can be extended to cover all databases.</p>
<p id="P35">For the analysis of the coffee sample, the main phenolic constituents cited in the literature were identified:<sup><a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a>–<a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a></sup> caffeoylquinic acid, feruloylquinic acid and dicaffeoylquinic acid derivatives (<a href="#T7" class="usa-link">Table 7</a>). Phenol-Explorer (database on polyphenol content in foods; <a href="http://phenol-explorer.eu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://phenol-explorer.eu</a>) reports 16 phenolic compounds for coffee all related to caffeoylquinic acid derivatives. Additionally, the occurrence of shikimic and quinic acid, benzoic acid and glucosyl benzoic acid, caffeic acid and other shikimic acid derivatives were detected. Apart from some flavonoids detected, the presence of catechins (catechin, a catechin hexoside and a catechin pentoside) are highlighted.</p>
<p id="P36">Not to depend strictly on an experimental database has obvious advantages. To be able to classify unknown compounds represents a step forward in the discovery of new products. The output generated by LipidXplorer and our MFQL files could identify over 100 features and provide a self-designed label related to each structure for further identification (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supporting Information</a>). For all samples studied here, LipidXplorer identified a broader range of compounds, due to its capability of survey for a range of substituent groups within a class.</p>
<p id="P37">Comparisons of literature data and MFQL queries for a commercial sample of green tea (<em>Camellia sinensis</em>) is provided<sup><a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">12</a></sup> (<a href="#T8" class="usa-link">Table 8</a>). Some of the flavonoid glycosides were not detected, but caffeoylquinic acid and catechin derivatives were found in the following forms: caffeoylquinic acid, epigallocatechin (“prCatechC6C3C6 [0:0:7]”), epicatechin (“prCatechC6C3C6 [0:0:6]”), gallocatechin (“prCatechC6C3C6 [0:0:7]”), catechin (“prCatechC6C3C6 [0:0:6]”), epigallocatechin gallate (“prCatechGalateC6C3C6 [0:5:8]”) and (epi-)cate-chin gallate (“prCatechGalateC6C3C6 [0:5:7]”). The absence of detectable signals of flavonoids might be due to seasoning, place of growth and/or ion suppression among other factors. Nonetheless, our approach enabled the detection of 39 compounds based on the phenolic’s targeted search with the MFQL queries.</p>
<p id="P38">In order to examine the specificity of our approach, a second set of MFQL files was developed for the unrelated ascarosides, a chemical group abundant in nematodes but not reported in plants. All the filters (phenolics and ascarosides) were applied to the plant and nematode samples. A heatmap of all plant samples against all <em>Caenorhabditis elegans</em> strains was used to compare the abundance of each class ranging the samples analysed within 5 ppm of mass error (<a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Figure 8</a>). Note the major occurrence of the ascarosides in the <em>Caenorhabditis elegans</em> strains rather than in plants. Some false positives can be found, probably due to isobaric compounds or even coincidental fragmentation patterns.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="F8"><h4 class="obj_head">FIGURE 8.</h4>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=8962509_nihms-1053957-f0008.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/9a11/8962509/9c7ae7f9c100/nihms-1053957-f0008.jpg" loading="lazy" height="278" width="800" alt="FIGURE 8"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/F8/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p id="P57">(A) Heatmap comparing the abundance of each targeted class in the samples analysed. Note the abundance of the ascarosides in the <em>Caenorhabditis elegans</em> strains rather than in plants and the abundance of phenolics in plants compared with <em>Caenorhabditis elegans</em>. (B) The pie chart was constructed by summing up the signal intensities detected by each class grouped as phenolics in plants, ascarosides in plants (blue false positive), phenolics in worms (yellow false positive), and ascarosides in worms</p></figcaption></figure><p id="P39">From the heatmap, it is possible to observe certain patterns such as the occurrence of shikimic acid in all plant samples, the high abundance of caffeoylquinic acid <em>m/z</em> features in the <em>Coffea arabica</em> samples and of hydroxy and methoxy flavones at the <em>Echinacea</em> spp., <em>Glycyrrhiza glabra</em>, <em>Mimulus lewisii</em> and <em>M. cardinalis</em>. The high ion intensities of methoxy flavonoids and monoglycoside derivatives in the <em>Echinacea</em> spp. is clear. From the data analysed here, it was not possible to correctly identify those false positive occurrences for the plant or the nematode samples. Signals at <em>m/z</em> 371, 387, 377 and 379 may be related to glucoside substituted jasmonic acid derivatives fairly common in plant leaves, and most of the other false positives might be related to alkyl sugar derivatives due to the loss of 162 and 180 units.</p>
<p id="P40">Hand annotation using the raw file spectra was done and compared to the output from LipidXplorer to corroborate the results. All of the semi-automatically-generated LipidXplorer results were identified as meaningful compounds. The most useful information to aid in such a validation was the “differenceFrag#” and “SUMCOMPOSITIONFrag#” parameters that yield the difference between the precursor and product ion masses. In most cases of positive results, this yielded the neutral loss of a hexose, pentose or a deoxyhexose, as expected. Thus, the effectiveness of this method will depend on how well the fragmentation pattern of different classes is described in the MFQL file. However, because fragmentation patterns can differ widely between different instruments, much deeper fragmentation might lead to false negative results. The user must consider carefully how detailed to describe the fragmentations at the MFQL file aiming to exclude the false positive results. Different instruments or even fine adjustments in the same instrument might give a slightly different fragmentation, mostly resulting in less intense signals.</p>
<p id="P41">The application of LipidXplorer was found to be successful to get a valuable qualitative snapshot of common phenolic contents within plant samples by applying the written MFQL files as restrictors. While direct infusion analysis can lose some information due to ion suppression, it has the great advantage of providing a fast screen, which is the main focus of this study. The output of our procedure simplifies dereplication of phenolic compounds from plants. All scripts are available in the <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supporting Information</a> or the Edison lab GitHub.</p>
<p id="P42">This method was evaluated against a well-used software (MZMine®) using well-known medicinal plants extracts. LipidXplorer was shown to be as useful to detect the presence of a compound regarding the MFQL file as MZMine that uses databases. Because our method is database-independent, it is better for possible new compound detection and classification. This simple generic method provides a complementary source of information for dereplication of natural products using MS2 based Molecular Networks.<sup><a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">9</a>,<a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">24</a>,<a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">25</a></sup> While being independent of databases, the presented detection filtering method for phenolics has the advantage to be generic and exchangeable between laboratories, an important aspect in natural products dereplication procedures.</p>
<p id="P43">Scripts were also created to aid in the generation of new MFQL files, which are relatively simple to create and apply to any class of natural products or metabolic pathways. This was illustrated here by the creation of a second set of MFQL files that is useful in analysis of ascarosides from nematodes. As other researchers develop and share similar MFQL files, dereplication and efficient annotation of natural products from the worldwide biodiversity will become easier. Even though LipidXplorer was designed for DI-ESI-MS data sets, it is possible to apply this approach for LC-MS/MS data using sequential values for the parameter “timerange” in the “Import Settings”.</p></section></section><section id="SM1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
