</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Barley (<em>Hordeum vulgare</em> L.) is a nutrient-rich
whole grain (WG) with pharmacological potential, partly attributed
to its phenolamide content. Using ultrahigh-pressure liquid chromatography
coupled with high-resolution electrospray mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRESI-MS)
integrated with Global Natural Products Social (GNPS) molecular networking,
we identified 50 phenolamides in WG barley, including 13 agmatines,
14 spermidines, two putrescine conjugates, and 21 hordatines. Notably,
we report for the first time two <em>p</em>-coumaroyl hydroxyagmatine
derivatives (including a novel <em>cis</em>-isomer), three
glycosylated spermidine conjugates, and two putrescine conjugates.
Diagnostic MS/MS fragmentation enabled differentiation of 12 spermidine
isomers, including CouCaf-Spd [Caf-(Put)-(<em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP)-Cou vs. Cou-(Put)-(<em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP)-Caf]
and CafFer-Spd [Caf-(Put)-(<em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP)-Fer
vs. Fer-(Put)-(<em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP)-Caf]. Targeted
quantification of 44 phenolamides across various barley-based products
(e.g., alcoholic and nonalcoholic beers, bread, flake, flour, hulled
grains, and pearl grains) revealed product-specific distribution patterns,
with hordatines and agmatine conjugates enriched in beers and spermidine
conjugates more abundant in hulled grains and flours. Our findings
expand the known diversity of barley phenolamides and offer a foundation
for further investigation into their functional significance.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> barley (<em>Hordeum vulgare</em> L.), phenolamides, hordatines, spermidine conjugates, molecular
networking, targeted quantitation</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><hr class="headless">
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/bc64/12412163/c18f2fe55d0f/jf5c04844_0011.jpg" loading="lazy" id="tgr1" height="372" width="666" alt="graphic file with name jf5c04844_0011.jpg"></p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>Whole grain (WG) cereals
are essential components of the human
diet and are associated with reduced risks of chronic diseases including
diabetes, obesity, cancer, and cardiovascular diseases. Among these,
barley (<em>Hordeum vulgare</em> L.; Poaceae) stands out due
to its long history of cultivation and its growing recognition over
the past three to four decades as a health-promoting cereal. In addition to dietary fibers, vitamins, minerals,
and essential amino acids, barely is rich in diverse bioactive phytochemicals.
These include phenolamides (e.g., hydroxycinnamic acid conjugates
of agmatine, spermidine, and putrescine), as well as hordatines, which
are dimers of hydroxycinnamoyl agmatines, alongside flavonoids, procyanidins/proanthocyanidins,
phytosterols, tocopherols, and folates, all contributing to its high
antioxidant potential.
</p>
<p>Phenolamides
are specialized metabolites that accumulate during
plant development and in response to environmental stress. Besides
reinforcing cell-wall integrity, they also play protective roles by
deterring herbivores and inhibiting opportunistic pathogens. Although
their pharmacological potential remains underexplored, phenolamides
represent a compelling example of metabolic cross-talk between nitrogen
and phenolic pathways. To date, only a
limited number of barley-derived phenolamides have been purified<em>
,
</em> or tentatively characterized without full structural elucidation.<em>
,
</em> Beyond their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antidiabetic properties,<em>
,
</em> hordatines have also shown potential as anti-SARS-CoV-2 agents. Their precursors, <em>p</em>-coumaroyl
agmatine and feruloylagmatine, have the capacity to detoxify methylglyoxal,
a reactive carbonyl compound linked to diabetic complications, via
mechanisms resembling those of metformin. Similarly, spermidine-conjugated hydroxycinnamic acids have exhibited
various pharmacological effects, including antibiotic, anti-inflammatory,
antitumor, anti-Alzheimer, antiviral, and antityrosinase properties. Nevertheless, the biological activities of many
phenolamides remain insufficiently characterized.</p>
<p>There are
few studies that reported hordatines in germinated barley,<em>
,
</em> as well as their precursors and spermidines conjugates in malted
barley and beer and hordatines and their
precursors in beer brewing byproducts. However, the chemical profiles of these compounds in WG barley remain
largely unexplored, which also leads to a lack of knowledge in terms
of the content of these bioactive phenolamides in barley-based products,
such as bread, flake, flour, hulled barley, pearl barley, and alcoholic
and nonalcoholic beers. Only limited studies have addressed this topic,
most notably, one reporting the average total hordatine content in
208 beers based on <em>p</em>-coumaric acid equivalents and another quantifying a single compound, hordatine
A glucoside, in 10 barley cultivars (including six hulled barley and
four hull-less barley).
</p>
<p>The Global
Natural Products Social (GNPS) molecular networking
is a mass spectrometry (MS)-based platform that can help researchers
gain a comprehensive understanding of chemical and metabolic profiling
through worldwide data sharing. GNPS
employs advanced algorithms to analyze fragmentation patterns that
might not be obvious using traditional methods and, therefore, to
build molecular networks of compounds that share similar fragmentation
patterns. With such as a powerful feature, it has recently been used
to facilitate the identification of new bioactive compounds, such as steroidal saponins from oat bran.
</p>
<p>This study aims to identify new phytochemicals
in WG barley and
build up the chemical fingerprint using fragmentation patterns of
phenolamides in both positive and negative modes in combination with
GNPS. Furthermore, the quantification of phenolamides in various barley-based
products is conducted using ultrahigh-pressure liquid chromatography
coupled with high-resolution electrospray mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HRESI-MS).</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Barley-Based Products, Chemicals, and Reagents</h3>
<p>The alcoholic beers (<em>n</em> = 2 types) were obtained
from a local supermarket (Food Lion, Kannapolis, NC), while the nonalcoholic
beers (<em>n</em> = 6 types), bread (<em>n</em> =
1 type), flake (<em>n</em> = 1 type), flour (<em>n</em> = 4 types), hulled grain (<em>n</em> = 4 types), and pearl
grain (<em>n</em> = 5 types) were ordered from Amazon. The
ACS-grade, HPLC-grade, and LC-MS-grade solvents, such as methanol
(MeOH), acetonitrile (MeCN), deionized water, and formic acid (FA),
were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Petersburg, PA). Hordatines
A–C (HA–HC), glycosylated hordatines A and B (HAG and
HBG), and hordatine precursors, including <em>p</em>-coumaroyl
agmatine (Cou-Agm) and feruloyl agmatine (Fer-Agm), and <em>p</em>-coumaroyl putrescine (Cou-Put) were either synthesized in-house
or purified from barley extract.
</p></section><section id="sec2.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Preparation of Barley Samples</h3>
<p>The
dry forms of barley-based products, such as flakes, breads, flour,
hulled grains, and pearl grains, were powdered using a SHARDOR Adjustable
Coffee Grinder (Changsha Shardor Electrical Appliance Technology Co.,
Ltd., Changsha, China) at 20,000 × <em>g</em> for 30
s per sample. A total of 100 mg of each sample was extracted with
1 mL of 80% MeOH containing 0.1% FA at 20 °C using a homogenizer
for 10 min. The samples were then sonicated for 10 min and centrifuged
for 15 min at 15,000 × <em>g</em>. This extraction process
was conducted twice. For the liquid forms of barley-based products,
alcoholic and nonalcoholic beers, 1 mL of MeCN was added to 1 mL of
each sample, followed by centrifugation for 15 min (15,000 × <em>g</em>) to remove any possible precipitate of proteins and undissolved
material. Finally, all centrifuged and filtered samples were diluted
10-fold before UPLC-HRMS analysis.</p></section><section id="sec2.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Preparation of Standards</h3>
<p>To quantify
the phenolamides in various barley-based products, we prepared a stock
solution of mixed barley standards. This included hordatines A–C
(HA–HC), glycosylated hordatines A and B (HAG and HBG), and
hordatine precursors, including <em>p</em>-coumaroyl agmatine
(Cou-Agm) and feruloyl agmatine (Fer-Agm), and <em>p</em>-coumaroyl
putrescine (Cou-Put). Preliminary analyses
of barley samples indicated the highest concentrations of 5 μM
for HA–HC, Cou-Agm, Fer-Agm, and Cou-Put and 50 μM for
HAG and HBG, which were present in higher quantities. The main stock
solution of mixed standards was then serially diluted 12-fold to have
distinct concentration ranges for calibration curves: 2.44–5000
nM for the first set of standards (HA–HC, Cou-Agm, Fer-Agm,
and Cou-Put) and 24.41–50000 nM for the second set (HAG and
HBG).</p>
<p>To ensure reliable and accurate data, a quality control
(QC) sample containing 1.56 μM each of HA–HC, Cou-Agm,
Fer-Agm, and Cou-Put and 0.156 μM each of HAG and HBG was included
every 12 samples to enhance the rigor of the quantitative analysis.
The relative standard deviation (RSD%) for individual standards in
the QC sample was measured as follows: HA (12.04%), HAG (11.30%),
HB (11.86%), HBG (9.27%), HC (16.84%), Cou-Agm (11.30%), Fer-Agm (10.84%),
and Cou-Put (7.76%). The concentration ranges (nM), linear equations
(<em>y</em>), and their regressions (<em>R</em>
<sup>2</sup>) are presented in <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table 
</a>. The standard curves are shown in <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.jafc.5c04844/suppl_file/jf5c04844_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure S1</a>.</p>

</div></div></section></section><section id="sec2.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. UHPLC-HR-ESI-MS Analysis</h3>
<p>An ultrahigh
pressure liquid chromatograph (UHPLC) coupled with an Orbitrap Q Executive
Plus (QE+) accurate mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was
used to quantify phenolamides in barley-based products (<em>n</em> = 20). The UHPLC was equipped with an autosampler (VANQUISH model),
a column compartment (VANQUISH model), binary pumps (VANQUISH model),
and a degasser (VANQUISH model). Chromatographic separation was obtained
using a reverse-phase Hypersil GOLD analytical column (100 ×
1.0 × 1.9 μm<sup>3</sup>) at a temperature of 40 °C,
with a solvent flow rate of 0.2 mL/min and injection volume of 3 μL.
The gradient elution consisted of eluent A (water + 0.1% FA) and eluent
B (MeCN + 0.1% FA). The gradient elution profile was as follows: 2%
B (0–1.0 min), 2–15% B (1.0–1.5 min), 15% B (1.5–6.0
min), 15–30% B (6.0–13.0 min), 30–50% B (13.0–16.0
min), 50–100% B (16.0–17.0 min), 100% B (17.0–21.0
min), and 2% B (21.0–21.5 min).</p>
<p>Mass analyses were performed
using a QE+ accurate mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray
ionization (ESI) source, operating in negative and positive ionization
modes. Collision energies (CE) were set at 20, 35, and 50 eV to optimize
fragmentation and maximize transmission of the desired product ions.
The resolutions were 70,000 (full mass) and 17,500 (ddMS<sup>2</sup>), with scan range of 100 to 1200 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>. The MS operating conditions included sheath gas (N<sub>2</sub>)
flow at 50 L/min, auxiliary gas (N<sub>2</sub>) flow at 5 L/min, sweep
gas (N<sub>2</sub>) flow rate at 3 L/min; capillary temperature at
350 °C; auxiliary gas heater temperature at 365 °C; spray
current at 12.5 μA; and spray voltage at 1.0 kV. LC-HRESI-MS
data were acquired and processed by using Xcalibur software (Thermo
Fisher Scientific).</p></section><section id="sec2.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking
(GNPS) Analysis</h3>
<p>The UHPLC-HRESI-MS raw data of WG barley
extract, obtained in positive and negative modes, were converted to
the mzML format using the MSconvert package (Version 3.0.21271, Proteowizard
Software Foundation, USA). This format ensures compatibility with
GNPS, and the data were uploaded to the GNPS platform using WinSCP
Version 6.3.6. The data were then subjected to GNPS analysis (Molecular
networking) through the online platform at <a href="https://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/static/gnps-splash.jsp" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/static/gnps-splash.jsp</a>. The resultant networks were analyzed online.
</p></section><section id="sec2.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Data Analysis</h3>
<p>The quantification
of phenolamides in barley products (<em>n</em> = 23 types)
was performed in two independent samples. The results were expressed
as mean ± SD.</p></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results and Discussion</h2>
<section id="sec3.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Identification of Two <em>p</em>-Coumaroyl
Hydroxyagmatine Derivatives</h3>
<p>In order to establish the chemical
profile of WG barley, a GNPS-guided strategy was applied. As a starting
point for molecular networking, <em>p</em>-coumaroyl agmatine
(Cou-Agm; <strong>1</strong>) (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>A), which serves as the precursor for hordatines A
and B, was selected. Using a stepped normalized collision energy (NCE:
20, 35, 50%), molecular networking delineated a clear relationship
between Cou-Agm (<strong>1</strong>) and an analogue (<strong>2</strong>)
with a 4 Da lower mass. Both compounds featured the diagnostic <em>p</em>-coumaroyl fragment (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 147.0441; C<sub>9</sub>H<sub>7</sub>O<sub>2</sub>). In the case
of compound <strong>2</strong>, higher-resolution MS<sup>1</sup> at NCE
20% revealed a molecular formula of C<sub>14</sub>H<sub>19</sub>O<sub>3</sub>N<sub>4</sub> (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 291.1448),
while the MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum exhibited a prominent ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 273.1342 [M – H<sub>2</sub>O
+ H]<sup>+</sup>, corresponding to dehydration during ionization (C<sub>14</sub>H<sub>17</sub>O<sub>2</sub>N<sub>4</sub>) (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>C). The LC-HRMS chromatogram
displayed two peaks at 7.46 and 9.81 min (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>B), both exhibiting the same molecular formula
and mass, indicative of isomeric forms. In the positive ion mode,
the fragmentation patterns of compounds <strong>1</strong> and <strong>2</strong> showed different behaviors, although they were similar in the <em>p</em>-coumaroyl part. Compound <strong>1</strong> lost 17 Da ([M
– NH<sub>3</sub> + H]<sup>+</sup>), while compound <strong>2</strong> underwent two successive neutral losses
of 18 Da; yielding <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 255.1236;
C<sub>14</sub>H<sub>15</sub>ON<sub>4</sub>), followed by a neutral
loss of 42 Da (CH<sub>2</sub>N<sub>2</sub>), which is characteristic
of the terminal part of the agmatine chain, yielding <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 213.1022 (C<sub>13</sub>H<sub>13</sub>ON<sub>2</sub>). Moreover, compound <strong>2</strong> exhibited a loss of 126
Da (C<sub>5</sub>H<sub>10</sub>N<sub>4</sub>), which is 4 Da less
than that of the agmatine fragment (130 Da). This observation suggested
that the agmatine moiety in compound <strong>2</strong> may undergo two
oxidative modifications, following the initial loss of a water molecule.
This formed two additional double bonds within the putrescine chain
(<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>D). To further
confirm this hypothesis, the fragmentation pattern of compound <strong>2</strong> was also analyzed in the negative ion mode. The precursor
ion appeared at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 271.1192 [M
– H<sub>2</sub>O – H]<sup>−</sup>, consistent
with the loss of a water molecule. In this mode, compound <strong>2</strong> displayed two major fragments: a neutral loss of 120 Da, corresponding
to a 4-vinylphenol residue (yielding <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 156.0621, C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>7</sub>ON<sub>4</sub>), and a neutral
loss of 152 Da, indicating the presence of a carbonyl group and an
oxidized agmatine residue (yielding <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 119.0499, C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>7</sub>O from the <em>p</em>-coumaroyl part) (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>E). Collectively, these findings identified compound <strong>2</strong> as <em>p</em>-coumaroyl hydroxydehydroagmatine (Cou-OHdhAgm),
newly reported in WG barley.</p>

to get authentic standards of these two isomers and further confirm
their structures using NMR.</p></section><section id="sec3.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Chemical Profile of Agmatine-Containing Phenolamides
in Whole Grain Barley</h3>
<p>Agmatine-containing phenolamides are
predominant in barley and are also present
in lower quantities in rye and wheat grains. However, their detailed chemical profile
has yet to be explored. In addition to the connection between <em>p</em>-coumaroyl agmatine (<strong>1</strong>) and <em>p</em>-coumaroyl hydroxydehydroagmatine (Cou-OHdhAgm, <strong>2</strong>),
further analysis of agmatine-containing phenolamides by GNPS in positive
ion mode revealed limited connections between compounds. Consequently,
the chemical profile of agmatine-containing phenolamides was examined
using knowledge-based approaches, involving a search for known compounds
reported in the literature with their structures confirmed by analyzing
HR-ESI-MS/MS data. The chemical structures, including agmatines (<strong>1</strong>, <strong>9</strong>, <strong>11</strong>), hydroxyagmatines (<strong>2a/2b</strong>, <strong>6</strong>, <strong>8</strong>), methylated agmatines
(<strong>10</strong>, <strong>12</strong>), and sugar-containing agmatines
(<strong>3</strong>–<strong>5</strong>, <strong>7</strong>), along with
their corresponding LC/MS profiles, are shown in <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>. It is worth mentioning that
the levels of <em>p</em>-coumaroyl- and feruloyl hydroxyagmatine
(<strong>6</strong> and <strong>8</strong>) were found to be higher than their
respective non-hydroxylated counterparts (i.e., <strong>1</strong> and <strong>11</strong>).</p>

<p>To expand the network of barley phenolamides,

through shared fragmentation pathways. The characteristic fragment

identified as follows: <em>p</em>-coumaroyl residue at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 119.0497 (C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>7</sub>O), caffeoyl residue at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 135.0446
(C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>7</sub>O<sub>2</sub>), and feruloyl residue at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 149.0603 (C<sub>9</sub>H<sub>9</sub>O<sub>2</sub>) (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>B). The network between <em>p</em>-Cou-Agm (<strong>1</strong>) and spermidine conjugates di-<em>p</em>-coumaroyl spermidine
(diCou-Spd; <strong>13</strong>) and <em>p</em>-coumaroyl feruloyl
spermidine (CouFer-Spd; <strong>14</strong>) was established based on
their shared <em>p</em>-coumaroyl fragment ion (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 119.0497; C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>7</sub>O). This
network extended from CouFer-Spd (<strong>14</strong>) to feruloyl caffeoyl
spermidine (FerCaf-Spd; <strong>15</strong>), diferuloyl spermidine (diFer-Spd; <strong>16</strong>), feruloyl hydroxyagmatine (Fer-OHAgm; <strong>8</strong>),
and feruloyl agmatine (Fer-Agm; <strong>11</strong>), all of which share
the feruloyl fragment ion (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 149.0609;
C<sub>9</sub>H<sub>9</sub>O<sub>2</sub>). Additionally, compound <strong>15</strong>, which contains both feruloyl and caffeoyl moieties, also
shares the caffeoyl fragment ion (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 135.0446; C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>7</sub>O<sub>2</sub>) with dicaffeoyl
spermidine (diCaf-Spd; <strong>17</strong>), suggesting a dual interaction
between feruloyl and caffeoyl derivatives in these polyamine conjugates.
The fragmentation pattern of compound <strong>17</strong> was found to
be similar to the findings of Kang et al., who identified the same
compound in red, brown, and white sorghum whole grains. Therefore, this compound is most likely a linear
spermidine diconjugate with caffeoyl moieties attached at the <em>N</em>
<sup>1</sup> and <em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup> positions.
Overall, the observed molecular network supports the hypothesis that <em>p</em>-coumaroyl, caffeoyl, and feruloyl residues play critical
roles in the structural organization and fragmentation behavior of
these spermidine-containing phenolamides.</p>

Characteristic Fragment Ions for Structure
Confirmation of Spermidine-Containing Phenolamides</h3>
<p>GNPS molecular
networking identified a variety of spermidine conjugates, including
monoconjugates (compounds <strong>18</strong> and <strong>20</strong>), diconjugates
(compounds <strong>13</strong>–<strong>17</strong> and <strong>22</strong>), and hexoside-containing conjugates (compounds <strong>19</strong> and <strong>21</strong>) (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figures 
</a>A and A). Building upon these results, we
further identified two structurally related compounds: <em>p</em>-coumaroyl spermidine (Cou-Spd; <strong>23</strong>) and feruloyl caffeoyl
spermidine hexoside (FerCaf-Spd Hex; <strong>24</strong>). While monoconjugates
(compounds <strong>18</strong>, <strong>20</strong>, and <strong>23</strong>),
dihomoconjugates (compounds <strong>13</strong>, <strong>16</strong>, and <strong>17</strong>), and diheteroconjugates (compounds <strong>14</strong>, <strong>15</strong>, and <strong>22</strong>) have previously been reported in
barley and beer, this is the first report
to completely elucidate their structures based on detailed analysis
of their fragment ions. Additionally, this is also the first report
of hexoside-containing spermidine conjugates (compounds <strong>19</strong>, <strong>21</strong>, and <strong>24</strong>).</p>
<p>Due to the asymmetric
nature of spermidine, which possesses two terminal NH<sub>2</sub> groups,
one associated with putrescine and the other with the <em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>-(3-aminopropyl) (<em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP)
portion, hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives can form amide bonds at
either end. As a result, <em>p</em>-coumaroyl, caffeoyl, and
feruloyl groups may attach to either the NH<sub>2</sub>-terminus of
putrescine (<em>N</em>
<sup>1</sup>) or the NH<sub>2</sub>-terminus of <em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP (<em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>). Although several spermidine-containing phenolamides
have been previously reported in WG barley, the exact linkage sites
of hydroxycinnamic acids on the spermidine backbone have not been
fully elucidated.</p>
<p>By analyzing MS<sup>2</sup> fragmentation
patterns, we identified
characteristic fragment ions that provide insight into the structural
isomerism of these conjugates. In monoconjugated spermidines, including
Caf-Spd (<strong>18</strong>), Fer-Spd (<strong>20</strong>), and Cou-Spd
(<strong>23</strong>), fragmentation resulted in pairs of diagnostic ions
at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 220 and 234 (for <strong>18</strong>), 234 and 248 (for <strong>20</strong>), and 204 and 218 (for <strong>23</strong>) (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>B), suggesting the presence of two isomeric forms in barley. This
pattern was also observed in dihomoconjugated spermidines, including
diCou-Spd (<strong>13</strong>), diFer-Spd (<strong>16</strong>), and diCafSpd
(<strong>17</strong>), which show similar dual-fragmentation signatures
(<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>C). To explore
this further, we examined the fragmentation patterns of spermidine
diheteroconjugates, including CafFer-Spd (<strong>15a</strong>/<strong>15b</strong>) and CafCou-Spd (<strong>22a</strong>/<strong>22b</strong>) (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>D). Although the GNPS network
(<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>A) showed
a single node for each compound, the LC-MS chromatograms exhibited
two closely spaced peaks, indicating the presence of structural isomers.
Detailed analysis of the fragmentation patterns enabled the structural
elucidation of these isomers. For CafFer-Spd (<strong>15</strong>), the
fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 234.1123 (C<sub>13</sub>H<sub>16</sub>O<sub>3</sub>N) indicated attachment of the
feruloyl group to the <em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP moiety, assigning
compound <strong>15a</strong> (<em>t</em>
<sub>R</sub> = 17.22
min) the structure Caf-Put-(<em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP)-Fer.
Conversely, in compound <strong>15b</strong> (<em>t</em>
<sub>R</sub> = 17.49 min), the fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 220.0967 (C<sub>12</sub>H<sub>14</sub>O<sub>2</sub>N) suggested
feruloylation at the putrescine moiety. This fragmentation pattern
was consistent with the structure of Fer-(Put)-(<em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP)-Caf. A similar strategy was applied to CafCou-Spd
(<strong>22</strong>). In compound <strong>22a</strong> (<em>t</em>
<sub>R</sub> = 16.85 min), the fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 204.1018 (C<sub>12</sub>H<sub>14</sub>O<sub>2</sub>N) supported the presence of a <em>p</em>-coumaroyl group
on the <em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP moiety, yielding the structure
Caf-(Put)-(<em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP)-Cou. In contrast, compound <strong>22b</strong> (<em>t</em>
<sub>R</sub> = 16.97 min) produced
a fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 220.0967 (C<sub>12</sub>H<sub>14</sub>O<sub>3</sub>N), indicating caffeoyl substitution
on the <em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP moiety. This fragmentation
pattern was consistent with the structure of Cou-(Put)-(<em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP)-Caf (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>D). Using the same analytical strategy, CouFer-Spd (<strong>14</strong>) was confirmed as Cou-Put-(<em>N</em>
<sup>10</sup>3AP)-Fer, based on the observation of a single chromatographic peak
and the presence of characteristic fragment ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 234.1123 and 218.1175 (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>D). Similarly, the structure of the sugar-containing
spermidine dihomoconjugate (diCafSpd-Hex; <strong>21</strong>) was confirmed
as shown in <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>D.</p></section><section id="sec3.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.5. Chemical Profile of Spermidine-Containing

<p>Hordatines, which are dimers of hydroxycinnamic acid agmatines,
constitute a class of barley-specific phytochemicals. Hordatines A,
B, and C and their corresponding glucosides are the major hordatines
in barley. Pihlava proposed the term hordatines A<sub>1</sub> and
A<sub>2</sub> for those hordatines that contain one and two hydroxyl
groups in their agmatine residues, respectively, which also applies
to hordatines B and C. However, the detailed
chemical profile of hordatines is insufficiently characterized.</p>
<p>To address this knowledge gap, we applied the same GNPS molecular
networking strategy as that used for spermidine conjugates to develop
the chemical profile of hordatines. Prior to GNPS analysis, we first
examined the ionization behavior and fragmentation patterns of the
hordatines. Hordatine A<sub>2</sub> (<strong>27</strong>), a homodimer
of two units of <em>p</em>-coumaroyl hydroxyagmatine (Cou-OHAgm),
was selected as an example to elucidate the fragmentation patterns
of hordatines in both positive and negative ion modes. In positive
ionization mode, this compound exhibited two ion types: singly charged
[M + H]<sup>+</sup> at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 583.2984
and doubly charged [M + 2H]<sup>2+</sup> at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 292.1530 (<a href="#fig6" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>A). Although the singly charged ion was detected at a lower
intensity (NL: 1.11 × 10<sup>6</sup>), it provided more informative
fragmentation data. Successive neutral losses of 146 Da (hydroxyagmatine
residue) led to the production of ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 437.1815 and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 291.0652.
Additional fragmentation of the hydroxyagmatine moiety resulted in
characteristic losses of 17 (−NH<sub>3</sub>), 25 (−CN+H),
18 (−H<sub>2</sub>O), and 86 Da (−C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>10</sub>N<sub>2</sub>). In contrast, the doubly charged ion displayed
much higher intensity in both the LC chromatogram (NL: 1.77 ×
10<sup>7</sup>) and MS<sup>1</sup>, making it advantageous for detecting
hordatines. However, its fragmentation pattern was approximately half
that of the singly charged ion (<a href="#fig6" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>A), suggesting a limited structural elucidation value.</p>

<p>Based
on the structures of the 19 hordatines (<strong>27</strong>–<strong>45</strong>) identified by GNPS molecular networking, additional hordatines,
such as methylated hordatines and di-, tri-, and tetra-glycosylated

Methylated hordatines B (<strong>46</strong>) and C (<strong>47</strong>)
were found in the extract. Although previously reported di-, tri-,
and tetra-glycosylated hordatines were detected based on their corresponding

either molecular network or the chemical fingerprint. The combination
of GNPS molecular networking and knowledge-based information identified
21 major hordatines. These hordatines can be classified into seven
groups, including hordatines A–C, hordatines A–C glucosides,
hordatines A<sub>1</sub>–C<sub>1</sub>, hordatines A<sub>1</sub>–C<sub>1</sub> hexosides, hordatines A<sub>2</sub>–C<sub>2</sub>, hordatines A<sub>2</sub>–C<sub>2</sub> hexosides,

</a>).</p>

<p>To quantify phenolamides in commercial barley-based
products, including alcoholic beers (<em>n</em> = 2 types),
nonalcoholic beers (<em>n</em> = 6 types), bread (<em>n</em> = 1 type), flake (<em>n</em> = 1 type), flour

a quantitative LC-HRMS method using previously synthesized and purified

glycosylated hordatines A and B. In total,
50 phenolamides were identified, encompassing putrescine, spermidine,
and agmatine conjugates as well as hordatines. Due to high variability
observed in the levels of compounds <strong>2a/2b</strong>, <strong>4</strong>, <strong>19</strong>, <strong>21</strong>, and <strong>24</strong> across product
types, quantitative analysis was performed on the remaining 44 phenolamides.
Their total contents are presented in <a href="#fig10" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>A,B, with concentrations expressed in μg
per 100 mL of beer or μg per 100 g of dry product. An example
of the chemical profile of various barley-based products is shown
in <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.jafc.5c04844/suppl_file/jf5c04844_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figures S2A,B</a>, while the quantification

production processes, and grain variety may influence their abundance.</p>

Notably, their concentrations in beer products (0.24–1.05 μg/100

mL beer for compound <strong>26</strong>) were higher compared to barely

and pearl grain (0.05–0.27 μg/100 g product for compound <strong>25</strong> and 0.08–0.58 μg/100 g product for compound <strong>26</strong>) (<a href="#fig10" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>, <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.jafc.5c04844/suppl_file/jf5c04844_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S4</a>).</p>

derivatives, 21 compounds were quantified
using LC-HRMS. Pure HA (<strong>36</strong>) was used as the quantification
standard for HA<sub>2</sub> (<strong>27</strong>), HA<sub>1</sub> (<strong>34</strong>), HA (<strong>36</strong>), HA<sub>2</sub>G (<strong>37</strong>), HA<sub>1</sub>G (<strong>41</strong>), and Met-HA (<strong>46</strong>), while pure HB (<strong>35</strong>) served as the standard for HB<sub>1</sub>G (<strong>28</strong>), HB<sub>2</sub> (<strong>29</strong>), HB<sub>1</sub> (<strong>33</strong>), HB (<strong>35</strong>), HB<sub>2</sub>G
(<strong>39</strong>), and Met-HB (<strong>40</strong>). determinant factor. Consistent with our findings, Kohyama and Ono

hulled and four hull-less varieties, and observed a significant reduction
in HAG content following pearling. This result indicated that HAG

18.7 mg.L<sup>–1</sup> PCAE.
</p>
<p>To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study
of WG barley phenolamides using GNPS molecular networking in combination
with MS<sup>2</sup> fragmentation patterns in both positive and negative
ion modes to generate a detailed chemical fingerprint of these compounds.
This integrative approach resulted in the identification of <em>p</em>-coumaroyl hydroxyagmatine derivatives, specifically <em>p</em>-coumaroyl hydroxydehydroagmatine (Cou-OHdhAgm) in two
isomeric forms, <em>cis</em>-Cou-OHdhAgm (<strong>2a</strong>)
and <em>trans</em>-Cou-OHdhAgm (<strong>2b</strong>). Our findings
revealed that barley phenolamides are primarily composed of hordatines
and their precursors (agmatine conjugates), followed by spermidine
conjugates and, to a lower extent, putrescine conjugates. Of particular
note is the first-time identification of sugar-containing spermidine
conjugates (Fer-Spd-Hex <strong>19</strong>, diCaf-Spd-Hex <strong>21</strong>, and FerCaf-Spd-Hex <strong>24</strong>), as well as putrescine conjugates
(Cou-Put <strong>25</strong> and Fer-Put <strong>26</strong>) in WG barley,
expanding its known chemical diversity. In positive ion mode, hordatines
generated both singly and doubly charged ions, with doubly charged
species displaying a higher signal intensity. Meanwhile, their systematic
fragmentation in negative ion mode provided valuable structural insights,
enabling the construction of a detailed GNPS molecular network and
allowing for a deeper exploration of hordatine diversity across barley
varieties. For spermidine-based phenolamides, positive ion mode offered
characteristic peaks, enabling rapid structural confirmation. Notably,
the structures of 12 out of the 14 spermidine conjugates, including
mono- (compounds <strong>18</strong>, <strong>20</strong>, and <strong>23</strong>), dihomo- (compounds <strong>13</strong>, <strong>16</strong>, and <strong>17</strong>), and diheteroconjugates (compounds <strong>14</strong>, <strong>15a</strong>/<strong>15b</strong>, and <strong>22a</strong>/<strong>22b</strong>), and a hexoside-containing dihomoconjugate (compound <strong>21</strong>), were fully elucidated for the first time. Quantification results
across barley-based products indicated that beers were particularly
rich in hordatines and their precursors. In contrast, hulled grains
and flours exhibited higher concentrations of spermidine conjugates
compared with beers, bread, and pearl grains. Among all products,
barley bread contained the lowest levels of phenolamides, likely due
to the effects of intensive processing.</p>
<p>While this study provides
a comprehensive chemical and quantitative
profile of barley phenolamides, it does have certain limitations.
Notably, in vivo or functional bioactivity data are not included,
which limits our ability to directly infer health outcomes. Additionally,
limited availability of authentic standards for certain subclasses
may affect the precision of quantification for less abundant or novel
analogs.</p>
<p>Nevertheless, our findings strongly suggest that barley
phenolamides
may contribute to the nutritional and health-promoting properties
of barley-based foods and beverages. However, these potential benefits
are modulated by fermentation, food processing, and genotype-specific
variation. These factors should be carefully considered in both future
research and food formulation.</p></section></section><section id="sec20"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
