<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Background</h3>
<p id="Par1">Fluoride-resistant <em>Streptococcus mutans</em> (<em>S. mutans</em>) strains have developed due to the wide use of fluoride in dental caries prevention. However, the metabolomics of fluoride-resistant <em>S. mutans</em> remains unclear.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objective</h3>
<p id="Par2">This study aimed to identify metabolites that discriminate fluoride-resistant from wild-type <em>S. mutans</em>.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h3>
<p id="Par3">Cell supernatants from fluoride-resistant and wild-type <em>S. mutans</em> were collected and analyzed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. Principal components analysis and partial least-squares discriminant analysis were performed for the statistical analysis by variable influence on projection (VIP &gt; 2.0) and <em>p</em> value (Mann–Whitney test, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05). Metabolites were assessed qualitatively using the Human Metabolome Database version 2.0 (<a href="http://www.hmdb.ca" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.hmdb.ca</a>), or Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (<a href="http://www.kegg.jp" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.kegg.jp</a>), and Metaboanalyst 6.0 (<a href="https://www.metaboanalyst.ca" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.metaboanalyst.ca</a>).</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p id="Par4">Fourteen metabolites differed significantly between fluoride-resistant and wild-type strains in the early log phase. Among these metabolites, 5 were identified. There were 32 differential metabolites between the two strains in the stationary phase, 13 of which were identified. The pyrimidine metabolism for <em>S. mutans</em> FR was matched with the metabolic pathway.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h3>
<p id="Par5">The fructose-1,6-bisphosphate concentration increased in fluoride-resistant strains under acidic conditions, suggesting enhanced acidogenicity and acid tolerance. This metabolite may be a promising target for elucidating the cariogenic and fluoride resistant mechanisms of <em>S. mutans</em>.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h3>
<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03310-8.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> <em>Streptococcus mutans</em>; Fluoride resistance; Metabolomics; Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate; Dental caries; Mass spectrometry</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Background</h2>
<p id="Par6"><em>Streptococcus mutans</em> (<em>S. mutans</em>) is a major cariogenic pathogen, known as a predominant etiological contributor to dental caries [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>, <a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>]. Its virulence factors are involved in the acidogenicity, the acid tolerance, and the biofilm formation [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>]. Currently, fluoride is one of the most representative caries-preventive agents through reducing the hydroxyapatite solubility. Fluoride can depress demineralization and enhance remineralization of the dental enamels [<a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>]. Fluoride is also proved to inhibit the <em>S. mutans</em> metabolism [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>]. Nevertheless, fluoride-resistant <em>S. mutans</em> (<em>S. mutans</em> FR) appears with the extensive, long-lasting application of fluoride. <em>S. mutans</em> FR obtains the phenotype of enhanced acidogenicity and acid tolerance due to the gene alteration of <em>eriC</em><sup><em>F</em></sup>, encoding fluoride antiporters [<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>–<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>]. Since the enamel demineralization prevention effects are reduced, fluoride cannot effectively inhibit <em>S. mutans</em> FR [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>–<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>].</p>
<p id="Par7">Until now, the specific mechanism remains unclear, by which the fluoride-resistant strains are more powerful in acidogenicity and acid tolerance than the wild-type ones. In recent years, metabolomics focusing on the quantitative analysis of small molecules with &lt; 1,000 molecular weight [<a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>], which may play critical roles in the metabolic processes [<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>], allows us to analyze the metabolomes of target cells with their phenotypic difference, thereby to better explore the underlying mechanism of their difference [<a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>]. The mass spectrometry (MS) is an indispensable tool for metabolomic analysis, especially when combined with liquid chromatography (LC).</p>
<p id="Par8">Previously, molecular changes and carbohydrate metabolism were demonstrated during the development of dental caries. For example, alteration of 37 metabolites was confirmed by MS when culturing <em>S. mutans</em> at different pH values [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. Furthermore, <em>S. mutans</em> biofilm growth [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>, <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>] and acid production [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>] were affected by carbohydrate metabolism. <em>S. mutans</em> membrane vesicles enhanced the metabolite expression of <em>Candida albicans</em> related to carbohydrate metabolism [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>]. In the carbohydrate metabolism process, when fluoride was added into the caries-associated bacteria, for instance, <em>Scardovia wiggsiae</em>, the pyruvate level decreases [<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>]. <em>Bifidobacterium</em> could metabolize carbohydrates and produce acid by the bifid shunt even in the presence of fluoride [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>]. Metabolomic effects of fluoride on plaque biofilm i<em>n vivo</em> revealed fluoride inhibited acid production, along with the phosphoenolpyruvate decrease in the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas pathway [<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>].</p>
<p id="Par9">However, metabolomic data on <em>S. mutans</em> FR are rare. Discriminant metabolomes between fluoride-resistant strains and wild-type ones have not been identified yet. Herein, we did a metabolic analysis of cells from the two <em>S. mutans</em> strains by LC-MS technique. The study aims to determine whether marked variations in the metabolome of the two <em>S. mutans</em> strains exist.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Bacteria and growth conditions</h3>
<p id="Par10">As in our previous study [<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>], we grew wild-type <em>S. mutans</em> (UA159, also named <em>S. mutans</em> UA), stored in a 50% (v/v) glycerol solution at − 80°C in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (BHIB; Difco) or on BHI agar (BHIA; Difco) under anaerobic conditions (37°C, 95% N<sub>2</sub>, 5% CO<sub>2</sub>). Then we sub-cultured UA159 on BHIA and obtained fluoride-resistant <em>S. mutans</em> (UA-FR, also named <em>S. mutans</em> FR) through increasing the sodium-fluoride (NaF) concentrations from 50 µg/mL to 1,000 µg/mL gradually. Each <em>S. mutans</em> FR isolate in this study was selected at a NaF concentration of 1,000 µg/mL.</p></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample collection and preparation</h3>
<p id="Par11">UA159 and UA-FR in suspension were anaerobically cultured in BHIB containing 1% glucose at 37°C through entering the early or the stationary stage of bacterial growth. The metabolic activities of the bacteria ceased when 5 mL broth was mixed with 10 mL 60% methanol aqueous solution at − 48°C. The clarified supernatants were discarded after centrifugation at 4500 r/min for 10 min at − 19°C.</p>
<p id="Par12">Bacteria were harvested under such conditions after washing twice. A total of 500 µL 100% methanol (− 48°C) was added to the bacteria. The mixture was frozen in liquid nitrogen and thawed on ice. After three freeze-thaw cycles, the mixture was centrifugated at 16,000 ×<em>g</em> for 5 min at − 19°C and the first supernatants were collected. After the centrifugation, the remaining bacteria were mixed again with 500 µL 100% methanol (− 48°C). The freeze-thaw cycles and centrifugation were repeated. The second supernatants were collected. Finally, the second supernatants were mixed with the first ones. The mixed supernatants were stored at − 80°C for metabolomic analysis using liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LC-MS).</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LC-MS analysis</h3>
<p id="Par13">The supernatants were analyzed by a liquid chromatography column (Agilent) coupled with a micrOTOF-Q II mass spectrometer (Bruker). The mobile phase included 0.1% formic acid solution (A) and acetonitrile (B). The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min. Initially, 2% acetonitrile was maintained for 0.5 min. Then, the acetonitrile concentration was linearly increased to 100% within 24 min and was maintained for 3.5 min. After that, the acetonitrile concentration was reduced back to 2%. The column was equilibrated for 2 min and the column temperature was 35°C. The temperature of the automatic sampler was maintained at 4°C. The sample load was 2 µL.</p>
<p id="Par14">The MS conditions were set as follows: source mode, both positive and negative modes of electrospray ionization (ESI); mass scanning range, 100 − 1,000 m/z; spray gas flow rate, 1.5 L/min; dry gas pressure, 0.2 MPa; heating temperature, 200°C; ion accumulation time, 20 ms; detection voltage, 1.6 kV; interface voltage, 4.5 kV and − 3.5 kV for the positive and the negative poles, respectively. The acquisition parameters are shown in Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>.</p>

<p id="Par17">The raw data collected using the LC-MS device were proceeded with peak extraction and peak matching. Peak discrimination, peak alignment, total peak area normalization, data scaling, and missing value removal by the correction of 80% rule were also performed. After the proceeded data were normalized by peak area, the potential discriminant metabolites were matched with entries in the version 2.0 of Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, <a href="http://www.hmdb.ca" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.hmdb.ca</a>), or Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, <a href="http://www.kegg.jp" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.kegg.jp</a>). Open database source (Metaboanalyst 6.0, <a href="https://www.metaboanalyst.ca" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.metaboanalyst.ca</a>) was applied to identify metabolic pathways.</p>
<p id="Par18">Orthogonal signal correction (OSC) method was applied to screen out part of the group-independent matrices. The three-dimensional matrix obtained was analyzed by SIMCA-P 12.0 software (Umetrics). The aggregation, dispersion and outliers of the samples were assessed by principal component analysis (PCA) in the filtered matrix. Subsequently, partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was used to identify the major variations that contributed to this aggregation and dispersion. The discriminant metabolites were determined in the single-dimensional statistics by variable influence on projection (VIP &gt; 2.0) and <em>p</em> value (Mann–Whitney test, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05). The quality of the PCA and PLS-DA was assessed by the parameters R<sup>2</sup> and Q<sup>2</sup>.</p></section></section><section id="Sec7"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<p id="Par19">The reversed phase (RP) LC-MS total ion current (TIC) chromatograms of the cells from <em>S. mutans</em> UA and <em>S. mutans</em> FR in early log phase and in stationary phase are shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>, where significant differences of some spectrum peaks existed between the two <em>S. mutans</em> strains. For example, in the RP LC-MS TIC chromatograms, most peaks of <em>S. mutans</em> FR in early log phase were higher than those of <em>S. mutans</em> UA in early log phase (e.g. peaks at retention time of about 8 − 10 and 23 min in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>a), while some peaks of <em>S. mutans</em> FR in early log phase were lower (e.g. peaks at retention time of 15–20 min in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>a). At retention time of 15–20 min, similarly, the peaks of <em>S. mutans</em> FR in stationary phase were also lower (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>b). Remarkable variations between them were also demonstrated in the hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) LC-MS TIC chromatograms (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>). Complementary to the RP method, the HILIC one was suitable to retain polar metabolites. At retention time of about 2 and 3 min, the peaks of <em>S. mutans</em> FR were lower than those of <em>S. mutans</em> UA both in early log phase (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>a) and in stationary phase (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>b).</p>

</div></div></section><p id="Par30">In early log phase and in stationary phase, the common significant discriminant metabolites were 1-octadecanoyl-sn-glycerol 3-phosphate, 6-phospho-D-glucono-1,5-lactone, CMP, and pyridoxamine 5’-phosphate. Interestingly, reduced 6-phospho-D-glucono-1,5-lactone and pyridoxamine 5’-phosphate levels from <em>S. mutans</em> FR were observed when compared to the levels from <em>S. mutans</em> UA in early log phase. However, the case was the opposite in the stationary phase. Deoxycytidine was the only discriminant metabolite in early log phase, whereas the 9 discriminant metabolites were 2,3-diphosphoglycerate, 3-keto-L-gulonate 6-phosphate, aminopropyl cadaverine, dCMP, D-ribose-5-phosphate, D-sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate, dUDP, fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, and GMP in stationary phase merely. Notably, in stationary phase, aminopropyl cadaverine and pyridoxamine 5’-phosphate were the 2 discriminant metabolites both by RP column and by HILIC column.</p>
<p id="Par31">Based on the assigned list of metabolites, pathway analysis was conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. However, since the <em>S. mutans</em> pathway was not available in the program, <em>Streptococcus pyogenes</em> M1 476 (serotype M1) was utilized for the analysis. The principal metabolic pathways associated with these compounds included vitamin B6 metabolism, purine metabolism, and pyrimidine metabolism (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>). To validate these pathways for <em>S. mutans</em>, the KEGG database was consulted. In particular, the pyrimidine metabolism pathway for <em>S. mutans</em> revealed the release of deoxycytidine during early log phase and dUDP during stationary phase, aligning with the finding where CMP was among the distinct metabolites, irrespective of the growth phase (Fig. <a href="#Fig6" class="usa-link">6</a>).</p>

<p id="Par34">After the LC-MS metabolic analysis, the PLS-DA scores plot showed clear separation between <em>S. mutans</em> UA and <em>S. mutans</em> FR, suggesting its potential for identification of biological variations that occur in <em>S. mutans</em>. In our metabolomics study, it is a practical approach to separate <em>S. mutans</em> FR from <em>S. mutans</em> UA by LC-MS. The discriminant metabolites identified with <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05 were validated with VIP (threshold set at 2.0). Successfully, 5 discriminant metabolites were identified in early log phase and 13 discriminant metabolites in stationary phase. Our results indicate that the metabolites from <em>S. mutans</em> FR may be related to the pathways of vitamin B6 metabolism (amino acid, carbohydrate, and fatty acid metabolism) and nucleotide metabolism (purine and pyrimidine metabolism).</p>
<section id="Sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Carbohydrate metabolism</h3>
<p id="Par35">Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (FBP) was identified as distinct markers from <em>S. mutans</em> in the carbohydrate metabolism pathway in this study. Acid production in the carbohydrate metabolism (the glycolysis process) is one of the virulent features of <em>S. mutans</em>. In comparison with the wild-type strains, <em>S. mutans</em> FR can produce more acid in the presence of fluoride [<a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>, <a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>, <a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>]. However, in the absence of fluoride, <em>S. mutans</em> FR acid production rates are inconsistent and can be higher [<a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>], lower [<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>], or the same [<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>]. In terms of acid tolerance, there are two controversial results. One shows <em>S. mutans</em> FR was more acid-sensitive, which could be more easily killed at a low pH value [<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>]. The other exhibits <em>S. mutans</em> FR had stronger ability to survive in acidified media than the wild-type strains [<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>].</p>
<p id="Par36">FBP can activate phosphofructokinase (PFK) and pyruvate kinase in the glycolysis process and can promote the conversion of glucose to lactate [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>]. Thus, the activity of lactate dehydrogenase in <em>Streptococcus</em> depends on the presence of FBP [<a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>]. The fructose phosphotransferase system (PTS) transporters from <em>S. mutans</em> produce fructose-1-phosphate and fructose-6-phosphate, which can be converted into FBP by PFK [<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a>].</p>
<p id="Par37">In our study, we found that the FBP levels from fluoride-resistant <em>S. mutans</em> increased in the stationary phase compared to the levels from wild-type one. FBP concentration as a sensor of carbon influx was positively correlated with the growth rate [<a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>]. FBP participates in the upstream process of glycolysis, as an important intermediate. It suggests the glycolysis of fluoride-resistant <em>S. mutans</em> might be more active than wild-type one. Further research includes the effect of FBP from fluoride-resistant strains on the glycolysis and why the acid-producing ability of fluoride-resistant strains enhances.</p></section><section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Fatty acid metabolism</h3>
<p id="Par38">Fatty acids in cell membrane play a critical role in maintaining normal physiological function of cells. When cells are subjected to some environmental stress, such as temperature, ion, salt, drug, and oxidative stress, they can rapidly change their fatty acid composition in the cell membrane, alter their morphology, or increase mobility to resist the damage caused by the external environment [<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>, <a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>]. In cariogenic bacteria, the membranous fatty acids keep the acid-base balance inside and outside the cell membrane. At pH 5, the proportion of long chain monounsaturated fatty acids in the cell membrane of <em>S. mutans</em> is increased in response to the acid-resistant stress [<a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a>]. In our previous work, we also found that the amount of long chain monounsaturated fatty acids in the cell membrane of <em>S. mutans</em> increased under acidic conditions, and that the amount of monounsaturated fatty acids in <em>S. mutans</em> FR increased to a greater extent than the parental strains with the enhanced ability of acid resistance after <em>S. mutans</em> FR induced [<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>]. In this study, we found that the FBP level of fluoride-resistant strains was higher than that of wild-type ones under the same conditions.</p>
<p id="Par39">It is widely accepted that FBP can affect the lipid metabolism [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>]. It can activate acetyl coenzyme A carboxylase (acetyl CoA), and thereby can regulate lipid synthesis in yeast [<a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>]. In addition, FBP can activate fatty acid synthetase in <em>E. coli</em>, which is probably because FBP increased the affinity of acetyl CoA in this bacterium, or because FBP promotes the stability of acetyl CoA [<a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a>].</p>
<p id="Par40">Collectively, it is likely that the increased concentration of FBP in the fluoride resistant strains promotes unsaturated fatty acid synthase activity, and thus the amount of unsaturated fatty acids in fluoride-resistant strains increases. The exact interaction between FBP and fatty acid remains unclear. Further studies are warranted.</p></section><section id="Sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolic pathway of <em>S. mutans</em>
</h3>
<p id="Par41">Currently, the database related to <em>S. mutans</em> metabolites has not yet been established. Since the study of <em>S. mutans</em> metabolomics is still at the exploratory stage, the potential biomarkers found in our study can only be speculated according to the database related to the metabolites of <em>Streptococcus pyogenes</em> M1 476 (serotype M1) (KEGG).</p>
<p id="Par42">In our study, we found that there were many other different metabolites, for instance, pyridoxamine 5′-phosphate (PMP) and UDP, between fluoride-resistant strains and their parental strains. These presumably identified metabolites are generally related to bacterial vitamin B6 metabolism (amino acid, carbohydrate, and fatty acid metabolism) and nucleotide metabolism (purine and pyrimidine metabolism). Our results are consistent with previous studies. In amino acid metabolism pathway, <em>S. mutans</em> was inhibited by PMP acting on the glucosyltransferase [<a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>]. UDP-associated metabolites [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>] were reduced in arginine-treated (dental caries preventive agent treated) <em>S. mutans</em> from the nucleotide metabolism pathway view. Although the understanding of the detailed function of these metabolites is still limited, Eva-Maria Decker and colleagues reported that the exposure of <em>S. mutans</em> to xylitol resulted in distinct gene expression patterns. Specifically, <em>GtfC</em> exhibited upregulation exclusively in the presence of xylitol. Furthermore, under xylitol exposure, the upregulation of <em>gtfB</em> was sixfold, whereas under sucrose exposure, it was threefold [<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a>]. Thus, the involved metabolic pathways of <em>S. mutans</em> FR in our study may be due to altered gene functions after the mutation of fluoride resistance.</p>
<p id="Par43">Meanwhile, it is reported that Lysine lactylation, a posttranslational modification, affects bacterial survival in altered environments and pathogenicity by regulating energy metabolism and amino acid metabolism [<a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a>]. A study on the acetylome patterns of <em>S. mutans</em> reveals that the acetyltransferase ActA acetylated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and hindered LDH’s enzymatic capacity to facilitate the transformation of pyruvate into lactic acid, consequently diminishing its cariogenicity in a rat caries model [<a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a>]. The functional role of lactylation at Lys<sup>173</sup> of RNA polymerase subunit α (RpoA) in <em>S. mutans</em> involves the regulation of exopolysaccharides synthesis in glycol-metabolic pathways [<a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">39</a>]. In dental caries, therefore, the excess fluoride use may be responsible for the metabolic reprogramming to be more favorable for <em>S. mutans</em> FR associated with its acidogenicity and acid tolerance.</p>
<p id="Par44">Systematic mapping of identified metabolites to metabolic pathways involves associating metabolites with the biochemical pathways in which they participate. By doing so, we can gain insights into the underlying biological processes that are influenced by the observed changes in metabolite concentrations. Recently, a genome-scale metabolic model for the UA159 strain, named iSMU, encompassing 675 reactions and incorporating 429 metabolites and the outputs of 493 genes, will pave the way to a comprehensive understanding of the metabolism of <em>S. mutans</em> [<a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">40</a>]. Further research will probably reveal a largely unexplored facet of metabolomes toward the comprehensive coverage.</p></section><section id="Sec12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Limitations</h3>
<p id="Par45">This study’s exclusive use of anaerobic cultivation for <em>S. mutans</em> does indeed prompt important considerations regarding the applicability of the findings to aerobic conditions in the oral environment. The metabolism of <em>S. mutans</em> can exhibit significant variations between anaerobic and aerobic environments due to differences in metabolic pathways, including available energy sources, oxygen sensitivity, and redox status. Specifically, <em>S. mutans</em> switches fermentation pathways towards oxidative phosphorylation to generate energy. This shift can lead to variations in metabolic intermediates, potentially impacting the levels of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate and other metabolites [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>]. Understanding these differences in metabolic pathways between anaerobic and aerobic conditions is crucial for comprehending the physiological adaptations of <em>S. mutans.</em> This knowledge is valuable in the context of dental caries, where variations in the oral environment can impact the metabolic strategies employed by this bacterium.</p>
<p id="Par46">Also, the single use of metabolomics technology may limit the identification of the distinguished markers between <em>S. mutans</em> UA and <em>S. mutans</em> FR. Due to a lack of a complete functional database for metabolomics, the unknown markers found in our study were not fully identified. Further research by multiple complementary analytical platforms needs to validate our results, such as transcriptomic analyses, DNA sequencing, and/or quantitative PCR.</p></section></section><section id="Sec13"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p id="Par47">In summary, the elevated FBP concentration of <em>S. mutans</em> FR indicates its upgraded ability to produce acid and its enhanced capacity to tolerate acidic environment. This discriminant metabolite marker of <em>S. mutans</em> FR may lead to an applicable strategy to explore the cariogenic and fluoride resistant mechanisms.</p></section><section id="Sec14"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Electronic supplementary material</h2>
