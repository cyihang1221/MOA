</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="Par1">Analysis of the blood proteome allows identification of proteins related to changes upon certain physiological conditions. The pathophysiology of necrotic enteritis (NE) has been extensively studied. While intestinal changes have been very well documented, data addressing NE-induced alterations in the blood proteome are scant, although these might have merit in diagnostics. In light of recent technological advancements in proteomics and pressing need for tools to access gut health, the current study employs mass-spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics to identify biomarkers for gastrointestinal health of chickens. Here, we report findings of an untargeted proteomics investigation conducted on blood plasma in chickens under NE challenge. Two MS-strategies were used for analysis: conventional data dependent acquisition coupled to standard nanoflow liquid chromatography (LC) (nano-DDA) and recently-developed data independent acquisition coupled to an Evosep One LC system (Evo-DIA). Despite superior completeness and quantification of the Evo-DIA-acquired data, high degree of agreement in identification and quantification was observed between both approaches. Additionally, we identified 15 differentially expressed proteins (shared by nano-DDA and Evo-DIA) that represent responses of animals to infection and may serve as potential biomarkers. Experimental validation through ELISA immunoassays and targeted MS for selected regulated proteins (CFD, HPS5, and MASP2) confirmed medium-to-high levels of inter-protein correlation. A GSEA analysis revealed enrichment in a number of processes related to adaptive and humoral immunity, immune activation and response in infected animals. Data are available via ProteomeXchange with identifiers PXD050461, PXD050473, and PXD061607.</p>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h3>
<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-97783-w.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Broiler chicken, DDA, DIA, Proteomics, Necrotic enteritis, Gastrointestinal health</p></section><section id="kwd-group2" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Subject terms:</strong> Biomarkers, Proteomics, Mass spectrometry, Proteomic analysis, Animal physiology, Diagnostic markers</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par27">Biomarkers have emerged as competitive and prominent alternatives to ‘gold standard’ testing methods in medical diagnostics<sup><a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>,<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a></sup>. According to the literature, a biomarker is a biological substance that can be objectively measured and analyzed to determine pharmacologic responses to therapeutic intervention, and to indicate normal physiological or pathological processes<sup><a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par28">The poultry farming industry urgently requires markers to assess health, disease, and performance<sup><a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>,<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a></sup>. Specifically, there is a growing need for markers to monitor gastrointestinal homeostasis and disease in chickens<sup><a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a></sup>. Currently, tools to evaluate intestinal health are limited to methods of macroscopic or microscopic observation, which are noted for being invasive, effortful and cumbersome in terms of costs and time. Recent studies have focused on the identification of intestinal or faecal protein biomarkers, having successfully identified proteins like ovotransferrin, as a valuable gut leakage marker in necrotic enteritis and coccidiosis models, with potential use in field applications<sup><a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>–<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a></sup>. Since cellular morphogenesis and tissue organization are intrinsically dependent on protein function, alterations in protein levels or localization in this type of samples can easily be informative of health status, disease onset or progression. However, protein stability issues arise when working with faecal or intestinal samples, due to interference with microbial proteases<sup><a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>,<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a></sup>. Blood biomarker research represents an interesting alternative that offers a much higher sample stability. In conventional veterinary medicine, blood analysis has been a canonical method of diagnostics, prognostics and health measurement<sup><a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>–<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a></sup>. Compared to human medicine, untargeted discovery of novel blood-based biomarkers in veterinary research remains at its dawn, although the overall engagement of so-called proteomic technologies in the field is increasing<sup><a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>,<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a></sup>. Several approaches exist, with mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics evolving as a standard technology for untargeted protein biomarker discovery.</p>
<p id="Par29">However, plasma has been a notoriously difficult sample type to handle, due to the wide concentration range of proteins, spanning 11 orders of magnitude in humans<sup><a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a></sup>, which limits the detection of lower abundant proteins and limits the number of identifications to hundreds. Furthermore, the blood protein levels can significantly vary between species<sup><a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a></sup>; in birds, it oscillates around 40 g/L compared to the 50–70 g/L range in mammals<sup><a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>,<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a></sup>. Recent years have seen significant biomarker discoveries in human plasma, attributed to technological advancements and improved study designs. However, similar progress in the veterinary field, especially within poultry science, remains limited<sup><a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a></sup>. Despite an increase in proteomics studies on other farm animal species, only a handful of MS-based studies have been conducted in poultry science, covering a broad spectrum of topics. Among these are descriptions of molecular features of bacterial chondronecrosis with osteomyelitis in chicken<sup><a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a></sup>, identification of biomarker candidates of bacterial enteritis<sup><a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a></sup>, exploration of susceptibility to coccidiosis<sup><a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a></sup> or response to infections<sup><a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>,<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par30">The present study employs comprehensive shotgun proteomics analyses to explore the blood plasma of chickens affected with necrotic enteritis (NE).</p>
<p id="Par31">The bottom-up MS-based proteomics workflow involves denaturing, reducing, alkylating, and digesting proteins into peptides for further analysis by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). A well-established method for spectral data recording is <strong>Data Dependent Acquisition (DDA)</strong>, in which the most intense signal precursors are prioritized for fragmentation<sup><a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>,<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a></sup>. The inherent stochasticity of DDA data collection can compromise reproducibility and data richness, often resulting in important data gaps, in particular from low abundant peptides<sup><a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>,<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par32">An evolved alternative to DDA is <strong>data-independent acquisition (DIA)</strong>, which improves on data reproducibility and quantification precision<sup><a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>,<a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a></sup>. In DIA, the precursor selection bias is quenched due to the combined fragmentation of all precursors that fall within predetermined mass-to-charge (m/z) isolation windows that sequentially cover the whole mass range (hence independent of the data). In recent years, advanced algorithms based on machine learning such as DIA-NN<sup><a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a></sup> evolved to handle very efficiently the complexity of DIA-generated complex spectra. Furthermore, quantification is very robust since it is done at the MS2 level, where dynamic range within spectra intensities is lower, improving on the limitations of MS1-level quantification typically used in DDA analysis<sup><a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par33">Historically, the vast majority of disease plasma biomarkers has consisted of lowly abundant molecules<sup><a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a></sup>. This is because physiological and immunological responses to disease-inducing agents are quite moderate, in particular at early stage<sup><a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a></sup>. For this reason, the field of proteomics is now transitioning to DIA as the method of choice for biomarker discovery research, given its improved reproducibility and data completeness<sup><a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>,<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par34">The present study aims to leverage both DDA and DIA for the discovery of poultry gastrointestinal health plasma biomarkers. In addition, we coupled DIA to a recent Evosep One LC system designed for high-throughput studies<sup><a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a></sup> (Evo-DIA), while DDA was coupled to separation on a standard nanoflow LC system (nano-DDA). Both approaches were used to analyze the plasma proteome of healthy broilers and broilers developing necrotic enteritis after infection with <em>C. perfringens</em>, a model for severe intestinal damage and inflammation. We investigated the unique strengths of each workflow to enhance the robustness and comprehensiveness of biomarker discovery and, thus to provide a more complete proteomic profile. The experimental setup of the study is detailed in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>.</p>

<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Challenged birds develop severe necrotic enteritis</h3>
<p id="Par36">In order to study alterations in the chicken blood proteome upon severe gut health impairment, we worked with a necrotic enteritis (NE) in vivo model. During the experiment, two birds from the challenge group died prior to euthanasia and were excluded from further analysis. Results of macroscopic lesion scoring showed that the majority of birds developed severe form of NE (lesion scores 5 and 6 corresponding to 29.5% and 24.4% of all birds, respectively), while an equally high number of animals suffered from a mild NE state (lesion scores 3 and 4, i.e., 9% and 28.2%, respectively). The results are presented in more detail in Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>.</p>

<p id="Par38">Plasma samples from broiler chickens with the most severe form of NE (i.e. lesion score 5–6, <em>n</em> = 17) and from healthy birds (non-challenged, <em>n</em> = 9) were selected for proteomics LC-MS/MS analysis and processed by both standard data-dependent (DDA) and emerging data-independent (DIA) acquisition modes; the recorded spectral data was analyzed by the MaxQuant and DIA-NN software, respectively.</p>
<p id="Par39">Besides the difference in spectral acquisition and data analysis, also the LC conditions differed between both workflows. While in the DDA workflow peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using a near 3-hour run on a standard nanoLC system (nano-DDA), for DIA a faster 1.5-hour method (15 samples per day (SPD)) was applied using an Evosep One LC system (Evo-DIA) (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>).</p>
<p id="Par40">To evaluate the performance of each workflow we first looked at the number of peptides and proteins identified over all samples. In total, the nano-DDA and Evo-DIA workflows led to the identification of 576 and 447 proteins, respectively. When only considering proteins found in at least 2 samples, these numbers reduced to 318 and 362 (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>A). Almost half of these proteins (46.8%) was shared between both workflows, supporting their high confidence of identification (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>B). In terms of peptide sequences, there were 7.469 and 5.221 identifications for the DDA and the DIA workflow, respectively (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>C). Again, roughly half of these sequences (52.9%) was shared between both workflows (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>D). Interestingly, both approaches resulted in higher numbers of identified peptides and proteins in the NE-samples compared to the control samples, hinting towards a potential difference in protein content between both biological conditions.</p>

Similarly, heatmap visualization of all protein intensities indicated lower numbers of missing values in Evo-DIA compared to nano-DDA, especially for lower-intensity proteins in control samples (greyer area at the bottom, leftmost part of the heatmap) (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>F). However, the data for the proteins of high intensity was more complete in the nano-DDA approach (less greyer area at the top of the heatmap). Although in both datasets the majority of missing information was found among the least abundant proteins, the dispersion of missing data was more even in Evo-DIA, reflecting the systematic sampling of peptides inherent to the DIA, in contrast to the stochastic selection of peptides performed in DDA mode<sup><a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a></sup>. Taken together, despite shorter LC-MS/MS runs and lower number of initial identifications, the Evo-DIA workflow yielded a higher number of quantified proteins and greater data completeness compared to the nano-DDA workflow. In turn, due to a significantly longer LC-MS/MS analysis, the nano-DDA output yielded higher number of quantifications, especially on precursor peptide level, thus providing more complete data for the most abundant proteins.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Necrotic enteritis leads to significant changes in the plasma proteome</h3>
<p id="Par43">We then took a closer look at the plasma proteome changes induced by NE. To this end, we first compared the precision in quantification for both workflows using the intensity values of 175 proteins that were quantified in both nano-DDA and Evo-DIA. Overall, similar coefficients of variation (%CV) were observed for both workflows, with median values fluctuating around 40 in each experimental condition for both nano-DDA and Evo-DIA (Ctrl<sub>nano−DDA</sub>= 42.1, Ctrl<sub>Evo−DIA</sub> = 39; NE<sub>nano−DDA</sub> = 39, NE<sub>Evo−DIA</sub> =41.4) (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>A). The relatively high %CV values observed likely represent a combination of a substantial degree of biological variation among individual animals, similar to what has been reported for humans<sup><a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a></sup>, with technical factors inherent to the tested methods. Medians of technical variation, estimated at 13.2 and 7.4 for nano-DDA and Evo-DIA, respectively (Supplementary Figure <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S1</a> online) were determined from separate repeated injections of selected samples. In our study, since every animal was considered as an independent statistical unit, we hypothesize that inter-individual differences in response to NE, despite decreased genetic variation in purebred broiler lines<sup><a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a></sup>, are likely responsible for this observation suggesting that individual variability should be considered in studies on NE in broilers. This indicates comparable precision in quantification of common proteins provided by both methods.</p>

The GSEA analysis showed that necrotic enteritis induces significant alterations in fundamental processes linked to immunity, such as humoral and classical immune response activation and regulation (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>). Categories additionally identified using nano-DDA predominantly highlight the involvement of peptidase (upregulated) and hydrolase (downregulated) activities. In the case of Evo-DIA, additional upregulated terms included cell killing, response to other organisms and interspecies interaction processes, while downregulated categories comprised system development, morphogenesis and extracellular matrix processes.</p>

Upregulation of potential NE biomarker proteins could be validated by immunoassay, opening perspectives for future monitoring of gut health in broiler chicken.</p></section></section><section id="Sec8"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p id="Par59">In broilers chickens, maintaining a balanced gastrointestinal function is a critical factor for overall health. In an industrial setting, this equilibrium is directly related to performance and productivity. Considering the importance of the balanced functioning of the gastrointestinal tract, minor-to-severe disturbances into the structural integrity of the gut elicit negative effects on the broilers’ health, ultimately leading to significant economic losses<sup><a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>,<a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">40</a></sup>. Many research studies highlighted the necessity for alternative tools/methods to monitor and evaluate the health state in broilers<sup><a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>–<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a></sup>. The present study fills this gap by MS-based analysis of the broiler chickens plasma proteome, resulting in the discovery of potential biomarker proteins for the gut health evaluation.</p>
<p id="Par60">The first objective of our study was to compare the performance of two MS-based approaches, nano-DDA and Evo-DIA, by analyzing the total number of peptide and protein identifications. Despite a high overlap on both levels (52.9% for peptide sequences, 46.8% for protein IDs), slightly higher absolute numbers of identified peptides and proteins were detected with nano-DDA before applying the filtering criterion that requires proteins to be found in at least two samples. This is remarkable considering the stochastic nature of data acquisition by this method. However, the difference in LC run lengths between both methods, 3 h for nano-DDA and 1.5 h for Evo-DIA, is most likely responsible for this effect, as reported previously<sup><a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>,<a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">41</a></sup>. Importantly, the reproducibility between samples was considerably higher in the Evo-DIA workflow. This is in line with previous studies performed using human sample material where a higher amount of missing values was present in DDA<sup><a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">42</a>,<a href="#CR43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR43">43</a></sup>. Indeed, with DIA near-complete quantitative data is achieved by the method of precursor sampling, within regular timeframes, which inevitably leads to recording the data on peptide fragment ions in a more systematic way<sup><a href="#CR44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR44">44</a>,<a href="#CR45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR45">45</a></sup>. Thus, our results highlight the superior performance of Evo-DIA over nano-DDA to quantitatively and reproducibly profile the chicken plasma proteome. It is important to note that both approaches might be limited by the quality and completeness of available proteomic data for chicken, which could substantially affect the accuracy of the results. Sequencing and annotation of the complete chicken genome is an ongoing effort, hence, the database resources are currently relatively short in information. Certainly, the number of chicken blood plasma proteins that can be detected at high confidence will rise once a complete annotation of the chicken genome is available<sup><a href="#CR46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR46">46</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par61">The second objective of this study was to compare the plasma proteome of chicken with a normal versus dysregulated gastrointestinal health caused by necrotic enteritis (NE), an infection model induced by toxin-producing <em>C. perfringens</em>. As a first indication of major differences between both groups, we observed overall higher protein amounts in each sample of the NE group, with both approaches, accompanied by slightly higher rate of intensity variation (CV) in Evo-DIA method, compared to nano-DDA.</p>
<p id="Par62">Minor differences between both workflows potentially could be induced by intrinsic differences in sampling and acquisition of the MS spectral data. While DDA is prone to capturing the most abundant peptides possibly leading to less variability in the detected proteins, systematic scanning in DIA is likely to include peptides with more variable abundance, thus, introducing more variability. Comprehensive sampling by DIA has likely resulted in detection of low-abundance proteins that are known to be more variable in expression, as well as capture wider dynamic range of protein abundances<sup><a href="#CR47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR47">47</a>,<a href="#CR48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR48">48</a></sup>. These findings suggest that a more sensitive and comprehensive picture of the proteomic changes upon disease condition can be provided by DIA, specifically in capturing proteins with high variability. This adds to the superior quantitative performance of this acquisition method compared to DDA. It should be stated, however, that the disease did not preferentially affect the performance of one method over the other, as observed by lack of significant differences in the %CV values between the nano-DDA and Evo-DIA approaches for both healthy and NE conditions. Given that assessment of the technical variation occurred independently of the original experiment, these values should be interpreted with caution.</p>
<p id="Par63">The high variation in protein intensities is both control and NE groups likely reflects substantial intra-individual biological differences. In the NE group, bird-specific responses to the infectious agent, including variable leakage of tissue proteins in response to increased gut permeability, might be responsible for outliers with extra high variation. Importantly, triple measurements repeated for selected samples (<em>n</em> = 3) separately from the original experiment indicated that technical variation was low with %CV values of 13.2 and 7.4 for the nano-DDA and Evo-DIA approach, respectively, and confirmed that the contribution of technical variation to the overall variation (35.4% for nano-DDA and 13.7% for Evo-DIA) was lower compared to the biological variation (64.6% for nano-DDA and 86.3% for Evo-DIA) (Supplementary Figure <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">S1</a> online). Overall, our findings compare well with the results reported by Nagaraj and Mann where inter- and intrapersonal variation contributed substantially (47.1% and 45%, respectively) to the total variability of the human proteome, unlike technical variation (8%)<sup>11</sup>. It was also concluded that high CV values do not necessarily detract from the relevance of these proteins as biomarkers suggesting that high protein variability in healthy conditions may still be informative if they exhibit consistent patterns of upregulation or downregulation in disease states. Moreover, the absence of a strong association between the abundance and the variability of quantitation further suggested that observed variations capture physiological aspects mainly<sup><a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a></sup>. Presumably, the sample matrix composition, i.e. the matrix effect, can negatively contribute to the ionization and detection of peptides<sup><a href="#CR49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR49">49</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par64">Our results suggest that, similar to human studies, standardized and meticulous sample collection methods should be applied for animal plasma profiling studies in order to diminish technical variation between samples. Such methods were applied in the present study as described in the Materials and Methods section and in Supplementary Note.</p>
<p id="Par65">High biological variability is a well-documented challenge in proteomics, particularly when analyzing complex biological fluids like blood. Similar levels of variability have been observed in human plasma proteomics, with CVs often reaching 40–50%, particularly in disease conditions<sup><a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>,<a href="#CR47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR47">47</a></sup>. Adding to this, studies such as Zhou et al. (2012)<sup><a href="#CR50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR50">50</a></sup> and Brunner et al. (2021)<sup><a href="#CR51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR51">51</a></sup> emphasized the importance of focusing on selected groups of proteins (e.g. 70% least variant) to manage variability. Zhou and colleagues worked with the least variable proteins to enhance biomarker studies, and in the study of Brunner a ‘core-proteome’ subset showed consistent expression despite perturbations. In the latter study, it was reported that biological variation, particularly across different cell cycle states, dominates over remaining technical variation, suggesting that despite minimal technical variation, biological differences can still contribute significantly to variability. Another label-free proteomics study demonstrated that pooling complex biological samples like cerebrospinal fluid samples significantly reduced biological variation<sup><a href="#CR52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR52">52</a></sup>. This approach allowed to achieve very low CV values (under 5%) for 81 proteins. Although the effectiveness of pooling to minimize variability this approach is however not feasible in all biological contexts. Our present findings align with those in human studies, emphasizing that robust experimental design and careful consideration of technical and biological variability are crucial for reliable plasma proteomics, both in human and animal studies. Most importantly, the blood proteome of <em>C. perfringens</em>-infected birds was characterized by presence of proteins related to immune response and immune regulation. GSEA revealed enrichment of 15 biological process terms shared between both the nano-DDA and Evo-DIA datasets, of which the majority represents up-regulated processes of innate immune and humoral responses, immune regulation, activation of complement pathways and adaptive immune responses. Alteration of the birds’ immune status due to <em>C. perfringens</em> infection has been well documented earlier<sup><a href="#CR53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR53">53</a>,<a href="#CR54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR54">54</a></sup>. Moreover, robust innate and humoral immune responses in affected birds was marked by activation of the complement pathway that aids in elimination of foreign microorganisms and necrotic cells. In chickens, so far, complement activation via the alternative pathway was suggested as a host response to virus infection, inhibiting virus growth and cytopathic effects<sup><a href="#CR55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR55">55</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par66">The importance of an acute phase reaction in the course of NE in chicken was earlier investigated by others and is also reflected in our findings<sup><a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>,<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>,<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>,<a href="#CR56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR56">56</a></sup>. Proteins linked to an acute phase reaction were top-ranked in our GSEA gene lists. Although not reported as a separate category, top-ranked proteins like ogchi, CP, A2ML4, HPS5, EXFAB, MASP2, CFD and, CCL26 are engaged in stress and defense responses and are constituents of the chain of acute phase reactions<sup><a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>,<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par67">Processes downregulated in the GSEA were related to extracellular matrix structure, encapsulation, organ/bone morphogenesis and hydrolase activity. Downregulation of these processes in the course of NE could be primarily induced by the action of toxins and enzymes secreted by the pathogen as virulence factors. Toxins produced by <em>C. perfringens</em> such as CPB (beta toxin) and <em>netB</em> (Necrotic Enteritis Like Toxin) mediate the formation of pores in the cell membrane, leading to an uncontrolled intercellular exchange of ions and, therefore, altering cellular homeostasis, regenerative processes, resulting in apoptosis<sup><a href="#CR57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR57">57</a>–<a href="#CR59" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR59">59</a></sup>. Similarly, dysregulation of hydrolytic processes might lead to adverse effects on enterocytes and the small intestine epithelium, contributing to the disease pathology<sup><a href="#CR60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR60">60</a></sup>. Overall, GSEA showed a high degree of similarity between nano-DDA and Evo-DIA results, suggesting that both methods are equally effective and reliable for identifying key biological processes and pathways important in the course of disease.</p>
<p id="Par68">Finally, we present double validation for selected proteins that were identified as potential markers for gut health in chickens- HPS5, MASP2 and CFD. These proteins were selected since they are involved in acute phase defense (HPS5)<sup><a href="#CR56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR56">56</a></sup> and immune responses (CFD, MASP2)<sup><a href="#CR61" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR61">61</a>,<a href="#CR62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR62">62</a></sup> and were found significantly upregulated in NE animals. A targeted MS-based PRM assay<sup><a href="#CR63" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR63">63</a>,<a href="#CR64" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR64">64</a></sup> enabled precise and sensitive quantification, peptide expression levels were consistent with those obtained by nano-DDA and Evo-DIA.</p>
<p id="Par69">Additionally, targeted antibody-based ELISA tests, compatible with routine veterinary practice<sup><a href="#CR65" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR65">65</a></sup>, were used to monitor the levels of HPS5, MASP2 and CFD. Consequently, the results of these validation measurements strongly support the application of MS-based proteomics for the discovery of gut health biomarker proteins, besides validating our proteomics results. Further research on the regulated reported proteins could provide crucial insights into the molecular processes underlying disease of necrotic enteritis, and aid in developing better strategies for diagnosis and prevention, next to the identification of gut health biomarkers as a major goal of the present study.</p></section><section id="Sec9"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Experimental design: in vivo necrotic enteritis model</h3>
<p id="Par70">In the current study, the NE model previously described by Gholamiandehkordi et al. (2007) was used, with some modifications<sup><a href="#CR66" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR66">66</a></sup>. Briefly, one-day-old non-vaccinated Ross 308 broiler chicks were purchased from a local hatchery and randomly allocated into challenged (NE) and non-challenged (Ctrl) groups with 80 and 20 animals in each, respectively. All birds were provided with <em>ad libitum</em> access to feed and water. Until day 17 of the experiment, all birds received a wheat/rye-based (43%/7.5%) diet with soybean meal as protein source (30%). Afterwards, the source of protein in the diet was replaced with fishmeal (30%) in challenged group. This type of diet composition predisposes broilers to the development of NE. It contains high level of poorly digestible proteins and non–starch polysaccharides that lead to digestibility losses. On days 14 and 16 birds, from the NE group where orally treated with 10-fold dose of live attenuated <em>Eimeria</em> vaccine Paracox-5 (containing <em>E. acervulina</em> HP, <em>E. maxima</em> CP, <em>E. maxima</em> MFP, <em>E. mitis</em> HP, <em>E. tenella</em> HP) (MSD Animal Health, Brussels, Belgium). The <em>netB</em> positive <em>Clostridium perfringens</em> type G strain CP56 was cultured in BHI broth (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, US) overnight at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions. Consecutively, broilers from the treatment group received an oral dose of approximately 5 × 10<sup>8</sup> CFU of pathogenic <em>C. perfringens</em> strain CP56 on days 18 and 19. All animals were euthanized on day 20 of the experiment by injecting intravenously an overdose of barbiturate (sodium pentobarbital 20% (KELA Laboratoria nv, Hoogstraten, Belgium)). Prior to euthanasia, 3 ml blood was withdrawn from a jugular vein and collected into EDTA-treated vacutainers for further separation of plasma (1900 rcf during 10 min at room temperature, with no brake in the swinging bucket centrifuge). The precision of biomarker identification in proteomics research is conditioned by the quality of the samples<sup><a href="#CR67" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR67">67</a></sup>. Consequently, blood collection procedures were meticulously executed to minimize any potential variations in handling, ensuring a robust foundation for accurate analyses and meaningful insights in the pursuit of biomarker discovery. Vacutainers with blood were inverted five times prior to centrifugation; after, a portion of plasma was carefully pipetted into a new tube, preventing contact between the pipette tip and ‘buffy coat’-part of the extract thus collection of the bottom 2–5 mm layer of plasma was omitted. Each sample of plasma was aliquoted, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at – 80 °C for further analysis. Applied standard operation procedure (SOP) for blood collection is described in detail in the Supplementary Note.</p>
<p id="Par71">For evaluating severity of NE lesions, the macroscopic scoring of small mid-intestinal segments (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) of all birds was performed according to scoring system established by Keyburn et al. (2006)<sup><a href="#CR68" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR68">68</a></sup>. Each animal was given an NE lesion score which was calculated as the average of the scores estimated for evaluated middle parts of intestinal segments. Birds with a lesion score of 2 and higher were classified as NE-positive. Statistical analysis of performance parameters and macroscopic scoring was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.4.3, San Diego, USA).</p>
<p id="Par72">All the experiments and manipulations involving animals were approved by the ethical committee of the Faculties of Veterinary Medicine and Bioscience Engineering of Ghent University (EC2020-045). All experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the Ethical Committee; this study complied with ARRIVE guidelines<sup><a href="#CR69" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR69">69</a></sup>.</p></section><section id="Sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Proteomics sample preparation</h3>
<p id="Par73">The proteomics analysis was performed on plasma samples obtained from birds with the most severe NE lesion score (score 5–6, <em>n</em> = 17) and birds that received no treatment (score 0, <em>n</em> = 9). Sample sizes were determined using G*Power software (ver. 3.1.9.2, Kiel, Germany) with applied parameters of α = 0.05 and power = 95%. Of each sample 1 µL plasma was added to 50 µL S-trap buffer containing 5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB), pH 7.55. Proteins were reduced by addition of 15 mM dithiothreitol and incubation for 30 min at 55˚C and then alkylated by addition of 30 mM iodoacetamide and incubation for 15 min at room temperature (later RT) in the dark. Phosphoric acid was added to a final concentration of 1.2% and subsequently samples were diluted 7-fold with binding buffer containing 90% methanol in 100 mM TEAB, pH 7.1. The samples were loaded on the 96-well S-Trap™ plate (Protifi), placed on top of a deepwell plate, and centrifuged for 2 min at 1,500 x g at RT. After protein binding, the S-trap™ plate was washed three times by adding 200 µL binding buffer and centrifugation for 2 min at 1,500 x g at RT. A new deepwell receiver plate was placed below the 96-well S-Trap™ plate and 50 mM TEAB containing trypsin (1/100, w/w) was added for digestion overnight at 37 °C. By centrifugation for 2 min at 1,500 x g, peptides were eluted in three times, first with 80 µL 50 mM TEAB, then with 80 µL 0.2% formic acid (FA) in water and finally with 80 µL 0.2% FA in water/acetonitrile (ACN) (50/50, v/v). Eluted peptides were dried completely by vacuum centrifugation and stored at − 20 °C until further use. For the analysis of technical replicates, randomly selected blood plasma samples (<em>n</em> = 3) corresponding to Ctrl group were divided into three independent samples each and measured individually. Sample preparation and the following steps were carried out separately.</p></section><section id="Sec12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LC-MS/MS analysis</h3>
<p id="Par74">For nano-DDA analysis, purified peptides of all 26 samples were re-dissolved in 20 µL loading solvent A (0.1% TFA in water/ACN (98:2, v/v) and the peptide concentration was determined on a Lunatic spectrophotometer (Unchained Labs)<sup><a href="#CR70" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR70">70</a></sup>. From each sample, 2 µg was injected for LC-MS/MS analysis on an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system in-line connected to a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer equipped with a Nanospray Flex™ Ion Source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Trapping was performed at 10 µL/min for 4 min in loading solvent A on a 20 mm trapping column (made in-house, 100 μm internal diameter (I.D.), 5 μm beads, C18 Reprosil-HD, Dr. Maisch, Germany). The peptides were separated on a 250 mm nanoEase column (M/Z HSS T3 column, 100 Å, 1.8 μm, 75 μm I.D., Waters) kept at a constant of 45 °C. Peptides were eluted by a non-linear increase from 5 to 55% MS solvent B (0.1% FA in water/ACN (2:8, v/v)) over 145 min, at a flow rate of 300 nL/min, followed by a 10-minutes wash reaching 99% MS solvent B and re-equilibration with 95% MS solvent A (0.1% FA in water). The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent mode, automatically switching between MS and MS/MS acquisition for the 12 most abundant ion peaks per MS spectrum. Full-scan MS spectra (375-1,500 m/z) were acquired at a resolution of 60,000 at 200 m/z in the Orbitrap analyser after accumulation to a target value of 3,000,000. The 12 most intense ions above a threshold value of 13,000 (minimum AGC of 1,000) were isolated for fragmentation at a normalized collision energy of 28%. The C-trap was filled at a target value of 100,000 for maximum 80 ms and the MS/MS spectra (200-2,000 m/z) were acquired at a resolution of 15,000 at 200 m/z in the Orbitrap analyzer with a fixed first mass of 145 m/z. Only peptides with charge states ranging from + 2 to + 6 were included for fragmentation and the dynamic exclusion was set to 20 s. QCloud was used to control instrument longitudinal performance during the project<sup><a href="#CR71" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR71">71</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par75">For Evo-DIA analysis, from each of the 26 samples redissolved in loading solvent A as described above, the peptide concentration was adjusted to 0.015 µg/µL with 0.1% FA. iRT peptides (Biognosys, P/N Ki-3002-1) were added to each sample according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, 300 ng of each sample was loaded on an Evotip (Evosep, P/N EV2003) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All loaded Evotips were stored in 0.1% FA at 4 °C until LC-MS/MS analysis could be started. Samples were run in data-independent mode on an Evosep One LC-system (Evosep, Denmark) in-line connected to a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were analyzed with the 15 SPD method (88 min run length) using the endurance Evosep column (15 cm x 150 μm I.D., 1.9 μm beads, Evosep, Denmark). For elution of the peptides from the column solvent A consisted of 0.1% FA in LC-MS-grade water and solvent B consisted of 0.1% FA in ACN. Full-scan MS spectra ranging from 375 to 1500 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> with an AGC target value of 5E6, a maximum fill time of 50 ms and a resolution of 60,000 at 200 m/z were followed by 30 quadrupole isolations with a precursor isolation width of 10 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> for HCD fragmentation at an NCE of 30% after filling the trap at a target value of 3E6 for maximum injection time of 45 ms. MS2 spectra were acquired at a resolution of 15,000 at 200 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> in the Orbitrap analyser without multiplexing. The isolation intervals ranging from 400 to 900 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> were created with the Skyline software tool. QCloud was used to control instrument longitudinal performance during the project<sup><a href="#CR71" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR71">71</a></sup>.</p></section><section id="Sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Proteomics data analysis</h3>
<p id="Par76">Raw files corresponding to the 26 nano-DDA runs were searched together using the MaxQuant software<sup><a href="#CR72" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR72">72</a></sup> (version 2.0.3.0) with mainly default search settings, including a false discovery rate (FDR) set at 1% on peptide and protein level. Recorded spectra were searched against the <em>Gallus gallus</em> proteins present in the UniProt database (reference proteome UP000000539 containing 18,112 sequences, downloaded from <a href="http://www.uniprot.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.uniprot.org</a>, release from June 2022). The mass tolerance for precursor and fragment ions was set to 4.5 and 20 ppm, respectively, during the main search. Enzyme specificity was set as C-terminal to arginine and lysine, also allowing cleavage at proline bonds with a maximum of two missed cleavages. Variable modifications were set to oxidation of methionine residues and acetylation of protein N-termini whereas carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues was set as fixed modification. Matching between runs was enabled with a matching time window of 0.7 min and an alignment time window of 20 min. Only proteins with at least one unique or razor peptide were retained. Proteins were quantified by the MaxLFQ algorithm integrated in the MaxQuant software. A minimum ratio count of two unique or razor peptides was required for quantification.</p>
<p id="Par77">Raw files corresponding to the 26 Evo-DIA runs were searched together using the DIA-NN software<sup><a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a></sup> (version 1.8.1). Mainly default settings were enabled, in combination with a library-free search with selected FASTA digest using the same protein database as described for nano-DDA. Trypsin was selected as the digestion enzyme (allowing cleavage N-terminally at proline and allowing up to two missed cleavages), and carbamidomethylation of cysteines, oxidation of methionine residues and acetylation of N-termini were set as variable modifications, with maximum number of variable modifications set to five. The maximum number of trypsin-missed cleavages was set to two. Precursor mass range filter was set at 400–900 m/z, the match between runs (MBR) option which was enabled, and “mass accuracy“ (MS2 mass accuracy) was set at 20 ppm and “MS1 accuracy“ (MS1 mass accuracy) at 10 ppm.</p>
<p id="Par78">Further statistical analysis was performed using the RStudio software<sup><a href="#CR73" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR73">73</a></sup> (version 4.1.1). Peptide expression matrices were prepared as follows: for nano-DDA, the MaxQuant peptides output table was used after removal of reverse database hits; for Evo-DIA, the DIA-NN main report output table was filtered at a precursor and protein library q-value cut-off of 1% and only proteins with at least one proteotypic peptide were retained. Protein expression matrices were prepared as follows: for nano-DDA, the MaxQuant proteinGroups output table was used after removal of reverse database hits and hits only identified by site; for Evo-DIA, the DIA-NN main report output table was filtered at a precursor and protein library q-value cut-off of 1% and only proteins with at least one proteotypic peptide were retained. Reported peptide and protein ID rates took into account features detected in at least two samples. For differential abundance analysis, MaxLFQ-normalized protein intensities were log<sub>2</sub> transformed and proteins with less than four valid values in at least one experimental condition were removed, leading to a list of 295 and of 344 quantified proteins in the experiment for nano-DDA and Evo-DIA, respectively. Missing values were imputed by random sampling from a normal distribution centered around the noise level (package DEP)<sup><a href="#CR74" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR74">74</a></sup>. Pairwise comparison testing between necrotic enteritis (NE) and control (Ctrl) groups was then carried out using the limma package<sup><a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">39</a></sup>, with statistical significance for differential regulation set at an FDR cut-off value of 0.05 and a |log<sub>2</sub>(fold change)| above 1.</p></section><section id="Sec14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Functional gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)</h3>
<p id="Par79">Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to identify gene ontology (GO) terms associated with proteomic changes upon NE was performed using software GSEA (version 4.2.3)<sup><a href="#CR75" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR75">75</a></sup>. Gene names corresponding to quantified proteins were ranked based on <em>t</em>-test metric and used as input data. A significance threshold cut-off of FDR <em>q</em>-value ≤ 0.05 was applied for both positive and negative enrichment hits. Queries for nano-DDA and Evo-DIA were performed separately, with a total of 248 proteins out of the 295 quantified getting annotated (and thus included in the analysis) for nano-DDA and 272 out of the 344 quantified getting annotated for Evo-DIA.</p></section><section id="Sec15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Protein detection by enzymelinked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)</h3>
<p id="Par80">To evaluate the analytical performance of MS in comparison to immunoassays and to examine the potential candidate biomarkers for chicken gut health in plasma samples from infected and non-infected birds, three commercially available ELISA kits were performed. The immunoassay kits for serum amyloid protein (HPS5), complement factor D (CFD) and Mannan Associated Serine Protease 2 (MASP2) were obtained from Abbexa LTD (abx257741,Abbexa LTD, Cambridge, UK) and MyBioSource (CFD, MBS2503729; MASP2, MBS2506173, MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA), respectively. The assays were executed according to the instructions of manufacturers, applying dilution factor of 1:1000 for HPS5, 1:7 for CFD, and 1:2 for MASP2, testing each individual sample in duplicate. Statistical analysis of immunoassay results was performed in GraphPad Prism (version 8.4.3, San Diego, USA) software using non-parametric testing considering the size and type of distribution of each group. Differences between NE and Ctrl groups were assessed by Mann-Whitney test; correlation between tested proteins was assessed by a Spearman rank test. Correlation coefficients between the two MS-methods and immunoassays were analyzed by Spearman rank test.</p></section><section id="Sec16"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Protein detection by parallel reaction monitoring (PRM)</h3>
<p id="Par81">The target peptides for PRM were selected from identified precursor lists of the above acquired data with nanoLC-DDA and Evo-DIA methods. More in particular three peptides per target protein biomarker CFD, HPS5, and MASP2 were manually selected using Skyline-daily software<sup><a href="#CR76" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR76">76</a></sup> (ver. 24.1.1.398) based on proteotypicity towards the chicken database present in the UniProt database (reference proteome UP000000539 containing 18,112 sequences, downloaded from <a href="http://www.uniprot.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.uniprot.org</a>, release from June 2022), signal intensity, absence of missed cleavages and modifications. Several additional target peptides of APOAB (as a non-regulated housekeeping protein) were included in the analysis for the downstream normalization across samples. Heavy isotopically labeled target peptides were purchased from JPT Peptide Technologies GmbH (Berlin, Germany). Target peptides developed for PRM assay are reported in Supplementary Table <a href="#MOESM5" class="usa-link">S5</a>.</p>
<p id="Par82">PRM acquisition method development was performed in several steps. First, 100 fmol of a pool of synthetic target peptides was loaded onto the LC-column in unscheduled PRM mode to determine the retention time intervals. A scheduled PRM method with 4 min time intervals was created through Skyline-daily (ver. 24.1.1.398) and evaluated on the same pool of synthetic peptides. Next, a pooled plasma sample obtained from 27 experimental animals was spiked in with 100 fmol, 10 fmol, 1 fmol peptide amounts to evaluate the scheduled PRM method next to estimate the spike-I amount of each peptide. Through Skyline, the windows were manually adjusted where needed and the optimal spike-in amount for each peptide was determined. The adjusted scheduled PRM schema was used in the final plasma sample PRM runs spiked in with the optimal peptide amount.</p>
<p id="Par83">For the actual PRM runs, plasma samples were prepared for proteome analysis as described above and the resulting peptides were redissolved in 20 µl of loading buffer A (0.05/95.5 ACN/H2O 0.1% TFA). A total of 11 µl of the samples was injected for LC-MS/MS analysis on an Ultimate 3000 Pro Flow nanoLC system in-line connected to a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Trapping was performed at 20 µl/min for 2 min in loading solvent A on a 5 mm trapping column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 300 μm internal diameter (I.D.), 5 μm beads). The peptides were separated on a 250 mm Aurora Ultimate, 1.7 μm C18, 75 μm inner diameter (IonOpticks) kept at a constant temperature of 45 °C in a butterfly oven (Phoenix S&amp;T). Peptides were eluted by a non-linear gradient starting at 0.5% MS solvent B reaching 10% MS solvent B (0.1% FA in acetonitrile) in 38 min, 26.4% MS solvent B (0.1% FA in acetonitrile) in 67 min, 44% in 68 min and 56% MS solvent B in 70 min followed by a 5-minute wash at 56% MS solvent B and re-equilibration with MS solvent A (0.1% FA in water) at a constant flowrate of 250 nl/min.</p>
<p id="Par84">The mass spectrometer was operated in PRM mode, automatically switching between MS and PRM acquisition. Full-scan MS spectra (150–1500 m/z) were acquired at a resolution of 60,000 in the Orbitrap analyzer after accumulation to a target value of 3E6 and 40ms maximum ion time. The PRM method included HCD fragmentation of the ions of interest listed in Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a> with a 1.5 m/z isolation window with a loop count of 1. MS/MS spectra were acquired at 30,000 resolution with maximum injection time of 100ms and an AGC target of 5E5 ions.</p>
<p id="Par85">Following manual evaluation of the recorded transitions in Skyline, the ratio of light to heavy peptide peak areas was determined for each target peptide using the peak areas of their shared transitions, calculated as (ΣLight)/(ΣHeavy). These ratios were then multiplied by the heavy peptide amount in fmol per injection, yielding the abundance of the light peptide in fmol per injection.</p>
<p id="Par86">Peptide intensities were normalized based on APOB protein peptides FALSGIVTPGAK, FLGGSHDNSISLTK, IQIQNIDIR, a protein with very low abundance differences between experimental groups. The reference sample for the normalization was chosen as the one that for all three peptides had an estimated amount in fmol closer to the median fmol amount across samples. Normalization factors per sample were calculated for each peptide independently as the ratio of peptide amounts of each sample relative to the reference sample. A consensus normalization factor per sample was then calculated as the mean of the normalization factors of the three peptides. These values were used to get the final peptide amounts, by dividing the original values by the corresponding sample normalization factor. Significant changes in peptide abundances between NE and Ctrl groups were assessed by Mann-Whitney test. Target peptide ratios detected with PRM assay are reported in Supplementary Table <a href="#MOESM6" class="usa-link">S6</a>.</p></section></section><section id="Sec17"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="Par87">In the present study, we used a broiler gut inflammation model of NE to identify potential blood plasma biomarkers for intestinal health of chicken by mass spectrometry-based proteomics. The primary objective was to demonstrate the performance of available DDA- and DIA-based methods, using standard settings. Rather than directly comparing these strategies, we aimed to leverage their unique strengths. While the nano-DDA approach resulted in higher initial numbers of identified proteins, the Evo-DIA workflow showed superior completeness and quantification of the acquired data. Nonetheless, both approaches quantified proteins with a similar precision and we affirm that both methods, with inherent advantages and limitations, serve the purpose for comparative studies between healthy and diseased samples, as well as for the biomarker discovery purposes. Furthermore, the identified potential biomarkers highlight the utility and effectiveness of presented approach, leveraging the agreement in performance between proteomics methods. This methodological innovation offers new insights into the molecular mechanisms of NE in poultry and fills a critical void in avian proteomics. The gut barrier failure in broilers may be diagnosed and evaluated using the prospective biomarkers that have been identified, as a basis for field validation by quick and easy diagnostic instruments.</p></section><section id="Sec18"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Electronic supplementary material</h2>
