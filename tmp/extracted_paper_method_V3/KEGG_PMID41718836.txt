>Materials and methods   Participants in the study   The study included 30 participants aged 30–60 years, comprising 15 in the MASLD-PD group and 15 in the PD group. MASLD-PD patients were required to meet diagnostic criteria for both PD and MASLD. According to the 2023 Multispecialty Delphi Consensus, MASLD diagnosis requires the presence of hepatic steatosis (confirmed by imaging or biopsy) alongside at least one cardiac metabolic risk factor (abnormal BMI/waist circumference, abnormal blood glucose or type 2 diabetes, elevated blood pressure, elevated triglycerides, or reduced high-density lipoprotein) (Rinella et al.,  2023 ). The 2018 Periodontitis Case Definition Criteria are used to diagnose periodontitis: Periodontitis can be identified during a clinical examination if one of the two following criteria is satisfied: Clinical attachment loss (CAL) is present on the interproximal surface of at least two non-adjacent teeth, or at least two teeth have CAL ≥ 3 mm on the buccal or lingual side, along with periodontal pockets ≥ 3 mm (Papapanou et al.,  2018 ; Tonetti et al.,  2018 ). Exclusion criteria include malignant tumors, pregnancy or lactation, history of periodontal therapy, use of immunosuppressants, antibiotics, or drugs related to metabolism during the previous six months, and other liver problems (drug-, alcohol-, or viral). The PD group’s diagnostic criteria are identical to those previously mentioned, with the following exclusions: pregnancy or lactation, a history of periodontal therapy, the use of antibiotics or anti-inflammatory drugs within the last six months, and other systemic disorders. No statistically significant differences were observed in baseline clinical characteristics such as age, CAL, and probing pocket depth (PPD) between the two groups (all  p  > 0.05), and these data are detailed in Table  1 . The sampling locations were completely cleaned of supragingival plaque using professional instruments, and the gingival border was dried with sterile cotton balls to avoid blood or saliva contamination. A single sample of deep biofilm is carefully scraped from the bottom of the periodontal pocket along the inner wall of the pocket by skilled medical personnel using a sterile Gracey curette. To get a second sample, the same process is carried out in a different quadrant that satisfies the requirements. After that, the plaque samples from the two collection regions were mixed together and put in a centrifuge tube with 750 µL of lysis buffer called PowerLyzer™ PowerSoil ®  Bead Solution (Qiagen, Germany). After that, the tube was moved to a freezer and kept there at −80 °C. The technique is carried out with strict adherence to aseptic operating norms. To guarantee the accuracy of results from microbiological analyses and the uniformity of sample collection, all staff members have received standardized training. This efficiently avoids contamination from non-target regions, which can otherwise affect the outcomes of experiments (Jensen et al.,  2018 ). Sample processing   Transfer all samples to 2 mL centrifuge tubes, add one 6 mm diameter grinding bead, then add 800 µL of extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1, v/v) containing four internal standards (e.g., L-2-chloro-phenylalanine (0.02 mg/mL), etc.) in extraction solvent (methanol: water = 4:1, v/v). Grind for 6 min at −10 °C and 50 Hz using a cryogenic tissue grinder, followed by cold ultrasonic extraction at 5 °C and 40 kHz for 30 min. Afterwards, the sample was left to stand at −20 °C for 30 min, then centrifuged at 13,000 g and 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to a sample vial with an insert tube for instrumental analysis. Additionally, 20 µL of supernatant was transferred from each sample, mixed, and used as a quality control sample. QC sample   Equal volumes of all sample metabolites were pooled to prepare quality control (QC) samples. During instrumental analysis, one QC sample was inserted every 5–10 samples to assess the repeatability of the entire analytical process. LC-MS/MS detection   The instrument platform employed for this liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis was Thermo Fisher Scientific’s Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Fourier Transform Ion Spectrometry UHPLC-Orbitrap Exploris 240 system (Shanghai Meiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). 3 µL of the sample was separated on an HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm) before entering mass spectrometry detection. Mobile phase A comprised a water/acetonitrile (95/5, v/v) solution containing 0.1% formic acid, whilst mobile phase B was an acetonitrile/isopropanol/water (47.5/47.5/5, v/v/v) solution containing 0.1% formic acid. Flow rate was maintained at 0.40 mL/min with column temperature set to 40 °C. Mass spectrometry signal acquisition employed positive and negative ion scanning modes, with a mass range of m/z 70–1050. Ion spray voltage settings were 3500 V for positive ions and − 3000 V for negative ions. Sheath gas was 50 argon (Ar), auxiliary heating gas 13 Ar, and ion source heating temperature 450 °C. Collision energy cycling was set at 20–40–60 V. Data analysis   Following instrument operation, the LC-MS raw data were imported into the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment. Yielding a final data matrix comprising retention times, mass-to-charge ratios, and peak intensities. Concurrently, MS and MS/MS mass spectrometry data were matched against public metabolic databases HMDB ( http://www.hmdb.ca/ ) and Metlin ( https://metlin.scripps.edu/ ), alongside MajorBio’s proprietary library, to derive metabolite identifications.   The searched data matrix was uploaded to the MajorBio Cloud platform (cloud.majorbio.com) for analysis. Preprocessing of the data matrix was performed as follows: Missing values were removed using the 80% rule, retaining variables with non-zero values in at least 80% of samples. Missing values were then imputed (using the minimum value in the original matrix). To minimise errors from sample preparation and instrument instability, the response intensities of sample mass spectrometry peaks were normalised using sum-normalisation, yielding a normalised data matrix. Simultaneously, variables with QC sample relative standard deviations (RSD) exceeding 30% were excluded. Logarithmic transformation (log10) was applied to the data, yielding the final matrix for subsequent analysis. Subsequently, the ropls package (Version 1.6.2) in R was employed to perform three complementary multidimensional statistical models: (1) Unsupervised Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was first used to verify the natural separation trend of metabolic profiles between groups without prior grouping information, ensuring that group differences were not artificially induced; (2) Supervised Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was applied to enhance the extraction of differential information related to grouping, maximizing the distinction between the MASLD-PD and PD groups; (3) Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) was further used to separate the predictive variables (group-related variation) from orthogonal variables (non-group-related variation), reducing the interference of irrelevant noise on differential metabolite screening. Model stability and reliability were assessed using seven-fold cross-validation (excluding one sample in each fold for validation, repeating seven times) to avoid overfitting. Significantly different metabolites were strictly screened based on three criteria: VIP_pred_OPLS-DA > 1 (reflecting the contribution of metabolites to group separation), FDR-adjusted  p -value < 0.05 (controlling for false discovery rate in multiple comparisons), and fold change (FC) ≥ 1 (upregulated) or ≤ 1 (downregulated) (reflecting the magnitude of expression differences). To further refine diagnostically valuable biomarkers, mitigate multicollinearity interference, and enhance model practicality, LASSO regression analysis was conducted on the 20 significantly upregulated differential metabolites using the glmnet package in R ( https://glmnet.stanford.edu/ ). Five-fold cross-validation was applied to determine the optimal regularization parameters (lambda.min and lambda.1se), which balance model performance and parsimony. Metabolites with non-zero regression coefficients after regularization were defined as core diagnostic features. For the core feature panel, key model evaluation metrics including Kappa coefficient, F1 score, Precision, Recall, and Accuracy were calculated to assess diagnostic consistency, comprehensiveness, and correctness. Additionally, ROC curve analysis was performed on the core feature combination to verify its discriminatory efficacy between MASLD-PD and PD groups, with the area under the curve (AUC) and 95% confidence interval (CI) computed to quantify the diagnostic accuracy. Differential metabolites underwent metabolic pathway annotation via the KEGG database ( https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html ), which is the most comprehensive public database for integrating genomic, metabolomic, and pathway information. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the scipy.stats package in Python with the hypergeometric test, a standard method for enrichment analysis that calculates the probability of differential metabolites being enriched in a specific pathway by chance. This method is particularly suitable for metabolomic data because it accounts for the background distribution of metabolites in the KEGG database, avoiding false positive enrichment results caused by random overlap. Result   Multidimensional statistical analysis of metabolomic differences   Multidimensional statistical analysis was performed on their LC-MS metabolomics data to clarify the metabolic phenotype differences between the MASLD-PD group and the PD group. PCA revealed that PC1 and PC2 explained 39.80% and 6.35% of the variance, respectively, with a cumulative contribution rate of 46.15%. As shown in Fig.  1 a (PCA score plot), the two groups clustered distinctly, exhibiting non-overlapping 95% confidence ellipses. Further analysis via PLS-DA revealed Component1 and Component2 explaining 43.40% and 4.36% of variance, respectively. Figure  1 b (PLS-DA score plot) shows that the two sample groups exhibited significant clustering and separation, with completely non-overlapping 95% confidence ellipses. Model parameter details in Table  2  (PLS-DA Model Parameter) show that the cumulative explanatory power of the model for the X matrix (R²X(cum)) reached 0.518, the cumulative explanatory power for the Y matrix (R²Y(cum)) was 0.997, and the cumulative predictive capability (Q²(cum)) was 0.941, all close to 1, indicating the model has good stability and reliability. Moreover, the PLS-DA permutation test results in Fig.  1 d confirm that R 2 Y = 0.997 and Q 2  = 0.941 ( p  < 0.05, 0/200), which further verifies that the model has no overfitting issues and possesses stable discriminatory performance. In OPLS-DA, Component1 accounted for 40.10% variance, while orthogonal Component1 explained 17.67% variance. As presented in Fig.  1 c (OPLS-DA score plot), the two sample groups exhibited clear clustering and separation, with no overlap in the 95% confidence ellipses. As shown in Table  3  (OPLS-DA Model Parameter), the model’s cumulative explanatory power for the X matrix (R²X(cum)) was 0.477, the cumulative explanatory power for the Y matrix (R²Y(cum)) was 0.98, and the cumulative predictive capability (Q²(cum)) was 0.906, meeting the criteria for a reliable model (Q² > 0.5). The OPLS-DA permutation test in Fig.  1 e further confirms the model’s reliability: R 2 Y = 0.98, Q 2  = 0.906 ( p  < 0.05, 0/200), ruling out overfitting and validating the stability of the group separation trend. These findings, supported by the score plots in Fig.  1 a–c, the permutation test results in Fig.  1 d, e, and the model parameters in Tables  2  and  3 , indicate significant metabolomic differences between the MASLD-PD and PD groups. The PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA models all demonstrate effective ability to distinguish the metabolic profiles of the two sample groups, laying a solid foundation for subsequent differential metabolite screening and pathway analysis. It is important to note that given the small sample size (15 cases per group) and lack of independent validation, the AUC values approaching 1.0 may be inflated, which is a key limitation of this exploratory analysis. As shown in Fig.  3 a; Table  4 , several metabolites show preliminary discriminatory potential, but their performance should be interpreted cautiously: for instance, 2-Hydroxynevirapine, Naloxonazine, and Gamma-Glutamyl-beta-cyanoalanine yielded AUC values of 1.0000 (95% CI: 0.6233–1.0000, 0.7125–1.0000, 0.6500–1.0000, respectively), and Azimexon with an AUC of 0.9500 (95% CI: 0.7500–1.0000). Figure  3 b; Table  4  show that the combined discriminatory performance of these 20 metabolites reached an AUC of 1.0000 (95% CI: 0.9998–1.0000) in this small cohort, but this result is likely overestimated due to the limited sample size and potential overfitting. This exploratory finding generates the hypothesis that differential metabolite expression may be associated with the metabolic distinction between MASLD-PD and PD, but it does not reflect true clinical discriminatory capacity, and no clinical application value is inferred at this stage.
