========== paper_workflow entry 3 ==========
related_tool: CFM-ID
sample_description: The study used experimental MS/MS data from three spectral libraries: (1) NIST'17, a commercial library containing MS/MS spectra from authentic compound standards, converted to .msp format using lib2nist and parsed with RDKit for molecular structure information (SMILES, MOL, InChiKey); (2) the public MS-DIAL spectral library (available in .msp format), which aggregates annotated spectra from MassBank and GNPS and provides standardized metadata including collision energy, retention time (RT), and collision cross-section (CCS); and (3) MSnLib v1.0, a recently published open-source library containing over 1 million multi-stage fragmentation spectra, including 177,390 MS/MS spectra for 15,967 unique compounds. All libraries were pre-processed uniformly: normalized collision energy (NCE) was converted to electron volts (eV) using the Proteomicsnews formula; spectra with missing or ambiguous collision energies were excluded; compounds with inconsistencies among SMILES, MOL, and InChiKey representations were filtered out; only [M+H]+ and [M−H]− ionization modes were retained (plus [M]+ and [M]− for MSnLib); molecular weight was capped at 1000 Da and collision energy at 100 eV; fragment–peak matching was performed at 50 ppm tolerance for NIST and MS-DIAL (10 ppm for MSnLib) against the theoretically generated fragment ion space F(G); ambiguous matches (multiple fragments mapping to same m/z within tolerance) were assigned equal probability; spectra were required to have ≥2 matched peaks, ≥50% peak intensity coverage, ≤90% precursor intensity, and either ≥50% of total peaks matched or ≥5 matched peaks or ≥80% intensity coverage; the NIST'17 and MS-DIAL libraries were combined into a single training set of 74,401 spectra from 10,692 compounds, while MSnLib was used separately to train an open-source FIORA variant; additionally, benchmarking used CASMI 2016 and CASMI 2022 challenge datasets—CASMI 2016 included stepped collision energy spectra (predicted at all three energies and merged), while CASMI 2022 challenge spectra were extracted from mzML files using OpenMS Python wrapper with 10 ppm precursor tolerance and 5-second RT window, followed by consensus spectrum generation per collision energy level; all test compounds were rigorously excluded from respective training sets for FIORA and ICEBERG, whereas CFM-ID’s training on METLIN 17 means no such guarantee exists.
complete_workflow_description: FIORA’s workflow consists of four core procedural stages: (1) Graph construction and preprocessing: Molecular structures are parsed from SMILES strings into undirected molecular graphs G where atoms are nodes and bonds are edges; atom features (element type, number of bound hydrogens, ring membership) and bond features (bond type, ring membership) are encoded as integers and embedded into 300-dimensional vectors via learnable embedding layers; (2) Graph neural network processing: A 6-layer Relational Graph Convolutional Network (RGCN) is applied by default (though GCN, RGCN, GAT, and Graph Transformer layers are supported), with Exponential Linear Unit (ELU) activation after each layer; node representations are updated via message-passing: h_i = ϕ(x_i, ⊕_{j∈N_i} ψ(x_i, x_j)), where ⊕ is summation aggregation and ϕ/ψ are learnable functions; (3) Prediction head computation: For fragment ion abundance θ_f, edge features are constructed by concatenating embeddings of the two incident atoms, the edge embedding, and covariates (molecular weight, precursor ion mode ([M+H]+/[M−H]−), collision energy, instrument type), then passed through a two-layer fully connected network outputting a 10-dimensional logit vector (5 ion modes × 2 fragment sides per edge break); precursor stability σ is computed from the global mean-pooled graph embedding concatenated with the same covariates, processed by two fully connected layers yielding a scalar logit; all logits (σ and θ_f for all f ∈ F(G)) are jointly normalized via softmax to produce fragment and precursor probabilities p(f); (4) Spectrum reconstruction and post-processing: Predicted subgraphs (fragments) are traced back using the edge map; exact m/z values are reconstructed from fragment mass and hydrogen loss count (0–3 H); peak intensities are obtained by summing probabilities of all fragment ions falling within the m/z tolerance window (50 ppm for NIST/MS-DIAL, 10 ppm for MSnLib); RT and CCS values are predicted separately using the same mean-pooled graph embedding fed into dedicated dense layers (frozen during fragment training) with standard MSE loss; training uses weighted mean squared error loss between predicted fragment probabilities and observed probabilities (derived from square-root-transformed peak intensities matched to F(G)), weighted by 1/(number of spectra per compound) to mitigate compound-level bias; optimization employs ADAM with learning rate scheduling on validation loss plateau over 200 epochs; ICEBERG was trained identically to FIORA (same splits, same workflow) using the GitHub repository (commit from Oct 21, 2023); all software dependencies rely on PyTorch Geometric for GNN implementation.

complete_workflow_description: The workflow involved: (1) filtering the NIST20 library by removing compounds overlapping with NIST17 or CFM-ID 4.0 training data; (2) generating in silico MS/MS spectra using CFM-ID 4.04 and MSRB 1.1.3 (Docker images); (3) computing unweighted dot product similarities between predicted and experimental spectra with 10 ppm mass tolerance and exclusion of ±2 Da around precursor; (4) normalizing all spectra to relative abundance before similarity calculation; (5) classifying compound structures using ClassyFire via batch web service; (6) obtaining CACTVS fingerprints using PubChem REST API/web tool; (7) performing dimensionality reduction (UMAP, PCA, t-SNE) on combined training and test fingerprints; (8) partitioning spectra into six instrument/adduct groups per Table 1; (9) analyzing collision energy effects by binning experimental energies into 1 eV intervals (1–45 eV for Q-TOF; 1–70 eV for Orbitrap, limited to first 50 bins due to data sparsity) and correlating with simulated 10/20/40 eV CFM-ID predictions; (10) restricting high-energy analysis to Thermo Finnigan Elite Orbitrap spectra with explicit eV metadata (35–45 eV) to mitigate %NCE instrument variability; (11) computing best-match dot scores across the three CFM-ID energies per spectrum and retaining the maximum; (12) conducting all statistical analyses and visualizations using custom Python scripts.

complete_workflow_description: The PyFragMS software tool was developed as a web application using Anvil (https://anvil.works/) for the front-end and Python for the back-end. ========== paper_workflow entry 6 ==========
related_tool: CFM-ID
sample_description: A total of 1269 unique chemical substances were spiked across ten synthetic mixtures (labeled 499 through 508), with each mixture containing 95 to 365 substances. These mixtures constitute the EPA's ENTACT (Environmental and Toxicological Assessment of Chemicals in Mixtures) reference set. Only substances previously classified as 'pass' — i.e., those linked to experimental features with strong diagnostic evidence (e.g., corroborating MS1 and MS2 data supporting 'probable structure' identification per Schymanski et al. confidence level 2) — were included in the current in silico library matching analysis. A total of 845 'pass' substances spanned all ten mixtures, and for each, MS2 spectra were acquired at three collision energy (CE) levels (10, 20, and 40 V) in both positive ([M+H]⁺) and negative ([M−H]⁻) ionization modes using LC/MS. MS2 spectra corresponding to these 'pass' substances served as the experimental query set; each spectrum was associated with a known ground-truth compound identified by its DSSTox MS-Ready DTXCID. Sample preparation and LC/MS acquisition used an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC coupled to an Agilent 6530B Q-TOF mass spectrometer with Dual AJS ionization, ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C8 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 μm), mobile phases of 0.4 mM ammonium formate in water and methanol, and mass calibration via continuous infusion of purine/hexakis(1H,1H,3H-tetrafluoropropoxy)phosphazene/TFA. MS1 and MS2 scans covered m/z 100–1000; Auto MS2 acquisition settings included up to 3 precursors/cycle, minimum threshold 3000 counts, scan rate 4 spectra/second, and dynamic exclusion/inclusion lists. Each sample was analyzed three times to generate one full MS2 dataset per CE level.
complete_workflow_description: The in silico library matching workflow was implemented using a custom Python script (source code available at https://github.com/NTA-Code/cfmid). First, experimental MS2 spectra were exported from Agilent .d files into MGF format. The Python script then performed formatting standardization and de-duplication: for each unique precursor m/z, only the MS2 spectrum with the highest sum intensity of fragment ions was retained. Next, for each experimental spectrum, candidate compounds were retrieved from the CFM-ID database based on 10-ppm mass match (and optionally formula match). Three scoring approaches were evaluated: (1) '1:1 matching' — only the CFM-ID spectrum at the same CE level as the experimental spectrum was scored; (2) '1:3 summation' — scores from all three CFM-ID CE levels (10, 20, 40 V) were summed for a given experimental spectrum; (3) '9-way summation' — scores from all nine combinations of experimental CE (10/20/40 V) × in silico CE (10/20/40 V) were summed. All scoring used the dot-product algorithm with 0.02 Da fragment tolerance. For each experimental spectrum, resulting scores were used to rank candidate compounds, compute percentile ranks (relative position among all candidates), and quotient values (candidate score divided by maximum candidate score for that spectrum). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed globally across all 845 'pass' compounds to determine optimal cutoff thresholds (percentile and quotient) achieving ≥0.90 true positive rate (TPR); these global thresholds were then applied mixture-wise to compute mixture-specific TPRs and false positive rates (FPRs) as performance metrics. Analyses were conducted both with and without formula filtering to assess robustness under realistic (mass-only) versus idealized (formula-known) conditions.

complete_workflow_description: The research employed a comparative evaluation framework for four in silico fragmentation tools—CFM-ID, MS-FINDER, MetFragCL, and MAGMa+—applied to CASMI 2016 Category 2 (in silico fragmentation only). A custom Java application was developed to standardize input/output handling across tools, resolve structure conversion issues (e.g., salt forms, isotopic elements, radicals), and process result files; associated scripts and source code are publicly available at https://sourceforge.net/projects/insilico-fragmentation/. Parameter optimization was first conducted on the 312-training-set spectra, followed by development of a voting/consensus model that integrated ranked outputs from multiple tools—optionally augmented with database lookups (ChemSpider, MS/MS libraries)—to produce improved rankings. All evaluations adhered to CASMI Category 2 guidelines: no secondary database ranking or external metadata was used in the baseline in silico assessment.

complete_workflow_description: The global workflow began with CASMI 2016-provided MGF-formatted experimental MS/MS spectra (312 training + 208 challenge) acquired on a Q Exactive Plus under ESI-CID conditions (stepped 20/35/50 eV, 35,000 resolution, <5 ppm mass accuracy). Performance was assessed by top-1 identification accuracy. Database boosting — i.e., prioritizing candidates already present in spectral libraries — was identified as a critical enhancement step.
