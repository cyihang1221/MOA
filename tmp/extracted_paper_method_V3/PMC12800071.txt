</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="Par1">Improving the efficiency of data compression remains essential for feature selection and data modelling. Current approaches for compressing epigenomic/genomic data highly rely on autoencoder that requires substantial computing resources, parameter fine-tuning, training, and time. Here, we developed a training-free, Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)-based method, for data compression with high efficiency and full interpretability. Our FFT method compresses epigenomic data of histone modification up to 1,000-fold while still maintaining high reconstruction fidelity (cosine similarity, 99.7%), does not require any training and completes ultrafast within 70 ms on GPU or 20 s on CPU opposite to extensive training in hours/days for autoencoder on GPU/CPU, and offers full interpretability of compressed features from frequency components of original signals in contrast to the uninterpretable “black box” from autoencoder. This enables high accuracy in the classification model prediction (AUC, 0.960). Thus, our novel FFT method represents a major paradigm shift in data compression.</p>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h3>
<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31254-0.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Subject terms:</strong> Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par2">The Fourier Transform (FT) has long been established as a robust method for compressing and processing image data and sound signals<sup><a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>–<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a></sup>. The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), first introduced by James W. Cooley and John W. Tukey, is an efficient algorithm for computing the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) and its inverse IDFT<sup><a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a></sup>. By factorizing the DFT matrix, the FFT significantly accelerated the computation and reduced the complexity to <em>O</em> (<em>n</em> log<em> n</em>)<sup><a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a></sup>, which is crucial for processing large datasets. FFT has been widely applied in various fields related to audio and image compression but never been used for processing epigenomic data.</p>
<p id="Par3">The landscape of epigenetic research has been transformed by next-generation sequencing technologies to map genome-wide locations of epigenetic signals and regulators or epigenomic data, for instance, mapping genome-wide locations of histone modifications by chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled high-throughput sequencing (ChIP-Seq)<sup><a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a></sup>. The scale of these large epigenomic data is ideal for data modelling using machine learning. While these advances have provided unprecedented insights into chromatin states, massive datasets were also generated, posing significant computational and analytical challenges. This necessitates the application of effective data compression methods for handling these large datasets.</p>
<p id="Par4">Many methods have been sought to project high-dimensional genomic and epigenomic datasets into lower-dimensional representations, such as the principal component analysis (PCA) integrated with sliced inverse regression<sup><a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a></sup> or partial Cox regression<sup><a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a></sup>, an L0 segmentation-based method<sup><a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a></sup>. Recently, deep learning models, particularly autoencoders, have been heavily explored for epigenomic data compression, such as an Epi-LSTM-based autoencoder<sup><a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a></sup>, a 1D-convolutional variational autoencoder framework (ConvNet-VAEs)<sup><a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a></sup>, and a 300-fold compression approach but only with 60% accuracy<sup><a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a></sup>. Despite the potential, deep learning models like autoencoders require extensive computing resources of large computing units of GPUs, prolonged training time, careful hyperparameter tuning, and multiple times of tuning and training, resulting in significant challenge to be implemented in the large-scale studies. Autoencoders are widely considered to be a “black box” with limited interpretability. Recently, many efforts have been employed to increase the interpretation of autoencoder models with assisting from other approaches, such as removing non-linearity in the decoder<sup><a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a></sup> and introducing dual embedding spaces with a weak correspondence term<sup><a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a></sup>. However, both approaches achieve only partial interpretability due to non-linear transformations in portions of their architectures and mathematical abstractions of their latent representations that require significant effort to connect to biological meaning. These limitations underscore the need for a more efficient, interpretable, and computationally tractable alternative.</p>
<p id="Par5">Given that histone modification signals can be represented as continuous waveforms, we hypothesized that FFT could provide an efficient compression solution for epigenomic data. While other compression methods exist, we focus our comparison on FFT-based and autoencoder-based approaches, which represent two fundamentally different paradigms: frequency-domain versus neural network-based compression. Here, we introduce a novel FFT-based method that achieves effective compression of histone modification data at single-nucleotide resolution, addressing the computing and interpretability limitations of autoencoder-based approaches.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data processing</h3>
<p id="Par6">We obtained genome-wide histone modification data of H3K4me3 from the Zenodo repository as published by Sadeh et al.<sup><a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a></sup>. This dataset was generated using cfChIP-Seq technology to profile histone modifications in cell-free plasma samples from 88 healthy donors and 128 colorectal cancer (CRC) patients.</p>
<p id="Par7"><em>Human ethics statement</em>: all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations of human ethics protocol (#<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/ET000631" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ET000631</a>) approved by the human ethics committee of the University of Western Australia. Given we used the publicly available patient datasets of de-identified information, no informed consent of human subjects are required.</p>
<p id="Par8">For the single-nucleotide resolution processing, we defined a genomic sequence <em>G</em> of length <em>n</em> base pairs, where each position <em>g</em> ∈ <em>G</em> is indexed from 1 to <em>n</em>. The set of H3K4me3 peaks <em>P</em> consists of tuples <em>(s,e,v),</em> where s and e denote the start and end positions, respectively, and <em>v</em> represents the peak intensity value. The signal intensity <em>S(g)</em> at genomic position <em>g</em> is defined as:</p>

Higher cosine similarity values (closer to 1) indicate a higher degree of pattern preservation and suggest that the reconstructed data closely follows the trends and structure of the original data.</p>

<p id="Par25">To rigorously evaluate the consistency of both FFT and autoencoder methods across variable genomic contexts, we partitioned each chromosome into contiguous non-overlapping 50 Mb segments and analysed them at single-nucleotide resolution. For each segment, we quantified reconstruction fidelity using MSE, MAE, and COS.</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Classification model</h3>
<p id="Par26">To evaluate the downstream utility of our compression methods, we developed a classification framework to distinguish between healthy and colorectal cancer samples using three datasets: (i) original signals of H3K4me3 from ChIP-Seq data, (ii) FFT-compressed signals, and (iii) autoencoder-compressed signals. Our analysis was focused on those genes with differential signals of H3K4me3 between healthy and cancer samples. For each gene, we analysed the entire gene body plus an additional 2 kb upstream of its transcription start site at single-nucleotide resolution to capture the complete signature of H3K4me3 signals.</p>
<p id="Par27">For classification, we employed XGBoost<sup><a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a></sup> with GPU acceleration, configured with 500 estimators, a maximum tree depth of 50, and a learning rate of 0.1 with five-fold cross-validation. The dataset was stratified into 80% training and 20% testing sets. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, area under the curve (AUC) scores, and average precision (AP) using scikit-learn<sup><a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a></sup>.</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Implementation</h3>
<p id="Par28">All computational analyses were performed using PyTorch 2.5.1 and Python 3.11.10. Both the autoencoder architecture (implemented with PyTorch’s neural network modules) and FFT operations (utilizing PyTorch’s native implementation) were accelerated on NVIDIA Tesla V100-PCIe-32 GB GPUs at Kaya supercomputing cluster owned by the University of Western Australia. For testing time performance between FFT and autoencoder on CPU, evaluations were performed on a single core CPU (Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6254 CPU @ 3.10 GHz). The classification model was implemented using XGBoost 2.0.3 and executed on the same GPU architecture. Performance metrics including MSE, MAE, COS, ROC-AUC, and AP were calculated using scikit-learn 1.5.2<sup><a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a></sup>.</p></section></section><section id="Sec9"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">FFT transforms epigenomic data into fully interpretable frequency components</h3>
<p id="Par29">Briefly, we developed a PyTorch-based FFT approach for epigenomic data compression with the frequency annotation of each original signal (refer to details in Methods). The dataset used in this study was collected from genome-wide H3K4me3 signals of ChIP-Seq data in cell-free plasma samples from 88 healthy donors and 128 colorectal cancer patients from a previous report<sup><a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par30">To further expand the scale of data, we calculated the intensity of H3K4me3 signals at single-nucleotide resolution (details in Methods) across a genomic region of 300 kilobase (kb) surrounding the locus of a housekeeping gene, <em>GAPDH</em>, and then plotted the signal intensity versus relative genomic positions (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>a), where the peaks represent the enriched areas of H3K4me3 signals, indicating potential regulatory regions near active transcription start sites. These signals showed characteristic of H3K4me3 distribution patterns with both sharp, localized peaks and broader regions of enrichment (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>a). Next, we applied our FFT method to convert these H3K4me3 signals into the frequency data (details in Methods). Figure <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>b shows the frequency spectrum and magnitude of H3K4me3 signals with wide dynamic range obtained from the FFT analysis. The magnitude of frequency was calculated by the square root of the sum of the squared real and imaginary parts of the frequency component (details in Methods), representing the strength or contribution of each frequency component to individual original signals<sup><a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a></sup>. Along the frequency axis, low-frequency components (near 0) illustrate sharp, distinct peaks, representing genuine H3K4me4 signals in the genome, whereas high-frequency components (closer to 0.5) typically represent noise, rapid fluctuations, and background variations in the signals (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>b). More importantly, these compressed features from our FFT method directly correspond to those specific frequency components in the original signals, providing full interpretability of compressed data, which completely opposite to the uninterpretable “black box” representations from the autoencoder-based approaches.</p></section><section id="Sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">FFT achieves high efficiency of data compression</h3>
<p id="Par31">The high-magnitude components represent frequencies with the strongest influence on the signal structure. Using the same 300 kb genomic region in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>, we filtered the frequency spectrum to retain only the components with the highest magnitude (highlighted in green in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>b) while removing noise (details in Methods). Next, we used these high-magnitude components for data compression/reconstruction with our FFT method (details in Methods). To evaluate our FFT method, we compared it with an autoencoder-based method reported previously<sup><a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a></sup>. Detailed autoencoder models architectures and the comparison pairs of selected features between FFT and autoencoder methods are described in Methods (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a> and <a href="#Tab2" class="usa-link">2</a> in Methods).</p>
<p id="Par32">An example of reconstructed and original data of the same region of 300 kb from Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a> at the 100-fold compression with the original signal intensity (blue) alongside two reconstructed signals, FFT (green) and autoencoder (orange) is shown in Extended Data Fig. <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">1</a>a. We also extended our analysis to lager genomic regions from 300 kb to 50 million bases (Mb) (Extended Data Fig. <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">1</a>,<a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">2</a>) and further to individual chromosomes of the entire human genome (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>).</p>

The analysis of selected genomic regions from 0.3 to 50 Mb showed that consistently lower values of both mean squared error and mean absolute error from the FFT analyses compared to the autoencoder ones while the decrease in both values following the increase in the data sizes for both methods; the cosine similarity was constantly higher in our FFT analyses than the autoencoder (Extended Data Fig. <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">2</a>), indicating higher fidelity of reconstructed data. Therefore, we used the 50 Mb as the segment size for genome-wide analysis.</p>
<p id="Par34">At the genome-wide scale, we conducted the parallelled analysis of H3K4me3 signals at single-nucleotide resolution for all consecutive 50 Mb segments across each chromosome using both our FFT and the autoencoder methods with three compression ratios of 100, 300, and 1,000 folds (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>). Specifically, with the 100-fold compression, slight variations in the values of mean squared error and mean absolute error across all 50 Mb segments within the same chromosome were observed from both methods but diminished substantially at higher compression ratios (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>a,b), indicating highly consistent results across all segments. Nevertheless, our FFT method still generated consistently and significantly lower values of mean squared error and mean absolute error in all chromosomes with all three compression ratios than those from the autoencoder method (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>a-d). These results are aligned with our findings in smaller genomic regions (Extended Data Fig. <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">1</a>,<a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">2</a>), indicating that the better signal reconstruction accuracy with minimal background noise of our FFT method compared to the autoencoder one.</p>
<p id="Par35">The cosine similarity analyses also showed superior performance from our FFT method to the autoencoder one (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>c), exactly like the patterns observed from smaller genomic regions (Extended Data Fig. <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">2</a>). More importantly, our FFT method showed consistent and super-higher cosine similarity (on average 0.997 for 100X, 0.987 for 300X, and 0.940 for 1,000X) in all chromosomes within each compression ratio, indicating the dependence on the compression ratios but independent of chromosome sizes; in contrast, the autoencoder method showed large variations of lower values of cosine similarity, particularly in those higher compression ratios of 300X and 1,000X (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>c,d). These results indicate that our FFT method could capture nearly all features in the original data during data reconstruction. This high fidelity of data compression could be due to the full interpretability of compressed features from the frequency components (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>b).</p></section><section id="Sec12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">FFT does not require any training and runs ultrafast</h3>
<p id="Par36">Of a unique advantage was that our FFT method for data compression did not require any training. In contrast, autoencoder models usually require extensive time of hours to days to train the models depending on the data size and also multiple rounds of training. Figure <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>a defines the inference time and total time used for both FFT and autoencoder methods. The training time shown for autoencoder is only one-time training required initially but does not include potential extensive and multiple rounds of tuning and training afterwards. Total time represents the time required for completing the entire process of data reconstruction for each method. For FFT, the total time includes inference time plus reconstruction time. For autoencoder, the total time includes the time for training, inference time, and decoding phases together (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>a). Data related to inference time are shown in Extended Data Fig. <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">3</a>.</p>

Shockingly, our FFT method was ultrafast and the FFT-based data compression was finished within 70 ms (68.10–68.69 ms) across all different sizes of genomic regions from 0.3 to 50 Mb with all three compression ratios on GPU; in contrast, the autoencoder analysis required about 0.94–1.52 h to complete with one round of training (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>b), representing at least 49,264 times of faster (0.94 h/68.69 ms) of our FFT method than the autoencoder one on GPU. Such identical total time were also observed for the autoencoder method (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>b), given that the training time of hours accounts for the majority of the total time.</p>
<p id="Par39">In summary, our FFT method dramatically outperformed the autoencoder one by delivering highly efficient data compression with substantially less computing time and computing resources.</p></section><section id="Sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">FFT retains key biological features in highly compressed data</h3>
<p id="Par40">To evaluate whether FFT-compressed data could be effectively utilized for the downstream analytical tasks, we developed a classification model for distinguishing between healthy and colorectal cancer samples using XGBoost<sup><a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a></sup>. To assess this model, we examined genomic regions associated with two key loci: <em>CDX1</em> (Caudal Type Homeobox 1) and <em>ELF3</em> (E74 Like ETS Transcription Factor 3). <em>CDX1</em> is an important tumour signature gene for colorectal cancer<sup><a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>,<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a></sup>. <em>ELF3</em> showed significant enrichment of H3K4me3 signals in colorectal cancer samples from this epigenomic dataset but was not previously identified as a tumour signature in the original study<sup><a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par41">We analysed H3K4me3 signals at these selected regions surrounding <em>CDX1</em> and <em>ELF3</em> using compressed data from both FFT and autoencoder methods. For the autoencoder analysis, we utilized latent feature values, while for the FFT method, we split complex numbers into two components and flattened them into a single one-dimensional array. Classification was performed using an XGBoost<sup><a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a></sup> model with five-fold cross-validation. The dataset of total 216 samples (88 healthy donors and 128 colorectal cancer) was randomly split into training set (80%, n = 172) and testing set (20%, n = 44). We assessed the model performance using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, Area Under the Curve (AUC), and Average Precision (Extended Data Fig. <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">4</a>a) with algorithms from scikit-learn<sup><a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par42">The models built on the FFT-compressed data outperformed both models from the original uncompressed data and the autoencoder-compressed data at all three selected genomic regions across all compression ratios, as evidenced by higher AUC values (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>). For <em>CDX1</em>, the FFT-based models reached AUC values of 0.802, 0.905, and 0.871 were all greater than the AUC values of 0.726, 0.780, and 0.763, below the standard threshold of 0.8, from the autoencoder-based models for three compression ratios of 100X, 300X, and 1,000X, respectively (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>a). Therefore, the classification models built upon our novel FFT method showing much better performance further demonstrate that this epigenetic signature can serve as a robust biomarker for colorectal cancer.</p>

This finding supports previous studies indicating <em>ELF3</em> overexpression as a diagnostic marker<sup><a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a></sup> and its association with poor prognosis for colorectal cancer<sup><a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par44">The combination of both markers yielded robust classification performance from the FFT-based models (AUC = 0.90) (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>c). Similar results were also observed from the combined regions associated with top 54 genes (Extended Data Fig. <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">4</a>b). This demonstrates that our FFT method is powerful not only for analysing the region associated with a single gene but also for integrating multiple regions spanning different chromosomes, paving the way for genome-wide classification.</p></section></section><section id="Sec14"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussions</h2>
<p id="Par45">In summary, we developed a novel, training-free, ultrafast, highly efficient, and fully interpretable FFT approach for data compression. The frequency data generated from our FFT method provides full interpretability with signal strength, position and periodicity. Therefore, such full interpretability allowed us to obtain accurate prediction of downstream targets from the classification modelling with high AUC values, shedding light on promising clinical applications. This approach could achieve up to a thousand fold of data compression while still preserving key biological features. Notably, compared to autoencoder-based approaches, our FFT method demonstrates superior accuracy and compression ratios, offering a more versatile, scalable, and fully interpretable alternative to neural network approaches for epigenomic data analysis. Moreover, our approach does not require any condition-specific training, enhancing its applicability across diverse datasets, particularly valuable in resource-constrained environments where GPU availability is limited. This innovation represents a major paradigm shift in data compression and also a significant advancement in the computational analysis of epigenomic data, enabling more comprehensive and nuanced investigations of chromatin states.</p>
<p id="Par46">Our study is the first to apply FFT to compress epigenomic data, though until very recently, FFT started to be used for processing transcriptomic data<sup><a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>,<a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a></sup>. Our FFT method can capture subtle differences in histone modification data at single-nucleotide resolution superior to the traditional ‘binning’ strategy for the epigenomic analysis<sup><a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>,<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a></sup>. Though our study focused on comparing FFT-based and standard autoencoder approaches, advanced autoencoder variational autoencoders (VAEs) has been applied to histone modification data including H3K4me3 across 935 samples<sup><a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a></sup>. While VAEs can capture complex non-linear patterns through probabilistic latent representations, they share similar limitations with standard autoencoders: requiring extensive training data, substantial computational resources for optimization, and careful hyperparameter tuning, while lacking direct interpretability of learned features. PCA also have been extensively applied to histone modification data for dimensionality reduction<sup><a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a></sup>, and while it shares some advantages with FFT as a linear, deterministic method with interpretable components, PCA components are data-dependent and require computation of covariance matrices across samples, which can be computationally intensive for large genomic datasets. Sparse coding have been successfully applied to RNA-seq transcriptomics<sup><a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">39</a></sup>, and genome-wide association studies<sup><a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">40</a></sup>, though their specific application to histone modification compression remains limited; like other learning-based approaches, they require an optimization phase to learn dictionary elements.</p>
<p id="Par47">From our exploration with cfChIP-Seq classification in CRC and Healthy in this paper, FFT does not only serve as a standalone compression method, yet an important direction is exploring how FFT-based compression can be integrated with machine learning approaches. FFT-compressed features could serve as input to downstream machine learning models for biological data processing<sup><a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">41</a></sup>, deciphering tumour evolution<sup><a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">42</a></sup>, and identifying predictive biomarkers<sup><a href="#CR43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR43">43</a></sup>, potentially reducing computational requirements while preserving biologically relevant information. More importantly, our FFT approach holds promise for future work exploring the application of this method to other types of genomic and epigenomic data, potentially revealing frequency domain characteristics of biological significance across different regulatory landscapes.</p></section><section id="Sec120"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h2>
