</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ab0005"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p><em>Metapanax delavayi</em> is widely consumed as a vegetable and herbal tea in western China; however, the impact of postharvest drying on its chemical composition and bioactivity remains insufficiently characterized. In this study, the effects of three drying methods-shade drying (SD), hot-air drying (HAD), and vacuum freeze-drying (VFD)-on the metabolite profile and antioxidant activity of <em>M. delavayi</em> were systematically investigated using UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS and GC–MS/MS-based untargeted metabolomics, combined with multivariate statistical analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment. A total of 742 non-volatile and 87 volatile differential metabolites were detected across the drying treatments, indicating pronounced processing-induced metabolic alterations. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the differential metabolites were mainly associated with flavonoid biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, galactose metabolism, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis. Antioxidant assays (DPPH, ABTS, hydroxyl radical scavenging activity, and FRAP) demonstrated significant differences among drying methods, with VFD-treated samples generally exhibiting higher antioxidant capacity. Correlation analysis suggested that phenolic and flavonoid metabolites were major contributors to antioxidant activity. Overall, compared with SD and HAD, VFD showed a greater capacity to preserve phytochemical composition and antioxidant potential. These findings provide a scientific basis for optimizing drying strategies and improving the quality of <em>M. delavayi</em>-derived products.</p>
<section id="ks0005" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> <em>Metapanax delavayi</em>, Drying methods, Untargeted metabolomics, Differential metabolites, Antioxidant activities</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="ab0010"><h2>Graphical abstract</h2>
<p>The <em>M. delavayi</em> was subjected to SD, HAD, and VFD treatments, respectively. Following processing, comparative metabolomic analysis was performed using coupled UPLC-Q-TOF-MS and GC–MS/MS techniques. Antioxidant activity was evaluated by measuring four indicators: DPPH, ·OH, ABTS, and FRAP.</p>

</ul></section><section id="s0005"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0030"><em>Metapanax delavayi (Franch.) J. Wen &amp; Frodin, widely consumed as a vegetable and tea-like beverage, is commonly cultivated in the northern regions of Vietnam and the western parts of China</em> (<a href="#bb0205" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0205">Yunnan Provincial Standards for Traditional Chinese Medicinal Materials Vol. 6: Yi Medicine III, 2005</a>). As both an edible plant and a source of beneficial health effects, <em>M. delavayi</em> has demonstrated therapeutic effects, including the alleviation of heat, rheumatism, asthmatic coughs, pain, and has shown expectorant and antioxidant activities (<a href="#bb0115" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0115">Luo et al., 2019</a>). In the Yunnan province of China, local residents prepare tea from the tender leaves of <em>M. delavayi</em> using traditional green tea processing techniques, such as kill-green, rolling, and sun-drying. The resulting tea is noted for its rich aroma, golden hue, and sweet taste; it is renowned for its ability to clear heat, soothe the throat, relieve summer heat, and promote digestion (<a href="#bb0240" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0240">Zhou et al., 2017</a>). Among the bioactive properties of interest, its antioxidant activity has attracted significant attention due to its crucial role in daily healthcare. Phytochemical studies have shown that <em>M. delavayi</em> is rich in various compounds, including triterpenoid saponins, phenolic acids, lignans, polysaccharides, and volatile oils (<a href="#bb0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0110">Lu et al., 2023</a>; <a href="#bb0165" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0165">Sun et al., 2019</a>), which contribute to its various effects (<a href="#bb0160" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0160">Sun et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#bb0190" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0190">Wei et al., 2020</a>).</p>
<p id="p0035">Drying is a critical stage that directly affects the qualitative and quantitative characteristics of plants, impacting the aroma, taste, and color of <em>M. delavayi</em>. By reducing the moisture content, drying not only inhibits microbial growth and slows down degradation reactions, thereby extending shelf life and preserving nutritional components, but it also alters the chemical composition and bioactive profiles of the products (<a href="#bb0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0040">Garcia et al., 2021</a>; <a href="#bb0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0085">Korus, 2022</a>; <a href="#bb0220" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0220">Zhao et al., 2020</a>). Given that free radicals can induce lipid peroxidation, reduce membrane permeability, damage membrane proteins, and ultimately lead to cell inactivation, it is crucial to evaluate the antioxidant efficacy of different drying methods for <em>M. delavayi</em> to determine the optimal processing. This poses a challenge in scientifically establishing the best drying technique for producing <em>M. delavayi</em> of the highest quality. Therefore, it is essential to systematically assess how different drying methods influence the chemical components and the antioxidant activity of <em>M. delavayi</em>. Moreover, drying not only facilitates its preservation but also enables the modern food industry to efficiently develop functional foods and nutraceutical products.</p>
<p id="p0040">Using untargeted metabolomics technology, this study comprehensively investigates the chemical differences in processed <em>M. delavayi</em> samples and tracks the dynamic changes of key metabolites during three drying processes: Shade Drying, Hot Air Drying, and Vacuum Freeze-Drying. Based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, key metabolic pathways were identified to elucidate the patterns of compositional and quantitative variations and their impact on antioxidant activity. For determining the optimal processing approach, the influence of each quality parameter was thoroughly assessed. The drying method is crucial for preserving functional constituents and bioactivities in <em>M. delavayi</em>, and process optimization can enhance its health benefits. This study provides a theoretical foundation for improving the drying protocol and product quality of <em>M. delavayi</em>, thereby offering scientific support for further development, storage, transport, origin-specific processing, and quality control (QC) of related products. The findings lay a foundation for enhancing resource utilization by identifying optimal drying treatments for specific practical requirements.</p></section><section id="s0010"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. <p id="p0055">Methanol, acetonitrile, formic acid (≥99%, Fisher Scientific), chloroform (≥99%, Greagent), <em>n</em>-hexane (≥98.5%, CNW), and pyridine (≥99%, Aladdin); <em>N</em>,<em>O</em>-bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (BSTFA, ≥95%, TCI); standards such as rutin (≥98%), gallic acid (≥98%), oleanolic acid (≥98%), and anhydrous glucose (≥99.9%) were purchased from the National Institutes for Food and Drug Control, Beijing, China. Bovine serum albumin, vitamin C (VC), vanillin, Coomassie Brilliant Blue G250, <em>O</em>-methylhydroxylamine hydrochloride (≥97%), and Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (≥99.7%) were supplied by Macklin (Shanghai, China). Other conventional chemical reagents, including aluminum nitrate, sodium nitrite, sodium carbonate, and sodium hydroxide, were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).</p></section><section id="s0030"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. Phytochemicals</h3>
<section id="s0035"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4.1. Total flavonoid content determination (TFC)</h4>
<p id="p0060">The TFC assay was conducted as described by <a href="#bb0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0100">Li et al. (2025)</a>. Briefly, rutin standard solutions of varying concentrations and <em>M. delavayi</em> extract solutions were prepared in 25 mL colorimetric tubes. To each tube, 1 mL of 5% sodium nitrite solution was added and mixed thoroughly, followed by an incubation period of 6 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of 10% aluminum nitrate was added, followed by mixing and a further 6 min of standing. The reaction was terminated by adding 10 mL of 4% sodium hydroxide, and the total volume was adjusted to 25 mL using 40% ethanol. After a reaction time of 15 min, the absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a spectrophotometer (BioSpectrometer, Eppendorf, Germany). TFC results were expressed as milligrams of rutin equivalents (RE) per gram of dried extract (mg RE/g DW).</p></section><section id="s0040"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4.2. Total phenolic content determination (TPC)</h4>
<p id="p0065">TPC was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method as outlined by <a href="#bb0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0170">Sun et al. (2023)</a>. In this procedure, 1 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 5 mL of 10% Na<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> were added to the diluted sample extract. After thorough mixing, the reaction was allowed to proceed in the dark for 1 h. The TPC was quantified by constructing standard curves with different concentrations of gallic acid at 760 nm. The TPC values were expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of dry weight sample (mg GAE/g DW).</p></section><section id="s0045"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4.3. Total soluble sugars content determination (TSC)</h4>
<p id="p0070">TSC was determined using the phenol‑sulfuric acid method detailed by <a href="#bb0140" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0140">Peng et al. (2024)</a>. Initially, diluted sample extracts (1 mL) were combined with 1 mL of 5% phenol solution and 5 mL of sulfuric acid. The mixture was allowed to react for 20 min, and the absorbance was measured at 490 nm. Quantification was conducted using a standard curve. TSC was expressed as glucose equivalents (GE) per gram of dry weight (mg GE/g DW).</p></section><section id="s0050"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4.4. Total triterpenoids content determination (TTC)</h4>
<p id="p0075">TTC was determined according to the method reported by <a href="#bb0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0130">Nakagawa et al. (2018)</a>. Briefly, 1.0 mL of supernatant was mixed with 1.0 mL of freshly prepared 5% vanillin-acetic acid solution and 2.0 mL of sulfuric acid. After thorough mixing, the solution was incubated at 60 °C for 20 min, immediately cooled in an ice bath for 10 min, and supplemented with 5 mL of glacial acetic acid, followed by a reaction period at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 550 nm, and quantification was performed based on an oleanolic acid standard curve. The results were expressed as oleanolic acid equivalents (OAE) per gram of dry weight sample (mg OAE/g DW).</p></section><section id="s0055"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4.5. Total protein content determination (TPNC)</h4>
<p id="p0080">Through the Kjeldahl method, the protein content was determined from the nitrogen content (<a href="#bb0200" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0200">Xiong et al., 2021</a>). The digested samples were distilled after the treatment with 10 mL of 40% <em>w</em>/<em>v</em> sodium hydroxide solution. The resultant distillate was collected in a 2% w/v boric acid solution and titrated using a 0.01400 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution. The titration endpoint was identified through potentiometry. Using an automatic Kjeldahl analyzer (KDN; NANBEI Instrument, Zhengzhou, China), the crude protein content was calculated from the measured nitrogen content. Specifically, 6.25 served as the nitrogen-protein conversion factor. These parameters were quantified using standard analytical methods as outlined by AOAC (<a href="#bb0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0005">AOAC, 2000</a>).</p></section></section><section id="s0060"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Antioxidant activity</h3>
<p id="p0085">Commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Research Institute Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) were employed to assess the DPPH, ABTS, hydroxyl free radical scavenging activities, and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) of <em>M. delavayi</em>. VC served as the positive control, and gradient dilution was used to achieve various concentrations.</p>
<p id="p0090">The DPPH assay was conducted according to the procedure described by <a href="#bb0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0095">Li et al. (2025)</a>. Specifically, 1 mL of the sample extract was combined with 4 mL of a 10<sup>−4</sup> mol/L 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical solution. This mixture was incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min, following which the absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a microplate reader (Rayto Life and Analytical Sciences Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China).</p>
<p id="p0095">In the ABTS assay, as per the protocol by <a href="#bb0235" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0235">Zhou et al. (2025)</a>, 200 μL of the diluted sample extract was added to 1 mL of ABTS working solution within a 2 mL centrifuge tube. The tube was incubated at 37 °C for 5 min, and the absorbance was subsequently measured at 734 nm using a spectrophotometer.</p>
<p id="p0100">Hydroxyl radical scavenging activity was assessed following the method described by <a href="#bb0125" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0125">Mensah et al. (2023)</a>. Briefly, 100 μL of the sample extract (5 mg/mL) was mixed with 1 mL of 9 mmol/L FeSO₄ solution and 0.5 mL of 9 mmol/L salicylic acid-ethanol solution. The reaction commenced upon the addition of 1 mL of 8.8 mmol/L H₂O₂ and proceeded with incubation in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min. The absorbance of the resulting mixture was then measured at 536 nm.</p>
<p id="p0105">FRAP values were determined using the protocol reported by <a href="#bb0185" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0185">Wang et al. (2022)</a>. The FRAP reagent was prepared by combining 30 mM sodium acetate buffer, 10 mM 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TPTZ), and 20 mM FeCl₃ in a volume ratio of 10:1:1. Subsequently, 0.2 mL of the sample extract was mixed with 6 mL of the freshly prepared FRAP reagent. The reaction mixture was then incubated in the dark for 30 min before the absorbance was measured at 593 nm.</p></section><section id="s0065"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. UPLC-MS/MS analysis of non-volatile metabolites in <em>M. delavayi</em> with different drying methods</h3>
<section id="s0070"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6.1. Sample preparation</h4>
<p id="p0110">30 mg of each sample was aliquoted into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes, along with two stainless steel beads and 600 μL of a methanol-water solution (v/v = 7:3) spiked with mixed internal standards at 4 μg/mL. The tubes were snap-frozen at −40 °C for 2 min before undergoing bead-based homogenization at 45 Hz for 2 min. After homogenization, the mixtures were ultrasonicated in an ice-water bath for 30 min and subsequently stored at −40 °C overnight. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C using a high-speed refrigerated centrifuge (TGL-16MS, Shanghai Luxiangyi Centrifuge Instrument Co., Ltd.). A 150 μL aliquot of the supernatant was collected using a syringe, passed through a 0.22 μm organic phase membrane filter, and dispensed into LC vials. Filtered samples were maintained at −80 °C pending LC-MS analysis.</p></section><section id="s0075"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6.2. UPLC-MS/MS analysis conditions</h4>
<p id="p0115">Liquid chromatography was conducted on an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled with a high-resolution mass spectrometer (Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class Plus/Thermo QE HF). Separation occurred on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters), which was maintained at 45 °C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water with 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile. The gradient program was established as follows: from 0 to 2 min, 5% B; from 2 to 4 min, 5–30% B; from 4 to 8 min, 30–50% B; from 8 to 10 min, 50–80% B; from 10 to 14 min, 80–100% B; from 14 to 15 min, maintained at 100% B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, with an injection volume of 3 μL.</p>
<p id="p0120">Mass spectrometric detection was performed using an electrospray ionization (ESI) source in both the positive and negative modes. The spray voltages were set at 3800 V (positive) and 3000 V (negative). The capillary temperature was maintained at 320 °C, and the auxiliary gas heater temperature was set at 350 °C. Sheath and auxiliary gas flows were set at 35 Arb and 8 Arb, respectively, with the lens RF level at 50. Full MS scans were acquired over an <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> range of 100–1200 at a resolution of 60,000, while MS/MS scans were conducted at a resolution of 15,000. Collision energy varied from 10 to 40 eV.</p></section></section><section id="s0080"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.7. GC–MS/MS analysis of volatile metabolites in <em>M. delavayi</em> with different drying methods</h3>
<section id="s0085"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.7.1. Sample preparation</h4>
<p id="p0125">A 120 μL aliquot of the supernatant from the UPLC-MS/MS sample preparation was transferred to a glass derivatization vial and dried under vacuum in a centrifugal concentrator. The residue was reconstituted with 80 μL of methoxyamine hydrochloride in pyridine (15 mg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min on an orbital shaker (THZ-82, Shanghai Lichen Bangxi Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.) to achieve methoximation. After cooling, 50 μL of BSTFA and 20 μL of <em>n</em>-hexane were added, followed by 10 μL of a mixed internal standard solution. The mixture was derivatized at 70 °C for 60 min, allowed to equilibrate to room temperature for 30 min, and then analyzed by GC–MS. QC samples were prepared by combining equal volumes of extracts from all biological samples.</p></section><section id="s0090"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.7.2. GC–MS/MS analysis conditions</h4>
<p id="p0130">Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis was performed using an Agilent 7890B-5977 A system equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). High-purity helium (≥99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The injection port temperature was maintained at 260 °C, and 1 μL of the sample was injected in splitless mode with a 5.5-min solvent delay. The oven temperature program was set as follows: an initial temperature of 60 °C (held for 0.5 min), increased to 125 °C at a rate of 8 °C/min, then to 210 °C at the same rate, raised to 270 °C at 15 °C/min, and finally to 305 °C at 20 °C/min, where it was held for 5 min.</p>
<p id="p0135">For mass spectrometry, the electron ionization (EI) source temperature was set to 230 °C, and the quadrupole temperature was 150 °C, with an electron energy of 70 eV. A full scan mode (SCAN) was employed, covering a mass range of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 50–500.</p></section></section><section id="s0095"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.8. Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="p0140">Metabolite data were log<sub>2</sub>-transformed and normalized to improve the distribution of data. Subsequently, multivariate statistical analyses, including hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), principal component analysis (PCA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were applied to non-volatile and volatile metabolite profiles. Significantly differential metabolites were screened by integrating variable importance in projection (VIP) values from the OPLS-DA model with independent samples <em>t</em>-tests. Furthermore, univariate statistical significance (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) was confirmed through one-way ANOVA using SPSS 20.0 and GraphPad Prism 8. All results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (<em>n</em> = 3).</p>
<p id="p0145">This study used three biological replicates (n = 3) per group, which is acceptable for exploratory metabolomics focusing on differential metabolite screening and pathway construction. To ensure robust statistical analysis with <em>n</em> = 3 biological replicates per group, statistical power was enhanced through the combined multivariate analyses with strict screening criteria, and biological variability was minimized by controlling plant growth and sampling conditions. Furthermore, we acknowledge inherent plant variability as a limitation. Notably, n = 3 indicates main metabolic trends but not all individual differences.</p></section></section><section id="s0100"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results and discussion</h2>
<section id="s0105"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Total flavonoid, total phenolic, total soluble sugars, total triterpenoids, and total protein content</h3>
<p id="p0150">The drying conditions significantly affected the flavonoid content (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>A). The VFD samples displayed the highest TFC at 100.56 mg/g, significantly exceeding those of the SD group (82.78 mg/g) and the HAD group (53.97 mg/g). The notably lower TFC in the HAD group was likely due to thermal oxidative degradation. This observation is consistent with reports indicating that flavonoids, especially those with phenolic hydroxyl groups and aldehyde structures, are susceptible to degradation under high-temperature conditions (<a href="#bb0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0045">Gebrie et al., 2025</a>).</p>

This value was significantly higher than those observed in the SD and HAD samples, in descending order of VFD &gt; SD &gt; HAD. The superior performance of VFD was attributed to the synergistic effects of low-temperature and high-vacuum conditions, which effectively mitigate thermal and oxidative damage, thereby preserving phenolic integrity and maximizing retention.</p>
<p id="p0160">The VFD group also exhibited the highest total soluble sugar content (58.25 mg/g), which was 22.80% and 24.05% higher than that of the HAD and SD groups, respectively (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>C). The SD and HAD groups displayed similar values without significant differences. The superior performance of VFD can be attributed to its low-temperature vacuum environment, which almost completely inhibits enzymatic activity and chemical reactions, significantly reducing the degradation of total soluble sugars during the drying process.</p>
<p id="p0165">VFD demonstrated the strongest preservation capacity for total triterpenoids, with contents reaching 61.82 mg/g (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>D). These values were significantly higher than those in the SD and HAD groups, which also differed significantly from each other. This outcome is primarily attributed to the fact that the low-temperature environment of VFD effectively prevents thermal degradation and enzymatic reactions, while the vacuum environment avoids oxidative loss. Additionally, by removing moisture through sublimation, VFD minimized carry-over loss.</p>
<p id="p0170">The results from the Kjeldahl method indicated that <em>M. delavayi</em> exhibited the highest protein content in the SD group (89.09 mg/g), with comparable values observed in the VFD (84.49 mg/g) and HAD (84.31 mg/g) groups (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>E). The superior protein content in the SD group was attributed to the mild drying conditions, which preserved the integrity of macromolecular proteins. In contrast, HAD disrupted the protein's hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, leading to peptide bond cleavage, or protein hydrolysis. VFD displayed slightly lower total nitrogen retention than SD, likely due to partial nitrogen loss caused by ice crystal formation.</p></section><section id="s0110"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Effect of drying on antioxidant activity</h3>
<p id="p0175">To investigate the effects of three drying methods (SD, HAD, and VFD) on the antioxidant potential of <em>M. delavayi</em>, we assessed DPPH free radical scavenging activity, ABTS free radical scavenging activity, hydroxyl free radical scavenging activity, and total antioxidant capacity using the FRAP assay. <a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>F-I compares the effects of different drying techniques on the antioxidant capacity of <em>M. delavayi</em>. Notably, the VFD-treated samples exhibited significantly higher antioxidant activity compared to those treated by SD and HAD, with no significant difference observed between the latter two. Specifically, the VFD-treated samples demonstrated DPPH radical and hydroxyl radical scavenging rates of 88.02% and 90.19%, respectively, in addition to a ferric reducing power and ABTS radical scavenging capacity of 72.02 mmol/L and 1738.37 mmol/L, respectively. This enhancement is likely due to the greater preservation of antioxidant compounds by VFD. Furthermore, the overall order of antioxidant capacity across the three drying conditions was consistent with the trends observed in the contents of total phenolics and flavonoids, indicating that these compounds primarily contribute to the significant antioxidant activity of <em>M. delavayi</em>. These findings suggest the potential of <em>M. delavayi</em>, particularly when processed by VFD, as a source for functional products with enhanced antioxidant properties.</p></section><section id="s0115"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3. UPLC-MS/MS analysis of non-volatile metabolites in <em>M. delavayi</em> treated with different drying methods</h3>
<section id="s0120"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3.1. Analysis of non-volatile metabolites in <em>M. delavayi</em> by total ion chromatogram (TIC) under different drying treatments</h4>
<p id="p0180">As indicated by untargeted metabolomics analysis employing UPLC-Q-TOF-MS, the TICs of <em>M. delavayi</em> samples under various drying treatments displayed similar metabolic profiles (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>A). Additionally, the retention times and peak intensities of metabolites demonstrated high consistency between the positive and negative ion modes. Alongside the tight overlap of TICs derived from QC samples (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>B), this finding underscored the analytical stability and validated the high reliability and reproducibility of the experimental data.</p>

Overview of non-volatile metabolites in <em>M. delavayi</em> with different drying treatments</h4>
<p id="p0185">To elucidate the metabolic alterations induced by different drying methods, this study, utilizing untargeted metabolomics, identified 1311 metabolites from <em>M. delavayi</em> samples across the three drying methods in both positive and negative ion modes. These metabolites were categorized into five primary groups (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>A): phenylpropanoids and polyketides (445, 33.94%), organic acids and derivatives (380, 28.99%), lipids and lipid-like molecules (342, 26.09%), organic oxygen compounds (138, 10.53%), and organic heterocyclic compounds (6, 0.46%). The categories of phenylpropanoids/polyketides, organic acids and derivatives, and lipids and lipid-like molecules were predominant in all samples, suggesting that carbohydrates, carbonyl compounds, and lipids are major precursors of non-volatile compounds primarily involved in lipid oxidation and Maillard reactions (<a href="#bb0215" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0215">Zhang et al., 2023</a>; <a href="#bb0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0100">Li et al., 2025</a>). A quantitative analysis of metabolite abundances across the drying methods is depicted in <a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>B. Although the overall retention of non-volatile compounds was comparable across the methods, significant variability in the abundances of individual components highlighted the substantial impact of drying conditions on the chemical composition and content. These variances are likely attributable to selective impacts on heat-sensitive components through thermal stress, oxidation, or physical alteration. Notably, the total relative abundance of all non-volatile metabolites was highest in samples treated with VFD, followed by SD, and was lowest in HAD-treated samples.</p>

Multivariate statistical analysis of non-volatile metabolites in <em>M. delavayi</em> with different drying treatments</h4>
<p id="p0190">The correlation coefficient (r) was utilized to assess the reproducibility between QC samples and test samples. A value approaching 1 indicates increased stability of the methodology. As depicted in <a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>C, data from different QC groups demonstrated strong consistency, with all correlation coefficients surpassing 0.99, thereby confirming the high stability of the experimental procedures. To ascertain whether the metabolite profiles could effectively distinguish among different drying method groups and to further examine the diversity of the overall metabolite profile while providing a comparative interpretation, PCA was initially applied to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset and to visualize variations between groups. PCA disclosed distinct separations among the various drying treatments, indicating significant metabolic changes (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>D). PC1 and PC2 accounted for 47.5% and 42.6% of the total variance, respectively. The close clustering of the SD, HAD, and VFD sample groups suggested high reproducibility and analytical stability. HCA further supported this finding by categorizing the samples into three major clusters, thus reinforcing the conclusion that drying treatments significantly affect the metabolite composition of <em>M. delavayi</em> (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>E).</p>
<p id="p0195">Supervised multivariate statistical analysis employing OPLS-DA was conducted on <em>M. delavayi</em> samples from the three drying treatments to eliminate irrelevant variations and to identify differential metabolites. The analysis revealed notable separation of metabolites among the drying method groups (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>F–H). Model reliability and potential overfitting were evaluated using sevenfold cross-validation and 200 permutation tests (<a href="#t0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). All groups exhibited R<sup>2</sup>X values greater than 0.9 and R<sup>2</sup>Y equal to 1, indicating an excellent model fit to the data and no evidence of overfitting. These results verify that the constructed models are valid and that the data possess strong explanatory power. Overall, the OPLS-DA models demonstrated robust predictive performance, facilitating reliable identification of differentially expressed metabolites for further analysis.</p>

These metabolites in <em>M. delavayi</em> include 319 identified as phenylpropanoids and polyketides, primarily comprising 155 flavonoids, 44 cinnamic acids and derivatives, 30 isoflavones, and 26 coumarins and derivatives, among other compounds. Phenylpropanoids and polyketides, which are abundant in the human diet, possess antioxidant properties and exhibit biological activities such as antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects, potentially reducing disease risk (<a href="#bb0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0150">Shen et al., 2022</a>; <a href="#bb0250" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0250">Zuo et al., 2024</a>). The results indicated that among the three drying methods, VFD is most effective in preserving phenylpropanoids and polyketides, with flavonoids being the most notably retained. The low-temperature environment of VFD helps suppress thermal degradation and enzymatic oxidation. Flavonoids, particularly in their glycosidic forms, are susceptible to glycosidic bond cleavage or oxidative decomposition at high temperatures. VFD prevents high-temperature-induced structural damage, thereby significantly maintaining the structural integrity of flavonoid compounds.</p>
<p id="p0230">Organic acids and their derivatives comprised 202 differential metabolites, predominantly including 151 amino acids, peptides, and analogues, as well as 20 medium-chain hydroxy acids and derivatives. Both HAD and VFD treatments resulted in significant enrichment of amino acids and their derivatives. Research has shown that heating facilitates protein degradation, thereby promoting the generation of peptides or amino acids (<a href="#bb0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0105">Liu et al., 2022</a>; <a href="#bb0210" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0210">Zeng et al., 2022</a><span class="text-underline">)</span>, which may account for the improved retention of amino acids and derivatives observed in HAD.</p>
<p id="p0235">This phenomenon can be attributed to the Maillard reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars in <em>M. delavayi</em>. Heating facilitates the generation of free amino acids from proteins. <em>M. delavayi</em> may undergo proteolysis, lipolysis, or enzymatic oxidation when exposed to oxygen, heat, or the action of enzymes. Proteins are hydrolyzed by proteases, yielding large peptides, which are then further hydrolyzed into small peptides and free amino acids (<a href="#bb0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0145">Rivas-Cañedo et al., 2021</a>). Although both reactions occur, when the amount of amino acids produced from the protein exceeds those consumed in the Maillard reaction, the final total amino acid concentration is higher than that in other samples. Similar results have been reported in studies by <a href="#bb0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0010">Bi et al. (2024)</a>, justifying the attribution of amino acid accumulation to the predominance of protein hydrolysis.</p>
<p id="p0240">The lipid profile comprised 156 differential metabolites, primarily consisting of isoprenol lipids, and included 128 terpenoids, specifically 46 triterpenoids, 34 diterpenoids, 33 sesquiterpenoids, and 15 monoterpenoids. The results indicated that SD was the most effective method for retaining these compounds, followed by VFD, and finally HAD, demonstrating that the drying methods significantly influence the lipid composition of metabolites in <em>M. delavayi</em>.</p>
<p id="p0245">Additionally, the analysis identified 62 carbohydrates, particularly vulnerable to thermal degradation, including monosaccharides and disaccharides, susceptible to hydrolysis, caramelization, or oxidation. The consistently low temperatures used in VFD effectively prevent these degradation pathways, thus promoting the enrichment of carbohydrates. Consequently, carbohydrate levels were significantly higher under VFD, while reductions were noted in both SD and HAD treatments.</p>
<p id="p0250">In summary, the VFD treatment proved more advantageous for retaining components such as flavonoids, coumarins and their derivatives, cinnamic acids and their derivatives, and carbohydrates, which displayed the highest relative abundance. Conversely, the HAD treatment resulted in a more pronounced enrichment of amino acid metabolites. In terms of terpenoid retention, the SD treatment exhibited distinct advantages, whereas the HAD treatment was least effective for most metabolites, likely due to the degradation of thermally unstable compounds under high-temperature conditions.</p>
<p id="p0255">Specifically, the SD vs. HAD group contained 436 differential metabolites, including 233 up-regulated and 203 down-regulated substances. This may be attributed to the slower moisture loss during the early stages of drying in the SD treatment, which facilitates the production of a broader array of metabolites (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>D).</p>
<p id="p0260">The SD vs. VFD group contained 448 differential metabolites, with 108 substances up-regulated and 340 down-regulated in the SD treatment. The number of up-regulated metabolites was higher in VFD, demonstrating superior retention through the VFD process by inhibiting their degradation, loss, or transformation (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>E).</p>
<p id="p0265">The HAD vs. VFD group contained 434 differential metabolites, with 125 substances up-regulated and 309 down-regulated in HAD (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>F). This disparity can be attributed to the oxidation and degradation of certain substances during high-temperature drying, leading to a reduction in metabolites.</p></section><section id="s0145"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3.6. <div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/t0030/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section><p id="p0275">The retention efficiency of different drying methods on phenylpropanoids and polyketides, particularly flavonoids, in <em>M. delavayi</em> exhibited significant variations. Overall, the retention capacities of the three drying methods ranked as follows: VFD &gt; SD &gt; HAD. Among these, kaempferol and its derivatives exert multiple effects within the nervous system, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-ischemic injury, anticonvulsant, antidepressant, and anti-dementia activities (<a href="#bb0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0090">Li et al., 2021</a>). Among the differential metabolites, Kaempferol 3-[2Gal-(6‴-feruloylglucosyl)-robinobioside] and Kaempferide 3-rhamnoside-7-(6″-succinylglucoside) were both significantly downregulated in HAD compared to VFD, with HAD causing significant losses due to high-temperature degradation. Consistent with previous studies (<a href="#bb0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0065">Hu et al., 2023</a>), prolonged exposure to high temperatures during hot-air drying induces hydrolytic degradation of flavonoid glycosides, resulting in reduced flavonoid content. Other kaempferol derivatives, such as Kaempferol 3-(6-acetylgalactoside), Kaempferol 3-(6″-acetylglucoside), Kaempferol 3-[2‴,3‴,5‴-triacetyl-alpha-L-arabinofuranosyl-(1 → 6)-glucoside, and Kaempferol 3-(2″-feruloylglucosyl)-(1 → 2)-(6″-malonylglucoside) were down-regulated in both the SD vs. VFD and HAD vs. VFD comparisons. Similarly, quercetin glycoside derivatives, which exhibit diverse biological effects including anticancer and antioxidant activities (<a href="#bb0080" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0080">Kashyap et al., 2016</a>), were notably affected. For instance, Quercetin 3-(6″-malonylglucoside)-7-rhamnoside and Quercetin 3-glucosyl-(1 → 4)-xylosyl-(1 → 4)-rhamnoside were down-regulated in HAD vs. VFD. The fold change values further confirmed the superior preservation of flavonoid glycosides by VFD.</p>
<p id="p0280">Furthermore, amino acid-related metabolites among organic acids and derivatives were significantly affected by the drying methods. For peptide metabolites, such as Val-Leu-Ile, Ile-Val-Val, and Pro-Ala-Leu, significant down-regulation occurred in SD vs. HAD and SD vs. VFD, with the more pronounced change in SD vs. HAD based on FC values. The observed accumulation of amino acids and their derivatives in HAD-treated samples, consistent with the literature (<a href="#bb0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0070">Jia et al., 2025</a>), indicates the activation of pathways related to amino acid biosynthesis and protein hydrolysis. Notably, this apparent increase in free amino acids represents only a partial picture. Concurrent degradation pathways triggered by severe thermal processing are crucial to consider. As evidenced by prior research, while protein hydrolysis at high temperatures increases total amino acids, severe thermal treatment can facilitate protein carbonylation, isomerization of amino acid residues, as well as the formation of Maillard reaction products (<a href="#bb0175" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0175">Tang et al., 2021</a>).</p>
<p id="p0285">The distinct profiles of amino acids and peptides observed among HAD, SD, and VFD likely represent the net outcome of the dynamic interplay between protein hydrolysis and the Maillard reaction. These processes exhibit a complex, substrate- and enzyme-specific bidirectional regulatory relationship. Hydrolysis facilitates the Maillard reaction by providing small-molecule peptides and free amino acids (<a href="#bb0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0025">Dai et al., 2024</a>), while the Maillard reaction inversely affects hydrolysis through the glycation of amino acid residues, with impacts varying according to protease specificity (<a href="#bb0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0030">Deng et al., 2017</a>). Together, these processes synergistically promote the formation of flavor and functional substances. The more pronounced changes in peptide levels in the SD vs. HAD group suggest that the higher temperature in HAD not only enhances hydrolysis but also accelerates the aforementioned degradation pathways, leading to a complex metabolic footprint. Ultimately, the differences between these processing methods can be attributed to how the specific combination of temperature and processing medium influences the balance between these competing biochemical reactions.</p></section><section id="s0150"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3.7. <p id="p0320">A Venn diagram identified 12 common differential volatile metabolites across all three sample groups (<a href="#f0040" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a>A), which were considered key metabolites among SD, HAD, and VFD samples. The relationships and relative concentrations of these metabolites in <em>M. delavayi</em> samples are depicted in a heatmap (<a href="#t0035" class="usa-link">Table 7</a>, <a href="#f0040" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a>B). Pairwise comparisons revealed that SD vs. HAD, SD vs. VFD, and HAD vs. VFD possessed 10, 6, and 9 unique volatile metabolites, respectively. These results suggest that different drying methods lead to the formation of distinct volatile compounds in <em>M. delavayi</em>, indicating that variations in drying methods exert specific hierarchical regulatory effects on metabolite composition.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/t0035/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section><p id="p0325">To further investigate the differences in volatile compounds among <em>M. delavayi</em> treated with different drying methods, 87 differential metabolites were screened based on the criteria of VIP ≥ 1, FC ≥ 2, and <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05. These primarily included 37 carbohydrates and conjugates, 12 carboxylic acids and their derivatives, 12 lipids and lipid-like molecules, 6 alcohols and polyols, among other compounds. Box plot results for major categories are shown in <a href="#f0040" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a>C. Research has demonstrated that carbohydrates can also influence the retention and release of volatile flavor compounds. Overall, the relative abundances of carbohydrates and their conjugates differed across the groups: the VFD group exhibited levels comparable to the HAD group and significantly higher levels than the SD group. This disparity suggests a plausible mechanism for the observed differences in flavor profiles among the samples. The superior retention of carbohydrates in the VFD and HAD treatments likely contributes to a more robust flavor foundation compared to the SD treatment.</p>
<p id="p0330">Lipid oxidation is a critical source of volatile metabolites, with its decomposition products, such as ketones and alcohols, directly contributing to food flavor and the formation of bioactive signaling molecules (<a href="#bb0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0050">Grebenteuch et al., 2021</a>). Among the three drying methods examined, the VFD group demonstrated the best retention of lipids and lipid-like molecules. This superior retention is likely attributed to the method's low-temperature and low-pressure vacuum conditions, which effectively shield these components from loss or degradation typically caused by high temperatures, oxidation, or hydrolysis. In contrast, during HAD, high temperatures not only accelerate lipid oxidation but also promote Maillard reactions. Lipid oxidation and decomposition are pivotal pathways leading to the formation of alcohol compounds, a finding consistent with previous reports and our observation that alcohol compounds were significantly more abundant in the HAD group than in the other two groups (<a href="#bb0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0075">Jia et al., 2023</a>). Esters, formed via the esterification of carboxylic acids and alcohols, possess strong volatility and aromatic properties, serving as key flavor components in foods, fragrances, and cosmetics. Examples include Succinic Acid, L-Pipecolic Acid, O-Acetylserine, (2s)-2-(Cyanoamino)Propanoic Acid, 3-Amino-4-Hydroxybutanoic Acid, 2-Aminohexanedioic Acid. The SD group exhibited the highest relative abundance of carboxylic acids and their derivatives, followed by the HAD group. These results underscore significant differences in the effects of drying methods on volatile metabolites, indicating that the selection of drying processes should comprehensively consider component retention, energy consumption, and final product quality requirements.</p>
<p id="p0335">As illustrated in the volcano plots (<a href="#f0045" class="usa-link">Fig. 9</a>A), differential volatile metabolites detected in comparisons between SD vs. HAD, SD vs. VFD, and HAD vs. VFD were 55 (24 upregulated, 31 downregulated), 56 (26 upregulated, 30 downregulated), and 50 (29 upregulated, 21 downregulated), respectively. The comparison between HAD and VFD was predominated by upregulated metabolites, whereas the other two comparisons primarily showed downregulated metabolites. Our analysis revealed a significant increase in the concentration of key volatile compounds in samples treated with HAD compared to those processed by SD and VFD. This enhancement can be attributed to the intensive thermal energy provided by HAD, which, as reported in the literature (<a href="#bb0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0065">Hu et al., 2023</a>), accelerates water loss and increases the activity of endogenous hydrolases, such as glycosidases and lipoxygenases. The heat provided by HAD not only accelerated water loss in fresh leaves but also increased the activity of endogenous hydrolases, thereby promoting the formation of volatile compounds. A close metabolic association exists between amino acid accumulation and volatile formation during heat treatment. Drought stress, induced by drying, drives amino acid accumulation through the activation of defense systems and protein degradation (<a href="#bb0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0135">Ogbe et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#bb0230" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0230">Zhao et al., 2021</a>). These accumulated amino acids serve as substrates for volatile formation, which is produced via pathways such as fatty acid oxidation and Strecker degradation. The heat treatment methods jointly affect the levels and components of both by regulating enzyme activity and reaction time, providing a reasonable explanation for the observed differences among the drying groups in this study.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/t0040/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section><p id="p0355"><span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-Glutamine demonstrates the highest retention in the SD samples. In food processing, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-Glutamine can promote the formation of volatile flavor compounds through the Maillard reaction or microbial metabolism. For instance, supplementing chicken with <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-Glutamine increases the content of saturated aldehydes and 1-octen-3-ol, thereby enhancing the aroma quality (<a href="#bb0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0020">Cui et al., 2025</a>). In wine fermentation, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-Glutamine acts as a nitrogen source, influencing the formation of volatile sulfur compounds (<a href="#bb0245" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0245">Zhu et al., 2023</a>). Phytol, a terpenoid, through its degradation products, serves as precursors for plant volatile organic compounds associated with grassy or green leaf odors (<a href="#bb0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0035">Dudareva et al., 2013</a>). Myristic Acid, significantly enriched in the VFD samples, is a saturated long-chain fatty acid that produces methyl ketones through microbial metabolism, contributing to cheesy or coconut-like aromas and becoming a key flavor component in fermented foods such as cheese. Moreover, fatty acids are susceptible to oxidation or interactions with other compounds to form flavorants, which are crucial for their distinctive aroma characteristics (<a href="#bb0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0015">Chen et al., 2019</a>). During drying treatments, dynamic changes in these key volatile differential metabolites significantly impact the product's aroma, quality, and bioactivity.</p>
<p id="p0360">In this study, we demonstrate that differential metabolites exhibiting elevated levels in the HAD group, such as Rhamnose and 1,2,3-Butanetriol, primarily serve as aroma precursors rather than directly contributing to desirable volatiles. For example, glycoside components like Rhamnose necessitate hydrolysis by endogenous or exogenous glycosidases to release free aromatic compounds. Their high accumulation in HAD thus provides a material basis for flavor diversity, but does not directly enhance aroma quality. Additionally, other metabolites that significantly accumulate in HAD include Phosphoric Acid, Succinic Acid, Urea, Niacinamide, L-Lyxulose, L-Pipecolic Acid, Rhamnose, 3r-Hydroxy-Butanoic Acid, Pyrogallol, L-Threonic Acid, 2-Hydroxyglutaric Acid, 3-β-D-Galactosyl-Sn-Glycerol, Melezitose, 2-Pyrrolidinone, 1,2,3-Butanetriol, L-(−)-Fucose, and Methyl α-D-Galactopyranoside. These can be categorized into three functional groups: (<a href="#bb0205" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0205">Yunnan Provincial Food and Drug Administration, 2005</a>) desirable flavor contributors (e.g., Succinic Acid, which imparts mild umami and nutty notes, and Rhamnose and Methyl α-D-Galactopyranoside as precursors for floral, baked terpenoid, and phenolic volatiles); (<a href="#bb0115" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0115">Luo et al., 2019</a>) substances related to quality deterioration (e.g., Pyrogallol, a phenolic oxidation product that induces bitter and burnt off-flavors; 2-Hydroxyglutaric Acid, which indicates metabolic imbalance). Collectively, a higher metabolite content in HAD does not equate to superior flavor quality, as this depends on the ratio of desirable to undesirable components and requires validation through sensory analysis and gas chromatography-olfactometry (GC-O). The identification of key differential metabolites provides insights for optimizing the drying process and elucidating the mechanisms of metabolite-flavor correlations.</p>
<p id="p0365">This study also reveals that drying methods significantly alter the metabolic profile of <em>M. delavayi</em>, particularly influencing amino acids and their related flavor precursors. SD is optimal for preserving flavor-enhancing amino acids like <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-Glutamine, making it suitable for producing products of high aroma quality. In contrast, HAD and VFD lead to the loss of some amino acids but promote the formation of distinct flavor compounds through pathways induced by stress and lipid oxidation, respectively. VFD effectively preserves a broad spectrum of native metabolites, including primary sugars (e.g., <span class="font-variant-small-caps">d</span>-glucose, maltose), organic acids (e.g., shikimic, tartaric acids), nucleosides (e.g., uridine), and flavor precursors (e.g., 4-Hydroxycinnamic acid), highlighting its superior ability to minimize thermal and oxidative degradation and retain the original phytochemical composition (<a href="#bb0180" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0180">Tong et al., 2025</a>). Conversely, HAD promotes the formation of specific thermal degradation and reaction products, such as polyphenol pyrolysis markers (e.g., Pyrogallol), organic acid degradation intermediates (e.g., Succinic acid, 2-Hydroxyglutaric acid), and Maillard reaction precursors (e.g., glucosamine), which are indicative of heat-driven chemical pathways including the Maillard reaction and Strecker degradation. Notably, the accumulation of Succinic acid, a key organic acid contributing to broth freshness, has been specifically linked to thermal activation during HAD processing (<a href="#bb0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0195">Wen et al., 2022</a>). Therefore, the selection of a drying method significantly influences the final chemical signature by directing specific metabolic and thermal reaction pathways. These findings provide a metabolic basis for selecting appropriate drying techniques to tailor the flavor and bioactivity of medicinal and edible plants, with differential impacts on the retention, transformation, or degradation of volatile metabolites mediated by temperature, oxygen exposure, and enzyme activity. These processes are closely linked to biological implications such as plant stress responses, antioxidant defense, and the regulation of secondary metabolism.</p>
<p id="p0370">In this study, a set of enhanced volatiles and degradation byproducts were identified that warrant special attention. The former category comprises metabolically active products produced under mild processing conditions, which impart positive flavor attributes or functional benefits to the samples. These include <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-Glutamine, which plays a role in amino acid metabolism, as well as 3-hydroxybutyric acid and levulinic acid, both of which are involved in carbon metabolism. Notably, Phytol, a volatile derivative of chlorophyll degradation involved in lipid biosynthesis, is a viscous liquid distinguished by its subtle floral-balsamic aroma (<a href="#bb0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0120">McGinty et al., 2010</a>). According to recent literature (<a href="#bb0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0155">Su et al., 2022</a>), this metabolite enhances the desirable floral flavor notes that are characteristic of brick tea, a significant processed tea variety. The VFD method, which operates at low temperature and pressure, effectively minimizes enzymatic inactivation and non-physiological oxidation, thereby preserving these metabolites that reflect the intrinsic quality of the samples. In contrast, the latter category consists of non-characteristic byproducts generated through excessive oxidation under severe conditions. Representative compounds in this category include 1-pentadecanol, 3-β-D-galactosyl-sn-glycerol, β-eudesmol, myrtenol, and stearyl alcohol. These compounds are markedly reduced in VFD samples but are abnormally accumulated in HAD samples, a phenomenon associated with high-temperature-induced lipid over-oxidation. This distinction highlights the critical impact of processing conditions on the composition and stability of volatile metabolites.</p></section><section id="s0180"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">3.4.5. KEGG enrichment analysis of differential metabolites</h4>
<p id="p0375">The pathway bar chart displaying the smallest P-values is depicted in <a href="#f0045" class="usa-link">Fig. 9</a>C. The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the differential metabolites in comparisons between SD vs. HAD, SD vs. VFD, and HAD vs. VFD were enriched in 41, 38, and 47 pathways, respectively. The differential metabolites across all three comparison groups were significantly enriched in pathways such as galactose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and ABC transporter pathways. Galactose metabolism plays a crucial role in regulating energy homeostasis via the galacturonic acid pathway. The notable accumulation of Methyl α-D-Galactopyranoside and L-(−)-Fucose in the HAD group suggests that high temperatures may enhance the activity of the galacturonic acid pathway. The increased activity in the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway is likely linked to cellular stress responses, such as when glycolysis becomes the primary energy source under hypoxic conditions (<a href="#bb0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0055">Heiden et al., 2009</a>). The significant enrichment of <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-Glutamine in the VFD group indicates that low temperature and reduced oxygen conditions may promote the supply of glycolytic substrates and enhance glutamine catabolism to provide gluconeogenic precursors, such as α-ketoglutaric acid. The ABC transporter pathway significantly affects cellular physiology and pathology by regulating metabolite transport, promoting drug efflux, and remodeling metabolic networks (<a href="#bb0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0060">Higgins, 2007</a>). This study demonstrates that drying methods distinctly influence metabolite profiles by differentially regulating pathways associated with energy metabolism and transmembrane transport.</p></section></section><section id="s0185"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.5. Correlation analysis</h3>
<section id="s0190"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">3.5.1. Enrichment pathways and metabolic network construction of differential metabolites</h4>
<p id="p0380">The comprehensive flavor profile of <em>M. delavayi</em> is influenced by both non-volatile and volatile metabolites. Non-volatile metabolites, such as organic acids, amino acids, and sugars, modulate the taste, aftertaste, and overall sensory experience in consumers. Meanwhile, volatile metabolites, including esters and alcohols, primarily define the aromatic characteristics of <em>M. delavayi</em>. The metabolic activities of microorganisms during drying processes impact the production of both metabolite types. Given the potential of non-volatile metabolites to serve as precursors for volatile flavor compounds (<a href="#bb0225" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0225">Zhao et al., 2025</a>), this study compared 59 non-volatile metabolites with a VIP score exceeding 2.0 and 12 volatile metabolites with a VIP score greater than 1.0. Spearman's rank correlation analysis was employed, applying criteria of |ρ| &gt; 0.8 and <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05. Based on this, 843 strong correlations with absolute values of correlation coefficients greater than 0.8 were screened, and a tertiary correlation network among key metabolic pathways, non-volatile and volatile metabolites was further constructed (<a href="#f0050" class="usa-link">Fig. 10</a>A). The results showed that differential volatile metabolites and their non-volatile counterparts exhibited 317 significant positive correlations (ρ &gt; 0.8, <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) and 526 strong negative correlations (ρ &lt; −0.8, P &lt; 0.05). Remarkably, Kaempferol 3-(6-acetylgalactoside) and its other derivatives are core metabolites of flavonoid biosynthesis, which established 149 strong correlations with volatile and non-volatile metabolites. This result revealed a potential crosstalk between volatile and non-volatile metabolites in <em>M. delavayi</em> under the regulation of enriched metabolic pathways.</p>

Correlation between phytochemicals and antioxidant activity</h4>
<p id="p0385">This analysis explored the relationship between bioactive components and biological activities in <em>M. delavayi</em> (<a href="#f0050" class="usa-link">Fig. 10</a>B). The results indicate highly significant positive correlations between TFC, TPC, TSC, TTC, and three antioxidant assays, DPPH, ·OH, and FRAP. Specifically, correlation coefficients were found to be 0.96, 0.89, and 0.94 between TFC and DPPH scavenging ability, hydroxyl radical scavenging, and FRAP, respectively (P &lt; 0.05). Similarly, the correlations between TPC and these antioxidant indicators were 0.95, 0.96, and 0.99 (P &lt; 0.05), underscoring the significant role of flavonoids and phenolic compounds in combating oxidative stress. The correlation coefficient between TFC and TPC was determined to be 0.99, indicating a strong intrinsic relationship between these two classes of compounds. Conversely, ABTS radical scavenging activity exhibited relatively weaker correlations with other antioxidant parameters, except for moderate positive correlations with TPC, TSC, TTC, and FRAP. In conclusion, the functional activity of <em>M. delavayi</em> arises from the synergistic effects of multiple components rather than being dominated by a single compound, supporting the concept of multi-component synergistic interactions and specific targeting characteristics in <em>M. delavayi</em>.</p></section><section id="s0200"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">3.5.3. Correlation between metabolites and antioxidant activity</h4>
<p id="p0390">To further investigate the potential antioxidant activity of <em>M. delavayi</em> processed using different drying methods, we analyzed the associations between the detected non-volatile metabolites and antioxidant activities using Pearson's correlation coefficients. This study identified nine key flavonoids that correlated significantly with antioxidant activity (<a href="#f0050" class="usa-link">Fig. 10</a>C). Among these differential compounds, Kaempferol 3-(6-acetylgalactoside), Kaempferol 3-(6″-acetylglucoside), and Kaempferol 3-(2″-feruloylglucosyl)-(1 → 2)-(6″-malonylglucoside) displayed pronounced antioxidant activities in both the FRAP and ·OH assays. Specifically, FRAP exhibited a significant positive correlation with Kaempferol 3-(6-acetylgalactoside) (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.01). Furthermore, ·OH scavenging activity showed significant positive correlations with both Kaempferol 3-(6″-acetylglucoside) and Kaempferol 3-(2″-feruloylglucosyl)-(1 → 2)-(6″-malonylglucoside) (P &lt; 0.01). DPPH was positively correlated with Kaempferol 3-(6-acetylgalactoside) and Kaempferol 3-(2″-feruloylglucosyl)-(1 → 2)-(6″-malonylglucoside), whereas ABTS results indicated a positive correlation with Kaempferol 3-(6-acetylgalactoside), although this was not statistically significant. In summary, the antioxidant activity of <em>M. delavayi</em> was positively correlated with an increase in bioactive constituents, particularly flavonoids.</p></section></section></section><section id="s0205"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Conclusions</h2>
<p id="p0395">This study utilized a comprehensive UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS and GC–MS/MS-based untargeted metabolomics approach to profile the metabolites of <em>M. delavayi</em> processed by three distinct drying methods: SD, HAD, and VFD. This approach elucidated compositional variations and dynamic changes, with key metabolic pathways further screened via KEGG pathway analysis, revealing that drying methods significantly altered the chemical composition and content of <em>M. delavayi</em>. A total of 742 non-volatile differential metabolites were identified, primarily consisting of phenylpropanoids, polyketides, organic acids and their derivatives, and lipids and lipid-like molecules. Samples processed by VFD exhibited the highest relative abundance of key bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, coumarins and their derivatives, cinnamic acids and their derivatives, and carbohydrates, which correlated with superior antioxidant activities. This enhancement is attributed to the gentle drying conditions of VFD, which minimized thermal isomerization and degradation while preserving key metabolic pathways. Conversely, HAD samples were enriched in amino acid metabolites, while SD samples retained significantly higher levels of lipids. Key differential metabolites across all comparisons were strongly correlated in <em>M. delavayi</em> and could serve as potential markers for quality assessment. KEGG enrichment analysis further revealed that these metabolites primarily participated in amino acid metabolism and flavonoid biosynthesis, highlighting the multidimensional regulatory impact of drying on the plant's metabolic networks.</p>
<p id="p0400">For volatile metabolites, we detected 87 distinct compounds, predominantly consisting of organic oxygen compounds, organic acids and derivatives, and lipids and lipid-like molecules. Key differential metabolites such as glucose, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-Glutamine, and Myristic acid exhibited significant content variations among different drying methods, influencing the aroma, quality, and bioactivity of the final product. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these volatiles were primarily associated with galactose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and ABC transporter pathways. The drying treatments distinctly influenced energy metabolism and transmembrane transport processes, ultimately shaping the unique metabolite profiles.</p>
<p id="p0405">Particularly, the key differential metabolites, including flavonoids, function as thermosensitive antioxidant components, with retention levels that vary significantly across drying methods. Correlation analysis confirmed a strong association between these metabolites and antioxidant activity. HAD substantially reduced these active compounds, primarily due to thermal degradation and the activation of polyphenol oxidase. In contrast, SD and VFD effectively preserved these bioactive constituents, with VFD demonstrating the highest retention. However, considering time and environmental factors, extended drying durations often lead to material deterioration due to mold or bacterial growth, making VFD a preferable alternative due to its higher efficiency. Although HAD promoted the accumulation of certain amino acids, it also induced the degradation of heat-labile components such as flavonoid glycosides. Moreover, VFD effectively retained volatile lipid metabolites under oxygen-limited conditions. The dynamic balance between lipid metabolism and volatile composition represents a critical target for drying optimization, as thermal processing initiates various biochemical reactions, including amino acid hydrolysis, lipid oxidation, and Maillard reactions. Overall, metabolomics provides a powerful approach for visualizing molecular compositional differences in food matrices and tracking metabolic alterations during processing.</p>
<p id="p0410">In conclusion, this study employed untargeted metabolomics to systematically evaluate the impact of different drying methods on both volatile and non-volatile metabolites in <em>M. delavayi</em>, offering a scientific basis for optimizing drying processes and improving product quality. Each drying method distinctly shaped the metabolite profiles of <em>M. delavayi</em>, displaying unique advantages in either preserving or altering specific groups of metabolites. Moreover, the dynamic variations observed in volatile metabolites provide important insights into flavor formation mechanisms, warranting further investigation into their relationship with bioactive components. Future research should focus on how drying methods influence the nutritional quality, flavor profile, and bioactivity of <em>M. delavayi</em> as a food product. Concurrently, efforts are underway to develop cost-effective drying technologies for industrial-scale applications, which will guide the production of high-quality functional foods and support the sustainable utilization of this resource.</p></section><section id="s0210"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CRediT authorship contribution statement</h2>
