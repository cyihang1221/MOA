</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Monoterpene indole alkaloids (MIAs) exhibit diverse structures and pharmacological effects. Annotating MIAs in herbal medicines remains challenging when using liquid chromatography combined with high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS). This study introduced a new annotation strategy employing LC-HRMS to efficiently identify MIAs in herbal medicines. Briefly, MS<sup>2</sup> spectra under multiple collision energies (MCEs/MS<sup>2</sup>) helped capture high-quality product ions across a range of mass-to-charge (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em>) values, revealing key MS<sup>2</sup> features such as diagnostic product ions (DPIs), characteristic cleavages (CCs), and neutral/radical losses (NLs/RLs). Next, feature-based molecular networking (FBMN) was created to map the structural relationships among MIAs across large MS datasets. Potential MIAs were then graded and annotated through systematic comparison with known biosynthetic pathways (BPs), derived skeletons, and their characteristic substituents. The MCEs/MS<sup>2</sup>-FBMN/BPs workflow was first applied to annotate MIAs in the alkaloids from the leaf of <em>Alstonia scholaris</em> (ALAS), a new botanical drug for respiratory diseases. A total of 229 MIAs were systematically annotated and classified, forming a solid basis for future clinical research on ALAS. This study offers an effective strategy that enhances the structural annotation of MIAs within complex herbal medicines.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> high-resolution mass spectrometry, feature-based molecular networking, biosynthetic pathways, monoterpene indole alkaloids, <em>Alstonia scholaris</em></p></section></section><section id="sec1-plants-14-02177"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>Liquid chromatography combined with high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) is widely used for identifying organic molecules in complex matrices, such as phytochemicals [<a href="#B1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-plants-14-02177">1</a>,<a href="#B2-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2-plants-14-02177">2</a>,<a href="#B3-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3-plants-14-02177">3</a>,<a href="#B4-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4-plants-14-02177">4</a>], food compositions [<a href="#B5-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5-plants-14-02177">5</a>], chemical migrants [<a href="#B6-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6-plants-14-02177">6</a>], drug metabolites [<a href="#B7-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-plants-14-02177">7</a>], and endogenous substances [<a href="#B8-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8-plants-14-02177">8</a>]. However, there are still significant challenges in confidently annotating structures in complex herbal medicines, which are crucial for better understanding the biological functions or effects of phytochemicals in organisms.</p>
<p>Ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography electrospray ionization quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-ESI-QTOF-MS<sup>E</sup>) is an advanced LC-HRMS platform that generally involves three data processing steps for structural analysis identification [<a href="#B1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-plants-14-02177">1</a>,<a href="#B2-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2-plants-14-02177">2</a>,<a href="#B6-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6-plants-14-02177">6</a>]. Firstly, establishing suitable chromatographic separation conditions for complex components is fundamental for covering most compounds, improving ionization efficiency, and reducing ionization competition. Next, the MS<sup>1</sup> mass spectrum provides accurate mass (AM) support based on qualitative scores that mainly include precise mass-to-charge (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em>) ratios, isotope matches (abundance and spacing), and alternative molecular formulas, as well as indicating retention time (RT). The final step involves analyzing the MS<sup>2</sup> mass spectrum (also called MS/MS) to convert molecular formulas into structures by examining fragment patterns, such as diagnostic product ions (DPIs), characteristic cleavages (CCs), and neutral/radical losses (NLs/RLs). This stage is crucial for proposing a confident structure from multiple candidates.</p>
<p>Experimental practices suggest that MS<sup>2</sup> spectra under multiple collision energies (MCEs/MS<sup>2</sup>) enable capturing ions across a wide <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> range. This range facilitates the detection of most fragment ions with a threshold, resulting in higher mass scores, accurate molecular formulas, and reliable fragment patterns such as DPIs, CCs, and NLs/RLs. These are crucial for increasing confidence in the structural annotation of compounds. The next challenge is efficiently marking and identifying analogs within complex MS datasets of target samples. Traditionally, the analytical process involves comparing retention behavior, molecular weight, and spectral similarities or differences with standard substances or literature reports. However, a single MS experiment can produce thousands of MS<sup>2</sup> spectra within minutes [<a href="#B6-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6-plants-14-02177">6</a>], and a project can generate millions of spectra. This creates enormous difficulties for manual analysis of these large datasets [<a href="#B9-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-plants-14-02177">9</a>,<a href="#B10-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10-plants-14-02177">10</a>], making systematic annotation time-consuming and labor-intensive for experts.</p>
<p>Global natural products social molecular networking (GNPS) addresses this gap. GNPS is a data-driven platform for storing, analyzing, and sharing knowledge about MS<sup>2</sup> spectra that allows community sharing of raw spectra, ongoing annotation of deposited data, and collaborative curation of reference spectra (called spectral libraries) and experimental data [<a href="#B9-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-plants-14-02177">9</a>]. Molecular networking (MN) serves as the foundation for the web-based MS infrastructure of GNPS, and each of these tens of millions of MS<sup>2</sup> spectra processed by MN is automatically compared to reference spectral libraries to identify compounds. The similarity of MS<sup>2</sup> spectra reflects the similarity of chemical structures in identifying potential molecules; chemical structural information can thus be represented as a network, and chemical relationships can be visualized [<a href="#B10-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10-plants-14-02177">10</a>]. The enhanced feature-based MN (FBMN) utilizes the capabilities of well-established MS processing software and improves traditional MN by incorporating not only MS<sup>1</sup> information, such as isotope patterns and RT, but also ion mobility separation when performed [<a href="#B9-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-plants-14-02177">9</a>]. FBMN enables precise visualization of isomeric or closely related compounds within molecular families, offering a promising alternative to fast mark analogs in complex herb matrices.</p>
<p>Monoterpene indole alkaloids (MIAs), an essential class of active molecules in herbal medicines, have multiple pharmacological activities in clinical use. For example, vinca alkaloids, first isolated from <em>Catharanthus roseus</em>, have been developed as microtubule-targeting agents (Vincristine, Vinblastine, Vinorelbine, Vindesine, Vinflunine, etc.) approved for treating hematological and lymphatic cancers [<a href="#B11-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11-plants-14-02177">11</a>]. Reserpine is initially derived from Rauvolfia serpentina and is used as a central nervous system depressant. It blocks the postganglionic nerve fiber from releasing the excitatory neurotransmitter norepinephrine in the synaptic gap [<a href="#B12-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12-plants-14-02177">12</a>].</p>
<p>MIAs are naturally produced by the condensation of tryptamine and secologanin via the Pictet–Spengler reaction [<a href="#B13-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13-plants-14-02177">13</a>]. They mainly possess molecular weights from 300 to 400 Da and display diversity in chemical structures due to multiple chiral carbons. This results in the prevalence of MIA isomers, analogs, or derivatives with similar structures in herbs, which still makes it difficult to quickly annotate MIAs in complex components using the MCEs/MS<sup>2</sup> coupling with the FBMN strategy. Additionally, potential MIAs can also be derived from basic skeletons and typical substituents based on the biosynthetic pathways (BPs) of the target herbs to enable more efficient annotation.</p>
<p><em>Alstonia scholaris</em> is a herb with leaves rich in MIAs that are widely used in Chinese traditional medicine to treat respiratory diseases, such as cough, asthma, phlegm, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [<a href="#B14-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14-plants-14-02177">14</a>,<a href="#B15-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15-plants-14-02177">15</a>]. The alkaloids from the leaf of <em>A. scholaris</em> (ALAS), a new investigational botanical drug (No. 2011L01436) for respiratory disease, have been confirmed to contain the major MIAs (e.g., scholaricine, 19-<em>epi</em>-scholaricine, vallesamine, and picrinine) for anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects [<a href="#B16-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16-plants-14-02177">16</a>], anti-tussive, anti-asthmatic, and expectorant activities [<a href="#B17-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17-plants-14-02177">17</a>], anti-airway inflammation [<a href="#B18-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18-plants-14-02177">18</a>] and anti-allergic asthma effects [<a href="#B15-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15-plants-14-02177">15</a>]; protective activity against emphysema [<a href="#B18-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18-plants-14-02177">18</a>] and post-infectious cough [<a href="#B18-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18-plants-14-02177">18</a>], the ability to alleviate pulmonary fibrosis [<a href="#B14-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14-plants-14-02177">14</a>], anti-microbial effects [<a href="#B19-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19-plants-14-02177">19</a>], and inhibition of influenza A virus replication [<a href="#B20-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20-plants-14-02177">20</a>]. ALAS has been approved for phase I/II clinical trials by the China Food and Drug Administration (CFDA).</p>
<p>This study aims to comprehensively annotate MIAs in ALAS using the proposed MCEs/MS<sup>2</sup>-FBMN/BP workflow (<a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Figure S1</a>). Fragment patterns of 48 reference MIAs (ten subtypes) were systematically illustrated in detail, and DPIs, CCs, and NLs/RLs were proposed accordingly. The non-target captured LC-MS<sup>2</sup> dataset of ALAS was processed with MZmine 2 to screen MS<sup>2</sup> features of MIAs, and the FBMN was successfully constructed. The potential structures of MIAs were derived from basic MIA skeletons and typical substituents based on the reported BPs of the <em>Alstonia</em> genus, as well as consulted from the in-house database, SciFinder, online libraries, and the literature. Consequently, 229 known and unknown MIAs in ALAS were interactively annotated and classified for the first time. This study provides a fundamental basis for further clinical investigations of ALAS and offers an LC-HRMS-based strategy and clues for annotating MIAs in herbal medicines.</p></section><section id="sec2-plants-14-02177"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Results and Discussion</h2>
<section id="sec2dot1-plants-14-02177"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Characteristic Fragment Patterns of MIAs</h3>
<p>MIAs were the most abundant metabolites in <em>A. scholaris</em>, biosynthesized through the condensation of tryptophan with secologanin [<a href="#B13-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13-plants-14-02177">13</a>]. The main MIA subtypes, including scholaricine-type, picrinine-type, vallesamine-type, and alstolactine-type, were used as examples to summarize characteristic fragment patterns of MIAs.</p>

<p><strong>P41</strong>, <strong>P45</strong>, <strong>P36</strong>, and <strong>P70</strong> were typical scholaricine-type MIAs that differ in the absolute configuration of C-19/C-20 and the substituents at C-12 and C-16. <p><strong>P61</strong>, <strong>P66</strong>, <strong>P109</strong>, and <strong>P143</strong> differed in the substituent at the C-12 position and were used to analyze common fragment patterns of scholaricine-type N4-oxide MIAs (<a href="#plants-14-02177-f003" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>): (1) an abundance of DPI [M+H-CH<sub>4</sub>O]<sup>+</sup> was generated by NL of CH<sub>4</sub>O (32.03 Da) from the COOCH<sub>3</sub> group on the C-16 position; (2) a specific DPI [M+H-•C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>14</sub>NO<sub>2</sub>]<sup>•+</sup> was produced after simultaneous ring D/E cleavage; (3) the DPI [M+H-122.05 Da]<sup>+</sup>, corresponding to the indole ring skeleton of scholaricine-type N-oxide MIAs, was generated through successive NLs of CH<sub>4</sub>O+H<sub>2</sub>O+C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>4</sub>O+CO (122.05 Da); and (4) a pair of ring D DPIs, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 82.06 ([C<sub>5</sub>H<sub>8</sub>N]<sup>+</sup>) and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 94.06 ([C6H8N]<sup>+</sup>), resulted from a<sub>1</sub>- and a<sub>2</sub>-cleavage on the fragment ion [M+H-CH<sub>4</sub>O-H<sub>2</sub>O-C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>4</sub>O]<sup>+</sup>, respectively.</p>
<p>Due to the rotation of the single bond between N-4 and C-5 during biosynthetic pathways, <strong>P162</strong> and its N4-oxide compound (<strong>P184</strong>) exhibited an ethyl chain at the C-14 position. Although the fragment patterns of <strong>P162</strong> and <strong>P184</strong> did not match the typical scholaricine-type MIAs, the characteristic NLs, including CO (27.99 Da), H<sub>2</sub>O (18.01 Da), C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>2</sub>, C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>4</sub>, C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>5</sub>N, and C<sub>3</sub>H<sub>7</sub>N, were detected in their MS<sup>2</sup> spectra.</p></section><section id="sec2dot1dot2-plants-14-02177"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1.2. Picrinine-Type MIAs</h4>
<p>Six standard picrinine-type MIAs, including picrinine (<strong>P160</strong>), picralinal (<strong>P169</strong>), burnamine (<strong>P96</strong>), 5<em>α</em>-methoxystrictamine (<strong>P150</strong>), strictamine N-oxide (<strong>P139</strong>), and scholarisine B (<strong>P202</strong>), showed [M+H]<sup>+</sup> as the precursor ion (<strong>P160</strong> and <strong>P139</strong>: <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 339.1703 [C<sub>20</sub>H<sub>23</sub>N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>]<sup>+</sup>; <strong>P169</strong>: <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 367.1652 [C<sub>21</sub>H<sub>23</sub>N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>]<sup>+</sup>; <strong>P96</strong>: <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 369.1809 [C<sub>21</sub>H<sub>25</sub>N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>]<sup>+</sup>; <strong>P150</strong>: <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 353.1860 [C<sub>21</sub>H<sub>25</sub>N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>]<sup>+</sup>; <strong>P202</strong>: <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 411.1914 [C<sub>23</sub>H<sub>27</sub>N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub>]<sup>+</sup>).</p>
<p>As shown in <a href="#plants-14-02177-f004" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>, the compound <strong>P160</strong> was used as an example to illustrate common fragment patterns of picrinine-type MIAs: (1) three concurrent DPIs with <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 108.08 ([C<sub>7</sub>H<sub>10</sub>N]<sup>+</sup>), 107.07 ([C<sub>7</sub>H<sub>9</sub>N]<sup>•+</sup>), and 106.06 ([C<sub>7</sub>H<sub>8</sub>N]<sup>+</sup>), generated through b<sub>1</sub>-cleavage, helped characterize the structure of ring D; (2) a specific DPI [M+H-H<sub>2</sub>O]<sup>+</sup> appeared if an oxygen bridge existed between C-2 and C-5; (3) a DPI with <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 120.08 ([C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>10</sub>N]<sup>+</sup>), produced by b<sub>2</sub>-cleavage on [M+H-H<sub>2</sub>O]<sup>+</sup>, also aided in characterizing ring D; (4) a DPI with <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 144.08 ([C<sub>10</sub>H<sub>10</sub>N]<sup>+</sup>), corresponding to the indole ring skeleton, resulted from the simultaneous removal of ring D and the carbon bridge between C-7 and C-18 from [M-H-H<sub>2</sub>O]<sup>+</sup>; and (5) a pair of DPIs with <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 307.18 ([M+H-CH<sub>4</sub>O]<sup>+</sup>) and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 279.15 ([M+H-CH<sub>4</sub>O-CO]<sup>+</sup>), generated by NL of CH<sub>4</sub>O (32.03 Da) and CH<sub>4</sub>O+CO (60.02 Da) from [M+H]<sup>+</sup>, could be used to identify the COOCH<sub>3</sub> group at C-16. </p>
<p>Comparisons with <strong>P169</strong> and <strong>P96</strong> showed that C-16 contained a CHO and CH<sub>2</sub>OH group, respectively, leading to a common DPI at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 339.17 ([C<sub>20</sub>H<sub>23</sub>N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>]<sup>+</sup>) in the MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum of both <strong>P169</strong> and <strong>P96</strong>, resulting from NL of CO and CH<sub>2</sub>O. Feature-Based Molecular Networking (FBMN) of MIAs in ALAS</h3>
<p>The datasets in the “.mZML” format were initially imported into MZmine 2, and most steps followed the general workflow shown in previous studies [<a href="#B21-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21-plants-14-02177">21</a>,<a href="#B22-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22-plants-14-02177">22</a>]. The simplified tutorial is provided in <a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Figure S57</a>. After completing the basic processing steps, the most important part of this study focused on screening MIAs’ MS<sup>2</sup> features. Therefore, the molecular weight was set in the range of 180 Da to 500 Da, elemental composition was limited to C 0–50, H 0–80, O 0–30, and N 0–2, and the DBE was constrained to 5–20. The charge of the precursor ion was set to one, and molecular formulas with ppm &lt; 2 were accurately generated. Finally, RT-consistent MS<sup>2</sup> features were manually compared to filter out false features caused by in-source collision-induced dissociation (IS-CID). The “.csv” and “.mgf” files were exported to GNPS. Then, FBMNs with different cosine thresholds were created, enabling efficient visualization of isomeric and similar MIAs in LC–MS<sup>2</sup> datasets of ALAS [<a href="#B9-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-plants-14-02177">9</a>]. In summary, a highly restrictive FBMN (cosine 0.70) was first generated to create a network with tightly clustered, highly correlated nodes, facilitating rapid identification of their characteristic MS<sup>2</sup> fragment patterns. Subsequently, moderate-restriction FBMNs (cosine at 0.40, 0.50, 0.58) were produced, forming an expanded network that connected some unlinked nodes within clusters for further annotation and classification (<a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Figure S58</a>) [<a href="#B23-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23-plants-14-02177">23</a>].</p>
<p>The FBMN (cosine 0.58) displayed the overall distribution of major clusters that were systematically analyzed (<a href="#plants-14-02177-f006" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a>). Compounds with similar structures were automatically grouped into clusters. The precursor ion intensity of some MIAs was too low to be detected in ALAS, which prevented them from reaching the threshold to trigger MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum collection. A total of 30 reference MIAs were automatically annotated in the ALAS FBMN. For instance, <strong>P41</strong> (node 7), <strong>P160</strong> (node 86), <strong>P91</strong> (node 4), <strong>P220</strong> (node 148), <strong>P179</strong> (node 126), and <strong>P225</strong> (node 34) were accurately identified as scholaricine, picrinine, vallesamine, scholarisine M, alstoscholarisine C, and vallesiachotamine, respectively (<a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Figure S59</a>). Additionally, some nodes matched more closely with higher cosine values in GNPS Libraries; for example, <strong>P107</strong> (node 31), <strong>P77</strong> (node 25), <strong>P29</strong> (node 84), <strong>P186</strong> (node 193), and <strong>P161</strong> (node 1) were effectively identified as yohimbine, akuammidine, echitamidine, leuconolam, and pleiocarpamine, respectively (<a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Figure S60</a>). In summary, 18 MIAs were precisely annotated based on the reference standards. The main clusters primarily contained six subtypes of MIAs, including scholaricine-type, picrinine-type, vallesamine-type, yohimbine-type, alstolactine-type, and alstoscholarisine-type MIAs (<a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Figure S58</a>).</p>

In recent years, using FBMN technology has helped researchers discover an increasing number of natural products with unprecedented structural frameworks and notable bioactivities, transforming the approach to natural product isolation and discovery. This approach can offer valuable insights into the unique MIA profile of the <em>Alstonia</em> genus and its potential medicinal benefits. However, these findings indicated the potential close relationships among these MIAs in BPs, which may help in the practical annotation of unknown MIA nodes.</p></section><section id="sec2dot3-plants-14-02177"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Potential MIAs in the Biosynthetic Pathways of the Alstonia Genus</h3>
<p>For efficient annotation of MIAs in ALAS, this study constructed hypothetical BPs of <em>Alstonia</em> MIAs based on the verified and reported chemical literature, serving as a key reference for annotating MIAs in FBMN (<a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Figure S61</a>). Partially characteristic biosynthetic pathways are shown in <a href="#plants-14-02177-f007" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>. Briefly, tryptophan and secologanin first formed the precursor strictosidine, which undergone ed s biochemical reactions such as deglycosylation, dehydration, reduction, and oxidation to produce yohimbine-type MIAs like ajmalicine and 19<em>E</em>-geissoschizine. The precursor 4,21-dehydro-geissoschizine, from which 19<em>E</em>-geissoschizine was derived, marked the start of the first branch’s differentiation; this pathway led to (+)-stemmadenine through complex reactions, and produced vallesamine, a typical vallesamine-type MIA. Vallesamine can be oxidized to vallesamine-N-oxide or hydrolyzed to form alstoniascholarine A. It can also undergo oxidation, reduction, and cyclization after N-4 methylation to generate alstoscholarisine-type MIAs, including alstoscholarisine I, C, and H. The second branch began with 19<em>E</em>-geissoschizine, which cyclized to form rhazimol. This intermediate can then be oxidized, methylated, and processed through various reactions to produce picrinine-type MIAs such as burnamine, picralinal, 5<em>α</em>-methoxystrictamine, and picrinine. Subsequently, picrinine can undergo N-4 methylation, ring cleavage, oxidation, and epoxidation, resulting in alstolactin-type MIAs like scholarisine M, L, and E. The third branch, also starting from 19<em>E</em>-geissoschizine, produced preakuammicine via geissoschizine oxidase activity, forming the core of scholaricine-type MIAs. Preakuammicine then spontaneously lost HCHO to form (−)-akuammicine, which, through further redox reactions, yielded characteristic scholaricine-type MIAs such as scholaricine and 19-<em>epi</em>-scholaricine.</p>

Systematic Annotation of MIAs in ALAS</h3>
<p>The integrated MCEs/MS<sup>2</sup>-FBMN/BP workflow was used to comprehensively annotate the main MIAs in ALAS (<a href="#plants-14-02177-f008" class="usa-link">Figure 8</a>A). In brief, (1) the accurate mass and molecular formula of all nodes in FBMN were characterized based on MCEs/MS<sup>2</sup> spectra; and (2) a comparative analysis of MS<sup>2</sup> features and cosine similarity between reference nodes (or automatically identified nodes in GNPS Libraries) and unknown nodes was performed. The similarities of DPIs and CCs indicated information about the parent skeleton, while differences in NLs/RLs helped deduce substituent modifications on the basic structure; (3) finally, the structural candidates of MIAs based on the MPs were applied for efficient annotation of unknown nodes in FBMN. These candidates were further indexed in SciFinder to determine whether they had been reported in the <em>Alstonia</em> genus and to predict their structural novelty. Using this workflow, the identified MIAs were tentatively classified into four annotation levels: Level 1, confirmed by the reference standard; Level 2, speculated based on the library spectrum match or diagnostic fragmentation evidence; Level 3, tentative candidate classification; and Level 4, exact molecular formula.</p>

<p>The nodes directly connected to authorized reference MIAs (nodes) that showed high similarities of MS<sup>2</sup> features were easily annotated by comparing DPIs, CCs, and NLs/RLs. The nodes indirectly associated with authorized reference MIAs (nodes) were first analyzed through fragment ions to identify potential DPIs for classifying the MIA subtypes, then referenced to BPs to preliminarily predict possible structures. For single nodes in FBMN, a SciFinder search revealed that the molecules were not reported in the <em>Alstonia</em> genus and exhibited significant variation in fragment patterns compared to MIA standards, suggesting potential novelty in their structures. Using the proposed MCEs/MS<sup>2</sup>-FBMN/BP workflow, the chemical profile of MIAs in ALAS was clearly illustrated, and 229 MIAs were interactively annotated and classified for the first time (<a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Table S3</a>).</p>
<p>Our study offered a thorough analysis of the MIA profiles in ALAS. By examining the subtypes of MIAs, we found that scholaricine-type MIAs showed the highest peak intensity, making up 23.22% of the total peak area, followed by picrinine-type MIAs at 20.59% and vallesamine-type MIAs at 19.17% (<a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Table S3</a>). This finding supported our earlier conclusion that scholaricine, 19-<em>epi</em>-scholaricine, picrinine, and vallesamine were the main bioactive markers in ALAS [<a href="#B14-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14-plants-14-02177">14</a>,<a href="#B15-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15-plants-14-02177">15</a>,<a href="#B16-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16-plants-14-02177">16</a>,<a href="#B17-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17-plants-14-02177">17</a>,<a href="#B18-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18-plants-14-02177">18</a>,<a href="#B19-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19-plants-14-02177">19</a>,<a href="#B20-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20-plants-14-02177">20</a>]. While our analytical framework greatly increased MIA annotation coverage, structural elucidation was limited to plausible planar structure assignments due to the inherent limits of mass spectrometry in distinguishing stereoisomers. Without standard substances, the stereoconfiguration of annotated MIAs cannot be fully confirmed based on LC-HRMS. Notably, the proposed structures of unknown compounds showed great potential for novelty due to substantial differences in fragment patterns from the known MIAs. These findings offered strategic guidance for future bioactivity-guided isolation studies to discover MIAs with new scaffolds and improved bioactivities.</p></section></section><section id="sec3-plants-14-02177"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec3dot1-plants-14-02177"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Chemicals and Reagents</h3>
<p>For LC-HRMS analysis, acetonitrile, ethanol, methanol, and formic acid were purchased from Merck KGaA Co., Ltd. (Darmstadt, Germany). Hydrochloric acid, analytical ethanol, and other reagents were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Distilled water was sourced from Watson’s Specialty Store (Hong Kong). Forty-eight authorized reference MIAs were isolated from <em>A. scholaris</em> by Luo’s group and stored in a refrigerator at −20 °C. According to published literature, the absolute configuration of MIAs was identified using NMR, HRMS, X-ray, etc. (<a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>). The MIAs (<a href="#plants-14-02177-f002" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>) included scholaricine-type (scholaricine, 19-<em>epi</em>-scholaricine, alstoniascholarine F, alstoniascholarine H, scholaricine N-oxide, alstoniascholarine P, N-demethylalstogustine N-oxide, alstoniascholarine Q, tubotaiwine, and tubotaiwine N-oxide); picrinine-type (picrinine, picralinal, burnamine, 5<em>α</em>-methoxystrictamine, strictamine N-oxide, and scholarisine B); vallesamine-type (vallesamine, vallesamine N-oxide, alstoniascholarine A, and 6,7-seco-angustilobine B); alstolactine-type (scholarisine M, scholarisine L, scholarisine E, alstolactine A, alstolactine B, alstoniascholarine L, and alstoniascholarine M); yohimbine-type (19<em>E</em>-geissoschizine abd ajmalicine); alstoscholarisine-type (alstoscholarisine D, alstoscholarisine E, alstoscholarisine A, alstoscholarisine C, alstoscholarisine B, alstoscholarisine H, and alstoscholarisine I); scholarisine-type (scholarisine I, scholarisine J, and scholarisine A); vallesiachotamine-type (vallesiachotamine and isovallesiachotamine); alstoscholarine-type (<em>Z</em>-alstoscholarine and <em>E</em>-alstoscholarine); and unclassified MIAs (strictosamide, (+)-vincadifformine, leuconoxine, and scholarisine H).</p></section><section id="sec3dot2-plants-14-02177"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. ALAS Preparation</h3>
<p><em>A. scholaris</em> was collected in June 2013 in Pu’er city (Yunnan Province, China) and identified by Dr. Xiaodong Luo, Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Kunming, China). The Chinese Academy of Sciences’ State Key Laboratory of Phytochemistry and Plant Resources in West China is home to the voucher specimen (Luo20130601). The dried <em>A. scholaris</em> leaf powder was extracted using 90% ethanol under reflux conditions (3 × 4), and the solvent was evaporated in a vacuum to obtain the ethanolic extract. The ethanolic extract was dissolved in 0.3% aqueous hydrochloric acid solution, and the residue was identified as a non-alkaloid fraction. Subsequently, the acidic solution was adjusted to pH 9–10 with 10% aqueous ammonia and extracted with EtOAc to yield ALAS (containing main MIAs) [<a href="#B14-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14-plants-14-02177">14</a>,<a href="#B19-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19-plants-14-02177">19</a>]. ALAS were dissolved in methanol to 100 μg/mL and then filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane for LC-HRMS analysis. Each sample was prepared three times in parallel. The reliability and stability of the instrumental analysis were confirmed through quality control (QC) samples, which were prepared by mixing equal amounts of the tested samples.</p></section><section id="sec3dot3-plants-14-02177"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3. LC-HRMS Conditions</h3>
<p>A 1290 Infinity II ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled with a 6545B quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry system, equipped with a dual AJS electrospray ionization (ESI) source (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), was used for LC-HRMS analysis. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an SB C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm, Agilent Technologies) maintained at a constant temperature of 35 °C. The solvent system consisted of phases A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid), with a gradient elution at a flow rate of 0.15 mL/min, as follows: 0–7 min, 4–12% B; 7–12 min, 12–17% B; 12–16 min, 17% B; 16–23 min, 17–30% B; 23–28 min, 30–45% B; 28–35 min, 45–65% B; 35–40 min, 65–98% B; and 40–48 min, 98% B. The injected volume was 0.5 μL.</p>
<p>Mass spectra conditions in positive ESI mode for accurate compound characterization were optimized as follows: gas temperature 325 °C, 9 L/min; nebulizer pressure 35 psi; sheath gas temperature 365 °C, 11 L/min; nozzle voltage 1.0 kV; VCAP voltage 4.0 kV; OCT1 RF Vpp 750 V; skimmer voltage 65 V; and fragmentor 150 V. MS<sup>1</sup> data were collected from 100 to 1000 Da, and MS<sup>2</sup> data were acquired from 25 to 1000 Da. Collision energies (CEs) from low to high values (10–40 eV) were optimized to accurately capture MS<sup>2</sup> spectra of individual compound features, based on our previous studies [<a href="#B27-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27-plants-14-02177">27</a>,<a href="#B28-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28-plants-14-02177">28</a>,<a href="#B29-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29-plants-14-02177">29</a>].</p></section><section id="sec3dot4-plants-14-02177"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.4. Construction of In-House and Online HRMS Database of Authorized Reference MIAs</h3>
<p>Each MIA was properly dissolved in methanol and then filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane for LC-HRMS analysis. The MCEs/MS<sup>2</sup> strategy was initially implemented using Agilent MassHunter Qualitative Analysis Navigator (B.08.00) to build an in-house MS<sup>2</sup> database of 48 reference MIAs at 10, 20, and 40 eV, respectively (<a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Figures S2–S49</a>). Meanwhile, the raw data in the “.d” format were converted to “.MGF” files and then uploaded to the online GNPS libraries. Each MIA’s MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum received a unique GNPS spectrum ID (<a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Figures S2–S49</a>), allowing researchers to access these spectra for free via the website <a href="https://gnps-library.ucsd.edu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gnps-library.ucsd.edu/</a> (accessed on 19 April 2022).</p></section><section id="sec3dot5-plants-14-02177"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.5. Construction of Feature-Based Molecular Networking of MIAs in ALAS</h3>
<p>The MSconvert tool was used to convert the original .d data to the .mzML format, which was then submitted to MZmine 2 following the previous processing flow [<a href="#B13-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13-plants-14-02177">13</a>,<a href="#B21-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21-plants-14-02177">21</a>]. The present study improved the processing of LC-MS<sup>2</sup> datasets of MIAs in ALAS. The main steps are shown in <a href="#sec3dot2-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Section 3.2</a>, and the “.csv” and “.mgf” files were exported to GNPS for FBMN analysis [<a href="#B9-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-plants-14-02177">9</a>]. FBMN parameters were configured as follows: The data was filtered to remove all MS<sup>2</sup> fragment ions within ±17 Da of the precursor <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>. MS<sup>2</sup> spectra were window-filtered by selecting only the top 6 fragment ions within the ±50 Da window across the spectrum. The precursor ion mass tolerance was set to 0.02 Da, and the MS<sup>2</sup> fragment ion tolerance to 0.05 Da. Edges between two nodes were retained in the network if each node was among the other’s top 10 most similar nodes. Finally, the maximum size of a molecular family was set to 100, and the lowest-scoring edges were removed from the molecular families until the size threshold was met. The spectra in the network were then searched against GNPS spectral libraries [<a href="#B30-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30-plants-14-02177">30</a>,<a href="#B31-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31-plants-14-02177">31</a>]. The library spectra were filtered in the same way as the input data. All matches between network spectra and library spectra had to have a score above 0.6 and at least six matched peaks. FBMNs were then generated using optimized values of the “Minimum Cosine Score” (a similarity score between two MS<sup>2</sup> spectra that allows linking them) at 0.70, 0.58, 0.50, and 0.40, respectively. The FBMN jobs can be accessed via the GNPS website, as shown in the <a href="#app1-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link">Supplementary Materials</a>. The molecular networks were visualized using Cytoscape software (v3.9) [<a href="#B32-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32-plants-14-02177">32</a>]. The nodes indicated the precursor ions, and cosine values showed the similarity between nodes [<a href="#B23-plants-14-02177" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23-plants-14-02177">23</a>].</p></section><section id="sec3dot6-plants-14-02177"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.6. Analysis of Potential MIAs of the Alstonia Genus Based on Biosynthetic Pathways</h3>
<p>The <em>Alstonia</em> genus mainly includes <em>A. scholaris</em>, <em>Alstonia macrophylla</em>, <em>Alstonia yunnanensis</em>, <em>Alstonia henryi</em>, <em>Alstonia mairei</em>, and <em>Alstonia rupestris</em>. <em>A. scholaris</em> is one of the representative plant species. The basic framework (individual subtype-MIAs) was first built based on the classical BPs of <em>Alstonia</em> MIAs according to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and the existing literature. Additionally, multiple databases such as SciFinder, MsSID, HMDB, and Phytohub were consulted under the <em>Alstonia</em> genus. Then, each MIA subtype’s typical substituents and substitution sites were systematically analyzed.</p></section></section><section id="sec4-plants-14-02177"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Conclusions</h2>
<p>The <em>Alstonia</em> genus has attracted increasing interest due to the distinctive chemical structures of MIAs and the diverse biological activities. In this study, a combined MCEs/MS<sup>2</sup>-FBMN/BPs workflow was developed using a UHPLC-ESI-QTOF-MS<sup>E</sup> platform, greatly improving the efficiency and accuracy of alkaloid component analysis. The fragmentation patterns of various structural skeletons (ten subtypes out of 48 reference MIAs), including scholaricine-type, picrinine-type, vallesamine-type, alstolactine-type, yohimbine-type, alstoscholarisine-type, scholarisine-type, vallesiachotamine-type, alstoscholarine-type, and five unclassified MIAs, were systematically examined. The fragmentation behaviors observed through MCEs/MS<sup>2</sup> were integrated with FBMN/BPs to enable detailed characterization of the enriched MIAs. A total of 229 MIAs were identified and classified in ALAS (<em>A. scholaris</em>) for the first time, including 30 previously unreported compounds. Overall, this study provides an effective workflow for identifying MIAs in complex herbal medicines. Notably, these newly discovered MIAs could serve as potential candidates for further MS-guided isolation and drug discovery investigations.</p></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
