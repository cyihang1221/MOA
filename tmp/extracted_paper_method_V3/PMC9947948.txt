<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/8046/9947948/eaca6c95b69a/ao2c06885_0006.jpg" loading="lazy" id="ab-tgr1" height="561" width="655" alt="graphic file with name ao2c06885_0006.jpg"></p>
<p>Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) in-hospital preparations
are
approved for use only in the hospital where they are prepared. They
are widely used in China because of their efficacy and affordable
price. However, few researchers focused on their quality controls
and treatment mechanisms, for which a key consideration is the elucidation
of their chemical composition. Runyan mixture (RY) is a typical in-hospital
TCM preparation comprising a formula of eight herbal drugs used for
adjuvant therapy of upper respiratory tract infections. The chemical
constituents of formulated RY have not yet been elucidated. In the
present work, RY was analyzed by a ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography
system equipped with high-resolution orbitrap mass spectrometry (MS).
The acquired MS data were processed by MZmine and a feature-based
molecular networking was constructed to identify the metabolites of
RY. 165 compounds including 41 flavonoid <em>O</em>-glycosides,
11 flavonoid C-glycosides, 18 quinic acids, 54 coumaric acids, 11
iridoids, and 30 others were identified. This study demonstrates an
efficient method to identify compounds in complex herbal drug mixtures
using high-resolution MS and molecular networking tools which will
support future research into quality controls and treatment mechanisms
of in-hospital TCM preparations.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>The practice of traditional
Chinese medicine (TCM) and the clinical
application of its herbal pharmacopoeia date back thousands of years.<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a></sup> The chemical components of TCM prescriptions
are complex mixtures, especially those composed of several herbs.<sup><a href="#ref2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref2">2</a></sup> The elucidation of the chemical composition of
TCM herbal formulae is required as a basis for research into a prescription’s
efficacy and safety.<sup><a href="#ref3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref3">3</a></sup></p>
<p>Ultrahigh-performance
liquid chromatography combined with high-resolution
mass spectrometry (UHPLC–HRMS) has proven to be an excellent
chemical analysis method for complex systems due to its high sensitivity
and specificity.<sup><a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a>,<a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> UHPLC–HRMS has been widely
used in the detection of small molecules from plants,<sup><a href="#ref6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref6">6</a></sup> animals,<sup><a href="#ref7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref7">7</a></sup> and human samples.<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> Despite abundant available MS data, unequivocal
identification of compounds in complex mixtures remains a challenge.
A variety of commercial software sets for chemical identification
have been developed, including Waters UNIFI<sup><a href="#ref9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref9">9</a></sup> and Thermo Fisher Compound Discoverer.<sup><a href="#ref10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref10">10</a></sup> These were developed primarily for the identification of chemicals
through targeted screening using known databases. Recently, a new
and open method, named Global Natural Products Social molecular networking
(GNPS, <a href="https://gnps.ucsd.edu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gnps.ucsd.edu/</a>), has been established by the Center for Computational Mass Spectrometry
of the University of California San Diego.<sup><a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a></sup> This platform and its molecular networking (MN) application identify
compounds by comparing their MS/MS spectral similarity. A development
of MN, feature-based MN (FBMN), supports MS data processing tools
such as MZmine in feature detection and alignment, with the additional
capacity to differentiate positional and stereoisomers in complex
systems.<sup><a href="#ref6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref6">6</a></sup></p>
<p>In China, some TCM formulations,
so-called in-hospital preparations,
are approved for use only in the hospitals in which they are prepared.
Clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy of same in-hospital
preparations in disease treatment.<sup><a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a></sup> Widely
used in China, especially in county or municipal hospitals, nearly
4000 in-hospital preparations are used in 253 hospitals in nine provinces.<sup><a href="#ref13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref13">13</a></sup> Due to the lack of research funding and multidisciplinary
interest, little research has focused on in-hospital preparations
and, in particular, their quality control and treatment mechanisms.
The identification of the active chemical components in in-hospital
preparations is a key factor in this regard, for which UHPLC–HRMS
is an effective, rapid, and affordable tool.<sup><a href="#ref14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref14">14</a></sup></p>
<p>Runyan mixture (RY) is a typical and effective in-hospital
TCM
preparation approved by a county municipal hospital for adjuvant therapy
in upper respiratory tract infections. It contains eight TCM herbal
drugs including <em>Solidaginis herba</em>, <em>Asparagi
radix</em>, <em>Scrophulariae radix</em>, <em>Ophiopogonis
radix</em>, <em>Rehmanniae radix</em>, <em>Chrysanthemi
indici Flos</em>, <em>Forsythiae fructus</em>, and <em>Glycyrrhizae radix et rhizome</em>. The chemical composition
of formulated RY remains unclarified. In the present work, UHPLC–HRMS
was applied to separate and analyze RY and its complex composition
was elucidated by FBMN. This study provides an efficient method to
support further research into quality control and treatment mechanisms
of in-hospital TCM preparations.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Results and Discussion</h2>

<p>MolNetEnhancer identified cluster MN28 as quinic acid derivatives,
further confirmed by the diagnostic fragmentation ions in MS/MS spectra
at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 191.05, 173.04, 137.02, 129.05,
and 127.04 (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.2c06885/suppl_file/ao2c06885_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S1</a>). In addition to the
diagnostic fragmentation ions, other diagnostic ions of acylated units
were also detected, including caffeoyl and coumaroyl. Compounds QA3,
QA5, QA6, QA8, and QA9 showed the same [M–H]<sup>−</sup> ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 353 with a molecular
formula of C16H18O9. In the MS/MS spectra, the diagnostic ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 179.03, 161.02, and 135.04 suggested
the caffeoyl unit, so these compounds were tentatively assigned as
mono-caffeoyl quinic acids. Three kinds of mono-caffeoyl quinic acids
have been isolated from <em>Chrysanthemum indicum</em> L.<sup><a href="#ref17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref17">17</a></sup> and <em>Solidago decurrens</em> Lour,<sup><a href="#ref24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref24">24</a></sup> including 3-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic acid (chlorogenic acid), 4-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic
acid, and 5-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic acid (neochlorogenic
acid), while at least five mono-caffeoyl quinic acids were detected
in our work, suggesting the presence of compounds such as 1-caffeoylquinic
acid or <em>cis</em>-chlorogenic acid. These two mono-caffeoyl
quinic acids have been detected in other members of the <em>Chrysanthemum</em> genus, including <em>Chrysanthemum
morifolium</em> Ramat.<sup><a href="#ref25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref25">25</a>,<a href="#ref26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref26">26</a></sup> Chlorogenic
acid, <em>cis</em>-chlorogenic acid, and neochlorogenic acid
have the same <em>C</em> log <em>P</em> value at −1.879
which is smaller than that of 4-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic
acid (<em>C</em> log <em>P</em> = −1.400) and
1-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic acid (<em>C</em> log <em>P</em> = −1.332), indicating a similar retention time
for the former three, while the latter two would be eluted later.<sup><a href="#ref27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref27">27</a></sup> Therefore, compounds QA3, QA5, QA6, QA8, and
QA9 were tentatively identified as chlorogenic acid, <em>cis</em>-chlorogenic acid, neochlorogenic acid, 4-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic
acid, and 1-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic acid, the elution order
of these mono-caffeoyl quinic acids being consistent with previous
reports.<sup><a href="#ref28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref28">28</a></sup> Compounds QA12 and QA15-18 gave
the same [M–H]<sup>−</sup> ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 515 with the diagnostic ions of the caffeoyl
unit and ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 353 [M–H–176]<sup>−</sup>, suggesting the presence of two caffeoyl moieties.
Thus, these compounds were tentatively identified as di-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic acid, also isolated from <em>C. indicum</em> L,<sup><a href="#ref17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref17">17</a></sup> and <em>S. decurrens</em> Lour.<sup><a href="#ref24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref24">24</a></sup> According to the difference in <em>C</em> log <em>P</em> values, compounds QA12 and QA15-18
were tentatively identified as 3,4-di-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic
acid (Rt = 20.61 min), 4,5-di-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic acid
(Rt = 34.25 min), 1,3-di-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic acid (Rt
= 35.88 min), 1,5-di-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic acid (Rt =
36.60 min), and 3,5-di-<em>O</em>-caffeoylquinic acid (Rt
= 41.35 min). The precursor ions of compounds QA1 and QA2 were 18
Da larger than those of mono-caffeoyl quinic acids and, although they

According to a recent report, chlorogenic acids may exist as water

displayed an obvious peak at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 609.1762([M–H]<sup>−</sup>) of C<sub>28</sub>H<sub>34</sub>O<sub>15</sub>, producing [M–H–C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>10</sub>O<sub>5</sub>]<sup>−</sup> at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 447.1432 by neutral loss of a glucose. The diagnostic
ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 179, 161, 135, and 153
indicate that two substituents including caffeoyl and hydroxytyrosol
are linked with a glucose. The C6–C2 glucoside conjugates have
been isolated from <em>Forsythiae fructus</em>.<sup><a href="#ref31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref31">31</a></sup> Therefore, compounds AD21 and AD25-27 were tentatively
assigned as forsythoside J (Rt = 28.03 min, <em>C</em> log <em>P</em> = −0.8258), calceolarioside C (Rt = 31.71 min, <em>C</em> log <em>P</em> = −1.2192), calceolarioside
E (Rt = 32.64 min, <em>C</em> log <em>P</em> = −1.4614),
and lianqiaoxinoside C (Rt = 32.98 min, <em>C</em> log <em>P</em> = −1.4716).</p></section><section id="sec2.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Other Compounds</h3>
<p>MN compounds could
also be tentatively identified by comparison of the experimental MS/MS
spectra with GNPS spectral libraries. Chemical similarity was evaluated
by MQScore. Ranging from 0 to 1, a score closer to 1 indicates greater
chemical similarity. 73 compounds were identified with MQScore &gt;0.9.
Among these, 41 compounds were flavonoids, quinic acids, coumaric
acids, or benzoic acids, tentatively identified above. Four compounds,
including piscidic acid, <em>N</em>-acetylphenylalanine, guanosine,
and calceolarioside A, related to single MN nodes, and the remaining
28 compounds in the clusters were tentatively identified by comparison
of the mass spectra of other nodes in the same clusters. The chemical
class of nodes in cluster MN64 (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.2c06885/suppl_file/ao2c06885_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S2</a>) was not identified by MolNetEnhancer, while two nodes were identified
by matching GNPS spectral libraries with MQScore &gt;0.9. The IR10
node
gave a precursor ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 493.1661,
assigned as harpagoside by the GNPS library by comparison with the
characteristic ions at 103.0528, 121.0275, 147.0427, and 165.0531.
The node directly connected with compound IR10 gave a precursor ion
at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 539.171, 46 Da greater than
that of IR10 with the same RT. This node at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 539.171 represents an adduct ion ([M + HCOO]<sup>−</sup>) of compound IR10. Another compound CC1 assigned by GNPS library
comparison was forsythoside E, isolated from <em>Forsythiae fructus</em>, with characteristic ions at 113.0222, 135.043, 153.0537, 315.1054,
and 461.1627. The node directly connected with CC1 gave a precursor
ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 475.1772, 14 Da greater
than CC1, indicating that it was a methylated derivative of forsythoside
E. After comparison with the in-house database, this node was tentatively
assigned as darendroside B, detected in both <em>Scrophulariae
radix</em> and <em>Rehmanniae radix</em>. Similarly, another
49 nodes were tentatively assigned and listed in <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.2c06885/suppl_file/ao2c06885_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S1</a>.</p></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Conclusions</h2>
<p>In the present investigation,
165 compounds associated with 193
nodes in the molecular network of RY were systematically characterized
by combining UHPLC–HRMS and FBMN data processing. The component
clusters represent flavonoid-<em>O</em>-glycosides, flavonoid-C-glycosides,
quinic acids, coumaric acids, and iridoids. This is the first study
to provide comprehensive chemical information on the multi-herb formula
RY.</p>
<p>By providing compositional information, the quality control
and
understanding of therapeutic mechanisms will be facilitated, allowing
effective and safe prescriptions of RY to be developed. Furthermore,
the analytical methods described can be applied to efficiently assign
and identify the chemical components of other complex herbal formulae.
In the future, the elucidation of potential chemical differences between
single herb and multi-herbal formulations should support quality control
efforts and preparation method development. RY is a case in point,
in which new chemical components have been identified in the combination
of eight herbs.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec4.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.1. Materials and Reagents</h3>
<p>RY was prepared
by Dongyang People’s Hospital. HPLC grade acetonitrile was
bought from the Tedia Company Inc. and formic acid was purchased from
Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.</p></section><section id="sec4.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.2. Instruments and Equipment</h3>
<p>An UltiMate
3000 high-performance liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher
Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with Eclipse SB-C<sub>18</sub> Rapid Resolution column and coupled with a Q-Exactive high-resolution
benchtop quadrupole orbitrap mass spectrometer (QE, Thermo Fisher
Scientific) was used for sample analysis. The equipment was controlled
by Xcalibur 3.0 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).</p></section><section id="sec4.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.3. Sample Preparation</h3>
<p>RY solution (10
mL) was accurately diluted with water into to a 50 mL volumetric flask.
After thorough shaking, the test solution was passed through a 0.22
μm filter before analysis.</p></section><section id="sec4.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.4. LC–MS/MS Analysis</h3>
<p>Samples
were eluted with the following gradient where the mobile phases were
0.5% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B): 0–5 min,
3% B; 5–65 min, 3–25% B; 65–90 min, 25–100%
B. The flow rate was 0.3 mL min<sup>–1</sup>. Peaks were detected
in the negative mode and the ESI source conditions were set as follows:
sheath gas flow rate 40 arb; aux. gas flow rate 10 arb; capillary
temperature 320 °C; full mass resolution 70,000; MS/MS resolution
17,500; collision energy 20/40/60 eV (NCE model); spray voltage −3
kV.</p></section><section id="sec4.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.5. Construction of MS/MS-Based Molecular Networking</h3>
<p>To convert the raw LC–MS/MS mass data into <em>mzXML</em> file format, a complete package was downloaded from the GNPS platform
(<a href="https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/fileconversion/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/fileconversion/</a>). The converted MS data were processed by MZmine 2.53. An MS/MS-based
MN of RY was constructed by uploading the processed MS/MS data to
the GNPS platform following the FBMN workflow.<sup><a href="#ref32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref32">32</a></sup> The mass tolerances of precursor ions and fragment ions
were both set as 0.0075 Da. To generate the MN, the cosine score &gt;0.6
and the number of matched fragment ions was &gt;5. Cytoscape 3.9.1
was
used for network visualization.</p></section><section id="sec4.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.6. Workflow of Chemical Constituent Identification</h3>
<p>First, an in-house database of chemical constituents was compiled
from literature reported chemical compositions of RY herbal drugs,
collected from the following databases: web of science, CNKI, and
SciFinder. Second, the constructed molecular network was further processed
by MolNetEnhancer, a useful tool to assign chemical classes in the
molecular network, combining GNPS tools including Network Annotation
Propagation, DEREPLICATOR, and ClassyFire.<sup><a href="#ref32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref32">32</a></sup> Third, from MN results, RY constituents were tentatively identified
by comparing their diagnostic fragmentation ions with literature data.
If no compounds from the in-house database matched the fragmentation
ions from our work, compounds were retrieved from GNPS library, PubChem,
or ChemSpider databases. Some nodes in the molecular network were
tentatively assigned by comparison of the previously identified differences
between connected nodes.</p></section></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
