>Methods   Ethical Statement   This study was conducted according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study design and protocol were approved by the Institutional Review Board of LV Prasad Eye Institute, Hyderabad, India (LEC-BHR-P-05-23-1045). Prior to the patient's participation, written informed consent was obtained. Study Design   The study included 29 eyes of 29 subjects who underwent pars plana vitrectomy for the diagnosis and/or treatment of presumed infectious endophthalmitis between May 2022 and March 2023 at the LV Prasad Eye Institute. The age of the subjects ranged from 2 to 74 years. All of the recruited cases underwent complete ophthalmological examinations, including B-scans, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and visual acuity (VA) measurements at the time of diagnosis and at the 6-month follow-up. The best-corrected visual acuity measurements were analyzed, and cases with uncertain diagnoses or uncharacterized clinical features of infection, a history of autoimmune disease, hypertension, or steroid usage were excluded. The study group consisted of 15 patients diagnosed with bacterial endophthalmitis (10 Gram-positive and 5 Gram-negative microbiologically proven organisms), and the control group included 14 patients undergoing pars plana vitrectomy for non-infectious retinal conditions such as retinal detachment, macular hole, and vitreous hemorrhage during the study period. The recruited patients from both groups were further subdivided into three age categories: group A (young), 0 to 30 years; group B (middle), 31 to 60 years; and group C (elderly), >60 years ( Fig. 1 ). Collection and PreProcessing of the Vitreous Humor Samples   A total of 500 to 1000 µL of the vitreous humor from all of the samples, including the control and infected groups, was taken as per the institute clinical protocol from the anterior vitreous just behind the lens with a 2-cc syringe plugged into the suction tubing of the vitreous cutter on cutting mode and before starting the infusion fluid during vitrectomy, of which 300 µL was transferred into a presterilized microcentrifuge tube, snap frozen within 30 minutes using liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for the metabolomic analysis using high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS). The remaining volumes of the vitreous samples were examined under direct microscopy, and microbiological evaluations were carried out by inoculating the samples on bacterial media (including blood agar, chocolate agar, brain heart infusion broth, and thioglycolate medium) and fungal media (Sabouraud dextrose agar and potato dextrose agar), which were incubated at 37°C and 25°C, respectively, for 7 days as per our standardized institutional protocol. Untargeted Metabolomic Analysis of Vitreous Humor from Endophthalmitis Patients   Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry Conditions   Reverse-phase liquid chromatography was performed using a Zorbax SB-Aq Rapid Resolution High Definition column (#883975-314; 3.0 × 150 mm, 5.0 µm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA); mobile phase A consisted of 18.2 mΩ ultra-pure water with 0.2% acetic acid, and phase B consisted of liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS)–grade methanol with 0.2% acetic acid. Gradient programmed elution was carried out using the Shimadzu CMB-20A system controller (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan); a mobile phase initiated with 5% phase B for 2 minutes was increased to 35% for 15 minutes, further increased to 95% for 5 minutes, and maintained at 95% from 20 to 25 minutes, when it was then reversed back to the initial conditions from 25.1 minutes and maintained until 32 minutes. A flow rate of 0.5 mL/min was maintained throughout the total runtime of 32 minutes; the autosampler and column oven temperatures were maintained at 15°C and 40°C, respectively. Eluent from the LC was passed into the duo spray ion source of the TripleTOF 5600+ System (Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA). A MS scan range of 50 to 1000 m/z ran in both positive and negative ion modes separately. The Sciex DuoSpray ion source temperature was maintained at 550°C. An information-dependent acquisition (IDA) method was employed to acquire high-resolution MS and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) levels, with a collision energy of 35. Data Acquisition and Processing   The data were acquired using Sciex TF1.6 software. The acquired mass spectra were matched with the National Institute of Standards and Technology spectral library database using Sciex OS software. Sample Treatment   Vitreous samples were thawed from −80°C to 4°C, and 150 µL of sample was added to the 600-µL extraction solvent comprised of 50:50 ethanol:methanol with 100-ng/mL epicatechin as the internal standard. The mixture was vortexed for 20 seconds, incubated in ice for 10 minutes, and then placed in an incubator shaker for 15 minutes at 100 rpm. Samples were centrifuged at 10,000 g  for 5 minutes; 600 µL of supernatant was transferred in a vial and was kept on ice. Samples were vacuum dried at room temperature with the addition of 100 µL of methanol. The dried samples were then stored on ice for MS use or at −20°C if there was a delay in analysis. Before injection in LC-MS, the samples were prepared by adding 60 µL of reconstitution solvent and sonicated for 10 seconds; they were then transferred to high-performance liquid chromatography vials containing inserts. Postprocessing Statistics and Pathway Analysis   Both univariate and multivariate statistical analyses were performed following data normalization using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 ( https://www.metaboanalyst.ca ). The data files were subjected to two sets of comparative analyses. 15  Set 1 compared the metabolomic variations in the young, middle, and elderly endophthalmitis groups versus their respective age-matched uninfected controls. Set 2 compared the metabolomic alterations among the different age groups with those in the endophthalmitis group ( Fig. 1 ). In the univariate statistical analysis, a volcano plot was developed to differentiate the significantly elevated or reduced metabolites with a fold change (FC) threshold of log 2 FC of ≥2. The unpaired Student's  t -test or Mann–Whitney test was used to derive the significance between the groups, and  P  < 0.05 was considered significant. The differential distribution pattern of metabolites was visualized by multivariate statistical analysis models, including unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) for set 1 and supervised partial least-squares discriminate analysis (PLS-DA) for set 2 comparisons. Further, the variable importance in projection (VIP) scores and heatmap analyses were used to analyze the important variables (metabolites) responsible for the differential cluster patterns of the principal components between the compared groups. The final significant metabolites were selected based on the observations made in both univariate and multivariate analyses with the criteria of log 2 FC of ≥2,  P  < 0.05, and VIP > 1. Alterations in the global metabolic pattern associated with the infection and senescence were identified using the pathway analysis module present in MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Results   Patient Demographics   In this study, vitreous samples were collected from 15 patients with bacterial endophthalmitis and 14 age-matched uninfected controls. The clinical demographics of all of these patients are summarized in  Table 1 . Among the 14 control patients, seven of the 14 controls had undergone retinal detachment surgeries, three had macular holes, two had epiretinal membrane complications, and two samples were obtained from retinal vasculitis and retinitis. The two controls with retinal vasculitis and retinitis were idiopathic and non-infective. Of the 15 endophthalmitis cases, two were positive for  Bacillus cereus , two for  Streptococcus mitis , and two for  Pseudomonas stutzeri . The other nine samples were found positive for  Staphylococcus epidermidis ,  Pasteurella canis ,  Pseudomonas fluorescens ,  Staphylococcus gallinarum ,  Enterococcus faecalis ,  Bacillus licheniformis ,  Staphylococcus  lugdunensis,  Staphylococcus kloosii , and  Pseudomonas aeruginosa . The clinical demographics of the three age-based groups with endophthalmitis are shown in  Table 2 . A total of 109 endogenous metabolites were identified from the subjected vitreous humor samples. Metabolomic Alterations Among Young, Middle, and Elderly Patients Within the Endophthalmitis Group   The acquired data were quantile normalized, cube-root transformed, and pareto scaled to reduce the skewness in the biological data and to obtain the Gaussian distribution. Comparative univariate analysis of the infected and control age groups showed 27 altered metabolites (17 elevated and 10 reduced) with a FC threshold of log 2 FC of ≥2 ( Fig. 3 a). Heatmap analysis also revealed a distinctly segregated cluster pattern between the groups ( Fig. 3 b). In multivariate analysis, the PLS-DA score plot clearly exhibited discrimination between two groups with 11.5% and 20.4% in PC1 and PC2, respectively ( Fig. 3 c). The metabolites that contributed to the differential cluster patterns between the groups are highlighted as VIP scores with the threshold of >1 ( Fig. 3 d). We observed the differential expression of metabolites such as 2,3-dinor-6-keto-prostaglandin F 1 α , leukotriene B4 ethanolamide, dihydrouracil, spermidine, uric acid, kynurenine,  N -acetyl-glutamic acid, hippuric acid, 2-hydroxybutyric acid, and butyrylcarnitine, all of which are associated with pathogenesis of endophthalmitis and aging. Furthermore, our analysis extended to the differential expression of metabolites irrespective of age by categorizing samples into Gram-positive and Gram-negative infection groups. Metabolites such as  P -hydroxyphenyllactic acid,  N 2 -acetylornithine, choline cation, vitamin C, and pantothenic acid were consistently upregulated in Gram-positive infected cases. Altered Metabolic Pathways in Different Age Groups With Endophthalmitis   Pathway analysis revealed several altered pathways in the vitreous humor of endophthalmitis patients compared to their age-matched controls. Differentially altered metabolic pathways in response to endophthalmitis were noted among the different age groups. The metabolism of amino acids (tyrosine, arginine, proline, phenylalanine, tryptophan, aspartate, and glutamate), nucleic acids (purine), steroids, and transsulfuration was highly altered in younger patients with endophthalmitis compared to their age-matched controls ( Fig. 5 a), whereas the metabolism of biotin, nucleic acids, amino acids, and cysteine sulfinic acid was altered in older patients ( Fig. 5 b) Additionally, pathways such as amino acid, propanoate, nucleic acid, and cysteine sulfinic acid metabolism were altered in older patients with endophthalmitis as compared to the younger patients with endophthalmitis ( Fig. 5 c). Discussion   Aging is a well-understood risk factor for several ocular complications, including age-related macular degeneration, cataract, diabetic retinopathy, and dry eyes. 16  Several studies have shown that the complexity of the host immune response is also influenced by age-related changes that, in turn, increase its susceptibility to infections. 17  Although the severity of ocular infections depends on an organism's virulence factor, host–pathogen interactions, and individual host immunological responses, understanding the host metabolism in endophthalmitis is crucial to obtaining insights into signature molecules that can lead to the rational design of therapeutic targets. 18  Our exploratory work on metabolomics was conducted with two goals: (1) compare the vitreous metabolome of endophthalmitis patients with their age-matched uninfected controls by categorizing them in three different age groups (young, middle, and elderly) to study the altered metabolome in endophthalmitis and decode the age-dependent variations in metabolic response for endophthalmitis; and (2) to compare the altered metabolomes among the different age groups (young, middle, and elderly) within endophthalmitis patients to relatively quantify age-dependent metabolome variations in infecti
