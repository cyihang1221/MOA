<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/c079/8945079/22805dab08a5/ao1c07272_0009.jpg" loading="lazy" id="ab-tgr1" height="298" width="658" alt="graphic file with name ao1c07272_0009.jpg"></p>
<p>Dissociation induced
by the accumulation of internal energy via
collisions of ions with neutral molecules is one of the most important
fragmentation techniques in mass spectrometry (MS), and the identification
of small singly charged molecules is based mainly on the consideration
of the fragmentation spectrum. Many research studies have been dedicated
to the creation of databases of experimentally measured tandem mass
spectrometry (MS/MS) spectra (such as MzCloud, Metlin, etc.) and developing
software for predicting MS/MS fragments in silico from the molecular
structure (such as MetFrag, CFM-ID, CSI:FingerID, etc.). However,
the fragmentation mechanisms and pathways are still not fully understood.
One of the limiting obstacles is that protomers (positive ions protonated
at different sites) produce different fragmentation spectra, and these
spectra overlap in the case of the presence of different protomers.
Here, we are proposing to use a combination of two powerful approaches:
computing fragmentation trees that carry information of all consecutive
fragmentations and consideration of the MS/MS data of isotopically
labeled compounds. We have created PyFragMS—a web tool consisting
of a database of annotated MS/MS spectra of isotopically labeled molecules
(after H/D and/or <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange) and a collection
of instruments for computing fragmentation trees for an arbitrary
molecule. Using PyFragMS, we investigated how the site of protonation
influences the fragmentation pathway for small molecules. Also, PyFragMS
offers capabilities for performing database search when MS/MS data
of the isotopically labeled compounds are taken into account.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Liquid chromatography–tandem
mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)
has become a key technique for the modern high-throughput omics technologies,<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a>−<a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup> and currently, it is the method of choice for screening drugs, pesticides,
and metabolites in complex biological mixtures. According to the regulation
rules, a compound can be considered identified if the measured chromatographic
elution time, accurate mass, and fragmentation spectrum match those
obtained for the pure chemical standard of this compound using the
same equipment and experimental conditions.<sup><a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> The rapid accumulation of fragmentation spectra began in 1950s,<sup><a href="#ref6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref6">6</a></sup> and currently, there are extensive databases
of electron ionization (such as NIST or Wiley) and collision-induced
dissociation spectra including MzCloud<sup><a href="#ref7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref7">7</a></sup> (<a href="https://www.mzcloud.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.mzcloud.org/</a>), Metlin,<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a>−<a href="#ref10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref10">10</a></sup> NIST, and several others.<sup><a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a>−<a href="#ref13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref13">13</a></sup> However, even
now, the size of such databases is insufficient. For example, the
most recent release NIST 20 contains tandem data for ∼30,000
compounds.<sup><a href="#ref14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref14">14</a></sup> At the same time, there are
more than 100,000,000 described molecules (PubChem) and many more
chemically possible compounds (GDB-17 contains 166 billion compounds).<sup><a href="#ref15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref15">15</a></sup></p>
<p>Another problem that is rarely paid attention
to is that during
ionization, gas-phase isomeric ions can be formed, which differ only
by the location of the site of protonation or deprotonation. For the
positive ion electrospray ionization (pos-ESI) mode, such ions are
called protomers, and they can be separated only using ion mobility
spectrometry.<sup><a href="#ref16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref16">16</a>,<a href="#ref17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref17">17</a></sup> Different protomers can be obtained
by varying the solvent composition<sup><a href="#ref18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref18">18</a></sup> or
by ion–molecule reactions.<sup><a href="#ref19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref19">19</a></sup> It
is known that the MS/MS spectra of different protomers can be remarkably
different. Fragmentation spectra available in the databases mentioned
above are in fact the overlap of fragmentation spectra corresponding
to different protomers. In addition, the ionizing proton can migrate
during the excitation. This is especially important when considering
the fragmentation of a peptide using the mobile proton model.<sup><a href="#ref20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref20">20</a></sup></p>
<p>Many research studies have been carried
out in the field of development
of in silico fragmentation tools, which can be used to identify compounds
without a reference MS/MS spectrum. Weissberg and Dagan published
68 fragmentation rules for the interpretation and prediction of ESI-MS/MS
spectra.<sup><a href="#ref21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref21">21</a></sup> Kebarle, Futrell, and others
used RRKM (Rice–Ramsperger–Kassel–Marcus) modeling
and consideration of internal vibrational energy distributions to
explain fragmentation of ions.<sup><a href="#ref22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref22">22</a>−<a href="#ref26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref26">26</a></sup> Many attempts were undertaken to use quantum chemistry for the interpretation
of fragmentation spectra.<sup><a href="#ref27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref27">27</a>−<a href="#ref32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref32">32</a></sup> Other software packages were created for the prediction of fragmentation,<sup><a href="#ref33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref33">33</a>−<a href="#ref36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref36">36</a></sup> and currently, the most advanced software package is MassFrontier
(uses a rule-based approach and expert curation), MS-FINDER<sup><a href="#ref37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref37">37</a></sup> (uses a set of hydrogen rearrangement rules),
MetFrag<sup><a href="#ref38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref38">38</a></sup> (uses iterative bond cleavage),
CFM-ID,<sup><a href="#ref39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref39">39</a>,<a href="#ref40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref40">40</a></sup> and SIRIUS 4,<sup><a href="#ref41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref41">41</a>,<a href="#ref42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref42">42</a></sup> which integrates
CSI:FingerID<sup><a href="#ref36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref36">36</a></sup> (uses fragmentation trees).
Since experimental MS/MS spectra contain ions formed by several fragmentation
events, it is convenient to use a fragmentation tree concept. A fragmentation
tree is the directed graph in which two product ions are connected
with an edge only if they can be directly attributed to a single fragmentation
event. Such a concept is implemented in different manners in MetFrag,
MS-FINDER, and CFM-ID. In the case of CSI:FingerID (see Böcker
et al.<sup><a href="#ref43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref43">43</a>−<a href="#ref47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref47">47</a></sup>), tree nodes are annotated with the molecular formulas of the fragments,
and the edges represent (neutral or radical) losses.</p>
<p>It is obvious
that the annotation of MS/MS fragments and even the
quality of in silico fragmentation algorithms can be considerably
improved by using large data sets of MS/MS spectra of isotopically
labeled compounds. Indeed, it was the use of labeled compounds that
helped discover intramolecular rearrangements such as the McLafferty
rearrangement,<sup><a href="#ref48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref48">48</a></sup> formation of the tropylium
ion,<sup><a href="#ref49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref49">49</a></sup> and so forth.<sup><a href="#ref50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref50">50</a>−<a href="#ref52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref52">52</a></sup> Unfortunately,
there are no such public data sets (due to the high cost of isotopically
labeled compounds), and only in MetFrag was an attempt undertaken
to use the MS/MS data of isotopically labeled compounds for improving
the quality of annotation and prediction.<sup><a href="#ref53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref53">53</a></sup> In addition, no software can account for different sites of ionization.</p>
<p>Recently, we demonstrated a cheap and simple approach for the combination
of isotope exchange reactions (H/D and <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O) with high-resolution MS<sup><a href="#ref54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref54">54</a>−<a href="#ref56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref56">56</a></sup> in order to improve the reliability
of compound identification.<sup><a href="#ref54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref54">54</a>,<a href="#ref57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref57">57</a></sup> Under the proposed
conditions, the number of exchanges can be predicted if the structure
of the molecule is known.<sup><a href="#ref58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref58">58</a>−<a href="#ref61" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref61">61</a></sup></p>
<p>The isotope exchange reaction combined with
high-resolution MS
is also a valuable analytical tool for the identification of unknowns.
Indeed, the number of exchanges (H/D and <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O) determined experimentally for an unknown molecule can serve as
an additional structural descriptor during the database search (by
filtering by the number of groups such as −OH, −COOH,
=O, and so forth). We recently showed that this can reduce
the number of candidates by a factor of 10.<sup><a href="#ref54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref54">54</a></sup> Additional improvement of the accuracy of identification can be
achieved by considering the MS/MS spectra of the compounds after isotope
exchange. Recently, we demonstrated that for the given MS/MS spectrum
of an unknown molecule after the <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange
reaction, the number of possible candidates with a known structure
but unknown MS/MS spectra can be reduced via the substructure mapping
based on the molecular formula of MS/MS fragments and predicted position
of <sup>18</sup>O labels.<sup><a href="#ref62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref62">62</a>,<a href="#ref63" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref63">63</a></sup> It is obvious that
the accurate consideration of the fragmentation pathways may improve
the quality of the identification even more.</p>
<p>Here, we report
PyFragMS—a web tool consisting of a database
of annotated MS/MS spectra of isotopically labeled molecules (after
H/D and <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange), tools and algorithms
for the creation of the fragmentation tree for an arbitrary molecule
(including isotopically labeled compounds), and tools for the identification
of molecules based on the experimentally measured MS/MS spectra after
the isotope exchange reaction. We demonstrate how it can assist in
the annotation of MS/MS product ions, investigation of the fragmentation
pathways, and identification of the unknown.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>
<section id="sec2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Samples</h3>
<p>All drug standards were provided by the Central
Toxicological laboratory of Russian Ministry of Health.</p></section><section id="sec2.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data Acquisition
and Processing</h3>
<p>Mass spectra were acquired
on a QExactive Orbitrap system (Thermo Fischer Scientific) with a
modified matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization/ESI injector
(Spectroglyph, LLC), operated in the ESI mode at a 140,000 resolution
both for MS and MS/MS measurements. Solutions of target compounds
in a methanol–water mixture (1:1) were infused with the flow
rate of 1 μL·min<sup>–1</sup> and the spray voltage
of 3 kV. Precursor ions corresponding to protonated (or deuterated)
molecules and the isotope exchange reaction products were isolated
with a 0.4 Da window and fragmented with various collision energy
values ranging from 10 to 90 NCE. Nitrogen was used as the collision
gas. Also, for some compounds, an LC–MS/MS system was used
(see the <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.1c07272/suppl_file/ao1c07272_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supporting Information</a> for more
details). All spectra were preprocessed using XCalibur 4.1 software
(Thermo Fischer Scientific) to extract MS/MS spectra for all target
ions averaged by different collision energies.</p></section><section id="sec2.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Isotope Exchange Reaction</h3>
<p>Hydrogen/deuterium (H/D)
exchange was performed in the ESI source using our previously developed
approach.<sup><a href="#ref54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref54">54</a></sup> The vapors of D<sub>2</sub>O were infused into the desolvation capillary heated to 300 °C.
H/D exchange occurs both in the gas phase and due to the moisture
penetration into the droplets<sup><a href="#ref64" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref64">64</a></sup> in the
liquid phase. The details of the ion source design and its application
to investigate various compounds can be found in our previous publications.<sup><a href="#ref65" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref65">65</a></sup> To perform oxygen <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange, we followed the procedure described by Samuel and Silver.<sup><a href="#ref66" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref66">66</a></sup> The target compound was dissolved in H<sub>2</sub><sup>18</sup>O; the solution was placed in a sealed glass vial and
heated at 95 °C for 15 h.</p>
<p>PyFragMS front-end was developed
using Anvil (<a href="https://anvil.works/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://anvil.works/</a>). Anvil allows us to build web apps using Python. PyFragMS back-end
was developed using Python. Operations with molecules were realized
using the RDKit library (<a href="https://www.rdkit.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.rdkit.org/</a>) and “molmass” package. The PyFragMS web interface
is described in the <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.1c07272/suppl_file/ao1c07272_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supporting Information</a>. For plotting of fragmentation, GraphViz software was used.</p>
<p>The PyFragMS database includes the largest public data set of MS/MS
spectra,<sup><a href="#ref67" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref67">67</a></sup> previously published MS/MS spectra
of compounds after H/D exchange,<sup><a href="#ref53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref53">53</a></sup> and
data acquired by us (MS/MS spectra of compounds after H/D and <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange). Assigning of molecular formulas
to fragment ions was carried out using Sirius 4 software.<sup><a href="#ref41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref41">41</a></sup> Currently, the database contains MS/MS spectra
for &gt;5000 molecules, including spectra for &gt;1000 molecules after
H/D
exchange and spectra for &gt;100 molecules after the <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange reaction. The majority of the spectra correspond
to pos-ESI, and for &gt;200 compounds, the negative ion ESI (neg-ESI)
spectra are available. For building fragmentation trees, users can
use the data stored in our database or upload their own data. If any
of the readers wish to contribute their data to the PyFragMS database,
we encourage you to contact the authors.</p>

is shown in <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>, and the PyFragMS algorithm to compute the
fragmentation tree is shown in <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>.</p>

<section id="sec3.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Database of Isotopically
Labeled Compounds</h3>
<p>Compounds
labeled with stable isotopes have found wide application in MS-based
studies. However, as they are primarily used for purposes of quantitative
analysis, these compounds are designed to carry isotope labels in
a stable part of a molecule (<sup>13</sup>C, <sup>15</sup>N, or deuterium
in −C–H covalent bonds). It provides a stable isotope
composition during long-term storage and use. The synthesis of such
isotope analogues is an expensive and complicated process<sup><a href="#ref68" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref68">68</a></sup> that limits the use of isotopically labeled
compounds to study fragmentation and for untargeted identification.<sup><a href="#ref69" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref69">69</a></sup> For example, the MzCloud database contains a
very limited number of deuterated molecules; most of them carry the
heavy isotope in the terminal methylene groups, which reduces their
usefulness for improving the annotation of MS/MS fragment ions.</p>
<p>Recently, we proposed an ion source and a method to perform H/D exchange
in the inlet desolvation capillary of a mass spectrometer (see <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>A). This technique
allows for the counting of labile hydrogen atoms. Such information
can enhance MS identification of chemical compounds. When ions with
isotope labels produced from the H/D exchange reaction are fragmented
in the collision cell of a mass spectrometer, additional information
about the molecular structure may be gained from the resulting MS/MS
spectra. In-source H/D exchange and in-solution <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange provide an easy and cheap way to collect an MS/MS
spectra database of isotopically labeled compounds with a diverse
distribution of the label across the molecular structure. Such a database
may be used to investigate fragmentation, improve fragment annotation,
or predict fragmentation spectra, which is important for untargeted
identification workflows. In PyFragMS, we accumulated previously published
MS/MS spectra of compounds after H/D exchange<sup><a href="#ref53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref53">53</a></sup> and the data acquired by us (MS/MS spectra of compounds after H/D
and <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange).</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="fig3"><h4 class="obj_head">Figure 3.</h4>

<p>We
will demonstrate the
developed approach by computing fragmentation trees and determining
the correct fragmentation pathways for methylenedioxypyrovalerone
(MDPV), a psychoactive designer drug. The acquired MS/MS spectra in
the pos-ESI mode for MDPV, MDPV after H/D exchange, and MDPV after <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange are shown in <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>B. We can see that certain oxygen containing
fragments carry an <sup>18</sup>O isotope; also, we can see that only
two fragments (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> = 234 and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> = 84) carry deuterium (note that we
are using only integer values of ions in the text). Assigning the
correct number of exchanges for each fragment was performed based
on the accurate mass difference for H/D exchange (1.006277 Da) and
for <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange (2.004245 Da). Despite
the fact that neutral MDPV does not have protogenic groups, the proton
attaching during ionization in the pos-ESI mode is labile and is exchanged
for deuterium. It is worth noting that we may not observe intermediate
short-lived metastable ions.</p>
<p>When investigating the fragmentation
pathways of any molecule, we must take into account the possible coexistence
of several protomers (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>C). The computed fragmentation trees for MDPV protonated at
two different sites are shown in <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>D. The proposed algorithm was able to assign
structures to most fragment peaks (seven out of eight), observed in
the experimental MS/MS spectrum of MDPV. The use of isotope labels
allowed considerable reduction of the number of possible candidate
structures. We can see that it is possible to propose structures for <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 233 for both protonation sites; however, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 126 can be formed only when the C=O
group is protonated. Accordingly, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values of 205, 175, 149, and 84 correspond to the protonation of
nitrogen. One peak with <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 135
was not annotated. In <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>E, the correct pathway that leads to this fragment formation
is shown, as was described by Davidson.<sup><a href="#ref70" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref70">70</a></sup> This pathway was proven by authors using MS<sup>n</sup> experiments,
DFT calculations, and infrared ion spectroscopy. Unfortunately, currently,
our algorithm cannot account for possible intramolecular rearrangement.
In <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>F, we show
the annotation of fragment ions performed using MzCloud and using
MetFrag. It can be seen that both MzCloud and MetFrag incorrectly
annotated <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 135. The proposed
annotation is wrong because such a fragment should carry an <sup>18</sup>O label after <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange; however, in
the experimental spectrum, there is no such label.</p>
<p>Note that
when computing a fragmentation tree, PyFragMS allows
the elemental composition of the theoretical structure to differ from
that experimentally measured by one hydrogen. This allows for the
possible hydrogen transfer during the fragmentation. Also, PyFragMS
produces a table output showing for each MS/MS ion the IDs of the
corresponding nodes in the fragmentation tree and IDs of nodes for
which experimental and theoretical formulas coincide or differ. The
decision on which nodes remain should be made by the user.</p>
<p>There
are many molecules that produce protomers in the ESI source,
and it often happens that there can be three or even more possible
sites of protonation. Generally, the bigger the molecule is, the more
the possible sites of protonation are. In <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">4</a></a>, we demonstrate the automatically generated
fragmentation tree for a bisacodyl molecule considering three different
sites of protonation. It is remarkable that when nitrogen is protonated,
then structures are found for only one fragment (out of six). Also,
the structures of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 319 are different
depending on which oxygen in the ester group is protonated. When the
C=O group is protonated, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 319 can be further fragmented to yield only <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 183, while when −O– is protonated, one of
the possible structures of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 319
can yield <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 226, which further
produces <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 183, 154, 199, and
166.</p>

<figcaption><p>Automatically generated fragmentation tree for the bisacodyl molecule
with three different sites of protonation (marked with an arrow).
(A–C) Trees for different protonation sites, (D) fragment ions,
and (E) structure of the bisacodyl molecule.</p></figcaption></figure><p>Since protomers produce different fragment ions during the fragmentation
process, it is important to accumulate a set of fragmentation trees
in order to understand which fragmentation pathway is favored. We
started computing and analyzing fragmentation trees for compounds
with only one exchangeable proton (the one attached during ionization).
Because the fragmentation is a multistep process, we focused on annotating
the fragments resulting from a single fragmentation event. Our results
for several selected molecules are summarized in <a href="#fig5" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig5" class="usa-link">5</a></a> (more data can be found in
the <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acsomega.1c07272/suppl_file/ao1c07272_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supporting Information</a>). We can see
that deuterium seldom appears in the fragment ions. Previously, many
research studies have been dedicated to the role of protonation on
the electron density rearrangement in the molecule and on the bond
dissociation.<sup><a href="#ref71" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref71">71</a></sup> The bond weakening induced
by the protonation may be explained by the tendency of a protonated
atom to recover its electroneutrality by lowering the electron density
along bonds with neighbor atoms. As a result of fragmentation, the
fragment with a proton (or deuterium) becomes neutral, and the rest
of the molecule becomes positively charged.<sup><a href="#ref71" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref71">71</a></sup> Summarizing the experimental results, we formulated the following
rules that explain the majority of the experimental data, not including
intramolecular rearrangements:</p>
<ul id="list1" class="list" style="list-style-type:none">
<li>
<span class="label">(1)</span><p class="display-inline">A molecule can be protonated at different
sites due to lone electron pairs of heteroatoms (N, O, and S).</p>
</li>
<li>
<span class="label">(2)</span><p class="display-inline">The primary fragmentation
occurs near
the protonation site due to the electron density rearrangement. As
a result of fragmentation, the fragment with a proton (or deuterium)
is neutral, and the fragment without a proton carries a charge. We
observe this in the spectrum.</p>
</li>
<li>
<span class="label">(3)</span><p class="display-inline">Less preferably, the fragmentation
can occur in any other place of a molecule; however, the even number
of electrons rule must be followed.<sup><a href="#ref72" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref72">72</a></sup> This
includes hydrogen rearrangement from neighboring atoms if necessary.</p>
</li>
</ul>

even electron rule.</p>

<p>PyFragMS offers functionality
for the identification of the unknown. PyFragMS allows choosing between
conventional cosine and Jaccard similarity measures when performing
MS/MS search. In addition, the reduction of the search space is possible
based on the number of H/D and <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O of the
precursor ion. Because PyFragMS contains the database of MS/MS spectra
of isotopically labeled molecules, it is possible to use isotopically
labeled fragment ions for MS/MS search. Such an approach considerably
improves the reliability of the identification. As a model problem,
we considered the identification of MDPV when we measured precursor
ions and only one fragment ion (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> = 149). However, we possess information of the number of labile
H and O in the precursor and fragment. In <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>, we can see how the use of the isotope exchange
improves the identification.</p>

at the first stage. Trees obtained for molecules and corresponding
MS/MS spectra look more reasonable.</p>

<p>We have created PyFragMS—a web tool consisting
of a database
of annotated MS/MS spectra of isotopically labeled molecules (after
H/D and <sup>16</sup>O/<sup>18</sup>O exchange), instruments for creating
the fragmentation tree for an arbitrary molecule, and tools for the
identification of the unknown using isotope exchange information and
MS/MS data. It was demonstrated that a simple isotope exchange experiment
performed by using a previously described approach and subsequent
consideration of MS/MS data of labeled compounds via computing fragmentation
trees improves fragment annotation and allows investigation of fragmentation
pathways. Using PyFragMS, it is possible to investigate how the site
of protonation influences the fragmentation pathway for small molecules.
Currently, we are using PyFragMS to build a large database of correct
fragmentation pathways taking into account different possible sites
of protonation or deprotonation. We believe that such a database will
considerably help improve the quality of the software for predicting
MS/MS fragments in silico, and we are inviting researchers working
with isotopically labeled molecules to share data that will be included
into the PyFragMS database (feel free to contact authors). The use
of the MS/MS data of isotopically labeled compounds can considerably
increase the reliability of the identification, which is especially
important when the precursor ion is not known (SWATH,<sup><a href="#ref73" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref73">73</a></sup> etc.).</p></section><section id="notes1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supporting Information Available</h2>
