>Materials and methods   Microorganisms and growth conditions   The pathogenic fungal strain  P. arachidicola  was obtained from the Institute of Plant Protection, Liaoning Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Shenyang. The antagonistic bacterial strain was isolated from cultivated peanut soil. A total of 10 soil samples were collected in sterile polythene bags, each containing 5 g of soil. A serial dilution was made to obtain a pure culture of the strain. One gram of soil was added to 9 mL of distilled water in a test tube, and a series of dilutions was prepared as follows: 10 − 2 , 10 − 3 , and 10 − 4 .   Then, 1 mL of the suspension was transferred into the next 3 test tubes, and at the final transfer, 1 mL from the last dilution of the soil sample was transferred by pipette and poured onto LB agar media (10 g of tryptone, 5 g of yeast extract, 5 g of NaCl, and 15 g of agar/L), and on nutrient agar media (10 g of peptide, 10 g of beef extract, 8 g of NaCl, and 15 g of agar/L) separately. The mixture was subsequently incubated for 7 days at 28 °C.   Finally, a colony was isolated and purified. The 6 purified strains were screened against the pathogen  P. arachidicola . On the basis of its strong antifungal activity, the most potent antagonistic strain was selected. The pure antagonistic strain WY-65 was cultivated for 3 days at 28 °C in the dark on LB agar medium. Pathogenic  P. arachidicola  fungi were cultured for 14 days at 28 °C in the dark on potato dextrose agar (PDA). Additionally, an inoculum suspension of  P. arachidicola  was prepared in potato dextrose broth (PDB), which consisted of 10 g potato extract and 10 g dextrose per 1000 mL of distilled water. For suspension preparation, 40 mL of PDB was dispensed into each 200 mL flask, and a 5 mm diameter agar plug containing actively growing  P. arachidicola  mycelium was inoculated into each flask. The cultures were incubated at 28 °C in the dark for 14 days [ 10 ]. Molecular and morphological identification of strains   After DNA extraction with a commercial kit from Sangon Biotech, the 16S rRNA,  rpoB , and  gyrA  genes were amplified via primers, and the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) conditions are detailed in Supplementary Table  S1 . The annealing temperature conditions for 16S rRNA were as follows: initial temperature 95 °C for 5 min; 30 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 57 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 90 s; and final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Similarly, for the  gyrA  gene, the initial temperature conditions started at 94 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. For the  rpoB gene,  the initial temperature conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 3 min; 30 cycles at 94 °C for 1 min, 56 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 2 min; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The resulting sequences were compared to GenBank reference sequences via the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST). The sequencing data were then used to construct a phylogenetic tree via the maximum likelihood (ML) method in MEGA v.11 software. The nucleotide sequence was deposited in GenBank under the accession number  PV770029 . For morphological observation, strain WY-65 was grown under defined conditions. The samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde. A dehydration procedure was performed using a sequence of ethanol solutions (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%) to eliminate water and avoid structural collapse. Dehydrated samples were coated with gold. To observe the morphology of strain WY-65, a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi, Japan) was used [ 39 ]. Strain WY-65 was streaked on LB media and incubated at 27 °C for 24 hours. Colony morphology was observed, and physiological and biochemical identification methods were performed according to standard protocols, as previously described [ 40 ]. Fermentation process   Basic fermentation media   The biphasic submerged fermentation procedure was executed following the method described by Wang et al. [ 9 ]. First, to synthesize the spore suspension, a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 40 mL of Luria–Bertani (LB) medium was inoculated with two 5-mm spore cakes of the WY-65 strain. Consequently, the basic fermentation medium was prepared with exact quantities of 7 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L tryptone, and 2.5 g/L NaCl. The pH was carefully adjusted from 6.6 to 6.8 by adding 0.5 M HCl and 1.0 M NaOH solution. Afterward, the fermentation medium was subjected to sterilization via an autoclave. To initiate the submerged fermentation process, a 5 mL aliquot of spore suspension, maintaining a concentration of 1 × 10 9  CFU/mL, was poured into each flask containing previously sterilized fermentation nutrient medium. After inoculation, the fermentation medium was incubated in a rotary shaker operating at 30 °C with shaking at 165 rpm for 96 hours. The fermentation broth of strain WY-65 obtained from both BFM and CFM cultures was first centrifuged at 10,000×g rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cells and debris. Cocktail fermentation medium   The same biphasic submerged fermentation procedure described above was executed. A novel cocktail fermentation medium (CFM) was formulated in this study. The composition was as follows: 4 g/L semolina flour, 4 g/L soybean meal, 4 g/L corn syrup, 4 g/L peanut root powder, 12.5 g/L beef extract, and 0.5 g/L MgSO₄. The initial pH of the medium was adjusted to 6.6–6.8 prior to sterilization. The peanut root extract was prepared by washing fresh peanut roots thoroughly with distilled water, drying them in an oven at 50 °C, and grinding them into a fine powder. To initiate the submerged fermentation process, a 5 mL aliquot of spore suspension of strain WY65, maintaining a concentration of 1 × 10 9  CFU/mL, was poured into each flask containing sterilized CFM. After inoculation, the fermentation medium was incubated in a rotary shaker operating at 30 °C with shaking at 165 rpm for 96 hours. These nutrient media were selected on the basis of antifungal activity, as described in a previous report [ 41 ]. The fermentation broth of strain WY-65 obtained from both BFM and CFM cultures was first centrifuged at 10,000×g rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cells and debris. In vitro bioassays of strain WY-65 against  P. arachidicola     The antifungal activities of CFM and BFM were assessed via the poisoned food method described by Grover and Moore (1962), with modifications. The CFSs derived from CFM and BFM were incorporated into molten potato dextrose agar (PDA) cooled to 40 °C. The medium was mixed thoroughly, dispensed into 90 mm sterile Petri plates at 20 mL per plate, and then allowed to solidify. The control plates contained PDA without the test agent. A 5 mm mycelial disc from the actively growing margin of a 7-day-old pathogen culture on PDA was placed at the center of each plate. The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 14 days or until the control plates exhibited nearly complete fungal growth. The radial growth of fungal colonies was measured along two perpendicular axes, and the mean colony diameter was recorded. Each treatment was performed in triplicate. Controlled environment study on plant disease control   In a controlled greenhouse environment, two peanut seeds were sown in each of four 4-inch plastic pots containing nutrient-rich, sterilized soil. There were three pots for each treatment. The plants were grown under a temperature regime of 30 °C during the day and 24 °C at night, with adequate sunlight and humidity to support optimal growth. After 30 days of growth, when the plants reached the five-leaf stage (comprising approximately 20 leaflets), inoculation treatments were applied by soaking the leaves for 5 minutes. For the positive control, a solution was prepared by dissolving 100 mL of commercial tebuconazole fungicide in 20 mL of water. Action of the WY-65 strain against  P. arachidicola     SEM analysis of mycelia and spores   The effects of CFS derived from CFM and BFM on the morphology of  P. arachidicola  hyphae were assessed by collecting mycelial samples from peripheral regions. Both treated and untreated control mycelia were excised and fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 4 hours. After fixation, the samples were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.5 M, pH 7.0), subjected to secondary fixation with 1% aqueous osmium tetroxide, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The samples were then immersed in isoamyl acetate overnight, subjected to critical point drying with carbon dioxide, and coated with a thin layer of gold via a sputter coater. Hyphal structural alterations were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) [ 42 ]. Efflux pump analysis   To assess the effects of CFM and BFM on the efflux pump activity of  P. arachidicola, a  Rh6G efflux assay was employed. Briefly, pathogen cells were incubated in potato dextrose broth (PDB) overnight. Then, in the later stages, the cells were harvested. The samples were subsequently washed with glucose-free PBS and adjusted to 10 7  CFU/mL. Later, a solution of Rh6G was added to the cell suspension at a final concentration of 10 μM, and the suspension was incubated at 35 °C for 50 minutes. At the same time, an ice water bath was used for 10 minutes to expose the sample. After harvesting, the cells were washed with glucose-free PBS. Glucose/PBS (5%) was added to resuspend the cells, and CFM and BFM were added to different samples. The fluorescence intensity was measured every 60 min for 4 h every 60 min with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 530 nm by flow cytometry [ 43 ]. ROS analysis   To determine the effects of CFM and CBM on the level of ROS in the pathogen. After being treated with both CFSs, the cells of the pathogen were processed with the probe 20,70-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCF-DA) at a final concentration of 3 µM. The probe 20,70-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (H2DCF) is produced when the esterase in the cell cleaves the acetate groups of H2DCF-DA. Later, the oxidation of H2DCF was caused by intracellular ROS. Later, it yielded the fluorescent product 20,70-dichlorofluorescein (DCF). The measurement of fluorescence was performed via a fluorescence microplate reader at excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 535 nm, respectively. The method was followed from [ 44 ] with modification. Extraction of metabolites   The cell-free supernatant of strain WY-65 was used for metabolite analysis. For each condition, a 100 μL aliquot of the clarified broth was transferred into a 2 mL Eppendorf tube and subjected to metabolite extraction. Later, an extraction solvent with a volume of 400 μL of methanol:acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) was added, which was labeled an isotopic internal standard mixture. Then, homogenization of the samples was carried out at 35 Hz for 4 minutes, and the samples were placed in a water bath for 5 minutes for the sonication process. For an efficient extraction process, the above mentioned whole cyclic process was repeated three times. The samples were incubated for 1 h at 40 °C, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm (RCF = 13800 ×g; rotor radius = 8.6 cm) for 15 min at 4 °C. The obtained supernatant was placed with certain care into clean glass vials for further analysis. To prepare a quality control (QC) sample, equal volumes of supernatants from each sample were mixed. In this investigation, four quality control (QC) samples and six experimental samples were examined. LC‒MS/MS analysis   A Vanquish ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) connected to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to conduct liquid chromatography‒tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS) investigations. For the chromatographic separation process, a Waters BEH amide column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm particle size) was used. The mobile phase was set up with two solutions: (A) 25 mmol L − 1  ammonium hydroxide and 25 mmol L − 1  ammonium acetate in water at pH 9.75 and (B) acetonitrile. The injection volume was set at 2 μL, and the autosampler was maintained at 4 °C. In “Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific),”, which continually monitors the full-scan mass spectrum to initiate MS/MS scans, data acquisition was carried out in “information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode.” The gas flow rate was set up in the following mode: the auxiliary gas flow rate was 15 Arb, whereas the sheath gas flow rate was 50 arbitrary units (Arb). The temperature of the capillary was set at 350 °C. These were the electrospray ionization (ESI) source characteristics. The full-scan MS resolution was 60,000, and the MS/MS resolution was 30,000. In stepped mode, normalized collision energies (NCE) of 20, 30, and 40 were applied. In positive ion mode, the spray voltage was set to + 3 kV, whereas in negative ion mode, it was set to −3 kV. Statistical analysis of differentially abundant metabolites and pathway annotation   A total of 24,329 peaks remained after denoising on the basis of the relative standard deviation from the initial 37,491 peaks that were found. Half of the lowest detected value was used to impute missing data. All the data were normalized according to the internal standard.   The processed dataset, including peak number, sample identity, and normalized peak areas, was imported into SIMCA 16.0.2 software (Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB, Umeå, Sweden) for multivariate statistical analysis. To reduce noise and account for variable variance, the data were scaled and log-transformed prior to analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA), an unsupervised dimensionality reduction technique, was employed to visualize the sample distribution and clustering. A 95% confidence interval in the PCA score plot was used to identify potential outliers.   To further distinguish metabolic differences between groups, supervised orthogonal projections to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed. Model robustness and predictive power were assessed through 7-fold cross-validation, yielding R 2  (explained variance) and Q 2  (predictive accuracy) values. To validate the OPLS-DA model, a permutation test was conducted with 200 iterations. Intercept values for R 2  and Q 2  were obtained to evaluate the risk of overfitting, with a lower Q 2  intercept indicating greater model reliability.   Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores from the first principal component of the OPLS-DA model were calculated. Metabolites with VIP > 1.0 and  p  values < 0.05 (Student’s t test) were considered significantly altered. These differentially abundant metabolites were subjected to metabolic pathway enrichment analysis via the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG;  http://www.genome.jp/kegg ) and MetaboAnalyst ( http://www.metaboanalyst.ca ). Univariate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was also performed, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the diagnostic potential of key metabolites. Confirmation of the compounds   To test the antagonism of the main compounds synthesized by the WY-65 strain, we selected only the commercially available compounds from the VIP compounds from the metabolomic analysis, i.e., 3-indoleacrylic acid (CAS no. 1204–06-4) and L-tryptophan (CAS no. 73–22-3), purchased from Sigma Aldrich. A food poisoning method was adopted to determine the bioactivity of these compounds. The stock solution was prepared at a concentration of 10 mg/mL by dissolving 1 g of each compound in 100 mL of sterile distilled water. For bioactivity in 20 mL of molten PDA medium, upon cooling, 150 μL, 200 μL, or 300 μL of each compound was added, and for both compounds, the same method was used. This mixture was subsequently inoculated with 5 mL of a spore suspension (1 × 10 ×7  spores/mL) of  P. arachidichola . The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 14 days or until the control plates exhibited nearly complete fungal growth. The radial growth of fungal colonies was measured along two perpendicular axes, and the mean colony diameter was recorded. Each treatment was performed in triplicate. Statistical analysis   All the statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism v10.5. In vitro data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Comparisons between BFM and CFM treatments were conducted via an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. Results   Molecular and morphological identification of the WY-65 strain   The colony morphology results revealed a whitish appearance of the WY-65 strain on LB agar media (Fig.  1 A). Similarly, SEM revealed that strain WY-65 exhibited a typical rod shape, resembling those of other strains of  B. velezensis  (Fig.  1 B). The phylogenetic tree based on the 16S rRNA (Fig.  1 C),  rpoB , and  gyrA  genes (Figure  S1 a, b) indicated that strain WY-65 belongs to  B. velezensis . The 16S rRNA,  rpoB , and  gyrA  genes of strain WY-65 were amplified and sequenced, resulting in amplicons of 1492 bp, 750 bp, and 1100 bp, respectively.
