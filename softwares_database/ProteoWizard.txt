These effluent samples contain a high organic load, resulting in pronounced matrix-associated effects in high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), such as signal overlay—making them particularly suitable for robustness testing of the centroiding algorithm. Importantly, the chromatographic domain was intentionally neglected during algorithm development—i.e., spectra were treated as independent observations—to ensure universal applicability regardless of whether prior chromatographic separation was performed.

software_configuration_description: ProteoWizard msconvert was used to process raw mass spectrometry data and implement the demultiplexing algorithm for overlapping-window DIA data. Specifically, the demultiplexing algorithm—based on linear non-negative least squares (Lawson-Hanson solver) and designed for partially overlapping isolation windows—was integrated into ProteoWizard msconvert to produce full demultiplexed spectra. The algorithm operates on MS/MS spectra acquired with 20 m/z overlapping windows (e.g., alternating cycles offset by −10 m/z), assigning fragment ion signals to correct 10 m/z demultiplexing regions (spanning 490–900 m/z in 10 m/z steps). It solves the matrix equation XA = Y, where X is a 41×41 design matrix encoding scan-to-demultiplexing-region inclusion (1 if included, 0 otherwise), Y is the observed intensity matrix (41×m, m = number of fragment ions of interest), and A is the unknown intensity matrix (41×m) constrained to non-negative values. Special-purpose heuristics enable block-wise approximate solving for computational efficiency (~1000 transitions from ~56,000 spectra in 10 min). The implementation supports arbitrary window widths and overlaps—not limited to 20 m/z width / 10 m/z overlap—and outputs demultiplexed spectra compatible with downstream analysis tools like Skyline.

software_configuration_description: ProteoWizard (specifically msConvert) was configured with three custom signal processing filters: CantWaiT (wavelet-based peak-picking), Turbocharger (precursor charge determination), and a Poisson model-based deisotoper. CantWaiT was applied to MS/MS spectra (though optionally usable on MS1) using a Ricker ('Mexican Hat') wavelet, adaptive wavelet scales dependent on m/z spacing (computed via 10-point sliding window), and limited to 10 scales; it performed single-pass local maxima detection with minimum m/z spacing of 0.1 Da (0.01 Da for Thermo Q-Exactive) and SNR thresholding (default SNR = 1.0, adjustable from 0.0 to >2.0); no preprocessing (smoothing/resampling) was required. Turbocharger used three scoring criteria—m/z spacing (SSE-based p-value vs. 10,000 simulated random chains), relative intensity distribution (Poisson model + KL divergence p-value vs. 10,000 simulated random peak sets from top 200 peaks across nine survey scans), and intensity rank-sum (p-value from fraction of possible rank-sums)—and searched charge states per dataset as specified in Table 1 (e.g., +2 to +14 for Agilent 6550 Human Serum); it analyzed two survey spectra per MS/MS spectrum (preceding for Thermo/Waters; one preceding + one following for AB SCIEX/Agilent), used isolation windows of ±1.00 m/z (AB SCIEX), ±1.25 m/z (Thermo), or ±1.50 m/z (others), and enforced a relative intensity p-value < 0.30 for final assignment, with fallback to default charges (+2 for Thermo, +2/+3 for Agilent/Waters, no charge for AB SCIEX) if no chain met criteria. Deisotoping used the same Poisson/KL approach but with 100 ppm m/z spacing tolerance (vs. fixed tolerance in Turbocharger) and charge range 1–3 by default; chains were removed if SSE and KL scores fell below length-dependent cutoffs (listed in Table S1). Subscan summation was applied to Waters data using msConvert’s built-in algorithm (summing intensities at identical m/z across MS/MS spectra sharing precursor m/z within ±0.05 m/z and retention time within 30 s). All parameters and command-line usage examples were documented in Supporting Information Tables S2–S6.
