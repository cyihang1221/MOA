<p id="Par7">Xiaoyao San (XYS) has been proven effective in treating depression, but its underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. This study aims to explore the key phytochemicals and mechanisms of the anti-depressant effects of XYS. We focus on the 16 blood compounds of XYS identified through the UPLC-MS/MS method. We also validated the results of the network pharmacology analysis using an lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced depression mouse model and the BV2 microglial cell line. In silico prediction, atractylenolide <em>I</em>, ferulic acid, kaempferol, quercetin, and vanillic acid exhibit good oral bioavailability, safety, and ADMET properties. The results of GSEA and GO enrichment analysis showed that neuroinflammation is the key mechanism of the anti-depressant effect of XYS. Kaempferol, quercetin, atractylenolide <em>II</em>, z-ligustilide, paeoniflorin, saikosaponin A, atractylenolide <em>III</em> and glycyrrhizic acid (GA) were identified as the main phytochemicals targeting neuroinflammation. XYS gavage treatment significantly ameliorated LPS-induced depressive-like behaviors in mice by reducing neuroinflammation (microglial activation) in the prefrontal cortex. The reduction of LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels by GA was also validated in BV2 cells, and its mechanism is associated with JNK pathway. The phytochemicals of XYS exhibit favorable safety and pharmacological parameters, with targeting neuroinflammation being the key mechanism underlying its anti-depressant effects.</p>

<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23296-1.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Xiaoyao san, Depression, Network pharmacology, Neuroinflammation, Phytochemicals, Lipopolysaccharide</p></section><section id="kwd-group2" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Subject terms:</strong> Diseases, Drug discovery, Neuroscience, Plant sciences</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par45">Depression, also known as major depressive disorder (MDD), characterized by significant and persistent feelings of sadness along with cognitive impairments, has emerged as a major contributor to the rising global suicide rate<sup><a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a></sup>. In China, depression is increasingly characterized by concealment and an earlier onset, leading to long-term negative impacts on families and society at large. Therefore, it is crucial to prevent and control both the occurrence and progression of depression<sup><a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a></sup>. The pathogenesis of depression remains inconclusive, primarily involving neurotransmitters, neuroimmune inflammatory responses, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and neuroplasticity<sup><a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a></sup>. The first-line treatment drugs for depression include selective 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin/norepinephrine (NE) reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and NE/specific 5-HT anti-depressants<sup><a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a></sup>. SSRIs include paroxetine, sertraline, and escitalopram, while SNRIs includeduloxetine and venlafaxine, among others. However, the overall efficacy rate of SSRIs is less than 60%<sup><a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a></sup>, and with the widespread and long-term use of these medications, some patients do not respond to SSRIs<sup><a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a></sup>. Anti-depressant medications are ineffective for approximately 40% of patients with depression<sup><a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a></sup>. Currently, clinical anti-depressant medications exhibit several drawbacks, including a slow onset of action, significant adverse effects, high relapse rates, and a propensity for the development of drug resistance<sup><a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>,<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a></sup>. The treatment of depression through traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has a long-standing history and offers unique advantages<sup><a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a></sup>. It is imperative to analyze the key phytochemicals and potential mechanisms of TCM compounds in treating depression, as this holds significant clinical importance.</p>
<p id="Par46">TCM compounds designed to soothing the liver and relieving depression hold therapeutic potential for treating depression<sup><a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a></sup>. Xiaoyao San (XYS) originates from the <em>Taiping Huimin Heji Jufang</em> of the Song Dynasty and is one of the classic prescriptions for soothing the liver and relieving depression. It consists of six Chinese herbs, namely <em>Radix Bupleuri</em>, <em>Radix AngelicaeSinensis</em>, <em>Radix Paeoniae Alba</em>, <em>Poria</em>, <em>Rhizoma Atractylodis Macrocephalae</em> and <em>Radix Glycyrrhizae Preparata</em>. Among them, <em>Radix Bupleuri</em> serves as the monarch drug of XYS, whereas <em>Radix AngelicaeSinensis</em> and <em>Radix Paeoniae Alba</em> function as the minister drugs. Substances with both medicinal and edible properties are deeply rooted in TCM cultures and exert significant health benefits<sup><a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a></sup>. The four TCM herbs in XYS are substances that serve both medicinal and edible substances, named <em>Radix AngelicaeSinensis</em>, <em>Poria</em>, <em>Rhizoma Atractylodis Macrocephalae</em> and <em>Radix Glycyrrhizae Preparata</em>. This suggests that XYS possesses good safety and potential for drug development. A recent systematic review and network meta-analysis indicated that XYS ranks third among TCM compounds in terms of reducing scores on the Hamilton Depression Scale, while also exhibiting the lowest incidence of adverse reactions<sup><a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a></sup>. Clinically, XYS has been used long-term for the treatment of depression accompanied by liver <em>qi</em> stagnation and spleen deficiency syndrome, demonstrating excellent efficacy and safety<sup><a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a></sup>. XYS has shown potent anti-depressant effects in various rodent models of depression, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS)<sup><a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a></sup>, chronic restraint stress (CRS)<sup><a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a></sup>, chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS)<sup><a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>,<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a></sup>, and chronic immobilization stress<sup><a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>,<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a></sup>. However, further investigation is needed to elucidate the pharmacodynamic basis, safety, and specific molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-depressant properties of XYS.</p>
<p id="Par47">Network pharmacology has facilitated the transition of TCM from an empirical medicine to an evidence-based approach, thereby enhancing our understanding of its therapeutic effects<sup><a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>,<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a></sup>. Microglia, the primary resident immune cells in the brain, mediate neuroinflammation that is closely associated with the onset of depression<sup><a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a></sup>. The substantial production of pro-inflammatory factors following microglia activation ultimately results in neuronal degeneration and necrosis. LPS is the most commonly used potent activator of microglia both in vitro and in vivo. The LPS-induced neuroinflammation model in mice has been reported and confirmed<sup><a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>,<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a></sup>. Regardless of whether LPS is administered peripherally or intracerebrally, it can induce depressive-like behaviors in experimental animals<sup><a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>–<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a></sup>. Unlike previous studies, we focus on the neuroinflammatory mechanisms of XYS in anti-depressant effects, identifying the phytochemicals in XYS that regulate neuroinflammation. We established a mouse model of depression by intraperitoneal injection of LPS, and an in vitro cell model of neuroinflammation was created by treating BV2 cells with LPS. This study integrates network pharmacology, gene expression profiles of patients with depression, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and both in vivo and in vitro experiments to explore the phytochemicals and potential mechanisms of XYS in targeting neuroinflammation. This study provides new insights into the anti-depressant mechanism of XYS and offers a series of lead compounds targeting neuroinflammation for the development of anti-depressant drugs.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">The main phytochemicals of XYS and their safety evaluation</h3>
<p id="Par48">This study focuses on 16 representative phytochemicals in XYS <strong>(</strong>Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a><strong>)</strong><sup><a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>,<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a></sup>. We identified 16 XYS phytochemicals listed in Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a> using a non-targeted metabolomics of TCM. The 16 main phytochemicals found in XYS interact with a total of 227 targets, which have been identified through reference mining, database mining, and structure-based prediction. XYS phytochemicals target multiple human organ systems, including the cardiovascular and nervous systems (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>A). For the cell type-specific enrichment analysis, the potential targets of XYS are primarily localized to macrophage, basal cell, epithelial cell, endothelial cell and glial cell (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>B). We employed ADMETlab 3.0<sup><a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a></sup> to calculate the physicochemical properties pertinent to the Lipinski, Pfizer, and GSK rules. Among these phytochemicals, atractylenolide <em>III</em>, ferulic acid, kaempferol, quercetin and vanillic acid fully comply with the aforementioned criteria, indicating that these components possess favorable oral bioavailability, safety, and absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) profile (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>C). According to the rule that acute toxicity greater than level 3 and fewer than 3 positive toxicity endpoints indicate non-toxic<sup><a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a></sup>, all phytochemicals in XYS except atractylenolide <em>II</em> are non-toxic (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>D). Atractylenolide <em>I</em> has the highest safety, with an LD50 of 5105 mg/kg, followed by isoliquiritin and paeoniflorin (LD50 = 4000 mg/kg). To evaluate the safety of the XYS, Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining was performed on the liver after 2 weeks of XYS treatment (1342.9 mg/kg/day). The results showed no significant pathological changes in the liver, indicating that XYS has no hepatotoxicity and good biosafety (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>E). Moreover, we also tested the levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in the serum, and the results showed that there was no significant difference between XYS and CON group (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>F, G).</p>

<p id="Par51">The MDD dataset <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE98793" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE98793</a> comprises gene expression data derived from whole blood samples collected from donors diagnosed with MDD and healthy controls. By utilizing gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), we identified that the signaling pathways related to neuroinflammation are closely associated with depression. GSEA indicated that the genes identified in <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE98793" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE98793</a> were significantly enriched in the inflammatory response (Normalized enrichment score, NES = 2.07, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.001), innate immune response (NES = 1.83, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.001), and cytokine production (NES = 1.69, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.001) <strong>(</strong>Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>A-C). <p id="Par66">GA (Synonyms: glycyrrhizin) derived from XYS demonstrated a significant inhibitory effect on neuroinflammation in various models of brain injury<sup><a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">40</a>,<a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">41</a></sup>. Among the phytochemicals in XYS, GA has the highest target ratio (the proportion of targets involved in neuroinflammation) and the best molecular docking scores with PTGS2 (−10.09 kcal/mol) and TNF-α (−7.05 kcal/mol) (Figs. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>H and <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>). We established an in vitro cell model of neuroinflammation by treating BV2 cells with LPS (1 µg/ml) for 24 h<sup><a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">42</a>,<a href="#CR43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR43">43</a></sup>. After treatment of GA for 6 h, result showed that the IC50 value of GA was 44.35 µM based on Cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) assay (Fig. <a href="#Fig8" class="usa-link">8</a>A). Based on the result of CCK8, we chose 20 µM as the appropriate treatment concentration for GA. Western blotting analyses revealed that the enhancement of TNF-α, IL6, 1L1β and Cox2 expression by LPS treatment was significantly attenuated by GA (Fig. <a href="#Fig8" class="usa-link">8</a>B-F). A total of 185 DEGs (161 up-regulated and 24 down-regulated) were obtained with the criteria of fold change &gt; 2and <em>p</em>-adjusted &lt; 0.05 between LPS treated BV2 cells and control. According to the PPI network of DEGs, TNF, IL6, and ILB are the top 3 hub genes ranked by degree, while PTGS2 is ranked 24th by degree. This suggests the critical role of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL6, 1L1β and Cox2 in LPS-treated BV2 cells. The JNK signaling pathway is intricately linked to the LPS-triggered inflammatory cascade in microglial<sup><a href="#CR44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR44">44</a>,<a href="#CR45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR45">45</a></sup>. Subsequently, we investigated whether the anti-inflammatory effects of GA were associated with the JNK pathway. Western blotting demonstrating that GA treatment significantly inhibited the LPS-induced activation of JNK in BV2 cells (Fig. <a href="#Fig8" class="usa-link">8</a>G, H).</p>

<p id="Par68">This study investigated the anti-depressant effects of XYS and its phytochemicals using network pharmacology, molecular docking, MD simulations, an LPS-induced depressive mouse model, and the BV2 microglial cell line. The results demonstrated that the primary mechanism of action for the anti-depressant effects of XYS targets neuroinflammation. In the phytochemicals of XYS, kaempferol, quercetin, atractylenolide <em>II</em>, z-ligustilide, paeoniflorin, saikosaponin A, atractylenolide <em>III</em>, GA, and liquiritin were identified as the main phytochemicals targeting neuroinflammation. This study enhances the understanding of the pharmacological basis and mechanism of action of XYS in the treatment of depression, while also providing a theoretical foundation for the clinical application of XYS and the development of anti-depressant drugs.</p>
<p id="Par69">The rapidly developing field of network pharmacology is facing a series of problems and challenges<sup><a href="#CR46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR46">46</a></sup>. The common pitfalls of network pharmacology were discussed in our previous studies<sup><a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>,<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>,<a href="#CR47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR47">47</a></sup>. A distinctive feature of this study is the implementation of multi-dimensional validation. We employed molecular docking to validate the optimal binding mode of direct interactions between XYS phytochemicals and their corresponding core targets, and further confirmed the binding stability through MD simulations. In addition, we also validated the anti-depressant effects of XYS at the animal level and the protective effects of GA against LPS-induced inflammatory responses in BV2 cells, along with its underlying mechanisms. This study focuses on the 16 blood compounds of XYS identified in plasma through the UPLC-MS/MS method<sup><a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>,<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a></sup>, rather than those predicted from public databases such as TCMSP. These 16 phytochemicals are considered to represent the pharmacokinetic characteristics of XYS, as determined through serum pharmacochemistry research and literature review. However, other phytochemicals in XYS with low bioavailability or undetectable levels may also play a role in the anti-depressant effects of XYS. This remains to be further studied in subsequent research. To advance the modernization of TCM, we have also conducted a systematic review of the pharmacological parameters and safety of the phytochemicals of XYS. The preclinical research of XYS has issues in the extraction methodology. Different researchers have adopted varying extraction times, frequencies, and solvents used, which affects both the composition and content of the XYS extracts. One of the advantages of this article is that XYS consists of commercially available Chinese herbal medicine prescription granules. Chinese herbal medicine prescription granules, processed using modern techniques, ensure the stability of medicinal ingredients and therapeutic efficacy.</p>
<p id="Par70">There are various modeling methods for animal models of depression, such as stress-induced modeling, drug-induced modeling, and surgical modeling. Neuroinflammation is closely associated with the pathogenesis of depression, and anti-inflammatory drugs may be an effective anti-depressant therapy<sup><a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>,<a href="#CR48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR48">48</a></sup>. Clinically, the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α have been utilized as biomarkers for depression<sup><a href="#CR49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR49">49</a>,<a href="#CR50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR50">50</a></sup>. Microglial activation is one of the characteristics of neuroinflammation. Microglia are significantly activated in the brains of depressive suicide victims<sup><a href="#CR51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR51">51</a></sup>. Activated microglia can release pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL1β, IL6, and TNF-α) and damage neurons. Knockout of IL1β in the hippocampus alleviated LPS-induced depressive-like behaviors in mice<sup><a href="#CR52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR52">52</a></sup>. In the expression profiling microarray of MDD patients (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE98793" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE98793</a> dataset), GSEA also revealed significant enrichment of multiple neuroinflammation-related signaling pathways, such as inflammatory response, innate immune response, and cytokine production (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>A-C). Therefore, we opted to construct a depression mouse model using the effective inflammatory response activator LPS<sup><a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>,<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a>,<a href="#CR53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR53">53</a>,<a href="#CR54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR54">54</a></sup>, rather than employing methods such as CRS or CUMS. The peripheral or intracerebral administration of LPS has been used to induce neuroinflammation in animals, and both peripheral and central administration of LPS can induce depressive-like behaviors in animals<sup><a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>–<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a></sup>. In the mouse brain following intracerebral LPS challenge, microglia and astrocytes were significantly activated and notably enriched in various BPs of neuroinflammation (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>). Our results also demonstrated that peripheral administration of LPS significantly activated microglia in the PFC, while XYS treatment alleviated the activation of microglia and LPS-induced depressive-like behaviors. As shown in the results of Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>, the behavioral outcomes of EPM, MBT, FST, and SPT support our conclusion. The reduction of LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels by GA was also validated in BV2 cells.</p>
<p id="Par71">The neuronal loss in specific brain regions is one of the critical pathological bases of depression. The PFC plays a significant role in neuropsychiatric processes such as depression and anxiety disorders. The PFC has become one of the most severely affected regions in MDD, and as the patient’s symptoms gradually worsen, the PFC undergoes atrophy<sup><a href="#CR55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR55">55</a></sup>. Moreover, postmortem studies of depressed patients have revealed reductions in neuronal volume, synaptic atrophy, and a decrease in the number of synapses in the PFC<sup><a href="#CR56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR56">56</a></sup>. In the PFC and hippocampus of animals exposed to chronic stress, neuronal atrophy and loss were observed, which correlated with a reduction in the volume of these brain regions<sup><a href="#CR57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR57">57</a></sup>. A study has shown that after drug-induced impairment of the morphology and function of pyramidal neurons in the PFC, mice exhibited depression- and anxiety-like behaviors<sup><a href="#CR58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR58">58</a></sup>. Recent multiple studies have shown that alleviating neuroinflammation in the PFC improves depressive-like behaviors in mice<sup><a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>,<a href="#CR59" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR59">59</a>,<a href="#CR60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR60">60</a></sup>. The knockdown of colony stimulating factor 1 in PFC prevents CUMS-induced depressive-like behaviors, with the mechanism involving the interaction between microglia and neurons<sup><a href="#CR61" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR61">61</a></sup>. The activation of microglia and astrocytes in the PFC mediates the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, ultimately resulting in neuronal loss and facilitating the onset of depression<sup><a href="#CR62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR62">62</a></sup>. This study focuses on the neuroinflammation in the central functional brain region PFC to explore the key phytochemicals of XYS for its anti-depressant effects. Similar to our results, XYS treatment was effective in controlling microglial activation and pro-inflammatory responses in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and hippocampus, thereby ameliorating obesity-associated anxiety and depressive-like behaviors<sup><a href="#CR63" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR63">63</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par72">This study identified a series of potential neuroinflammatory modulators, including kaempferol, quercetin, atractylenolide <em>II</em>, z-ligustilide, paeoniflorin, saikosaponin A, atractylenolide <em>III</em> and GA. GA is the active triterpenoid saponin component found in <em>Radix Glycyrrhizae Preparata</em>, a substance that serves both medicinal and edible substances. Molecular docking, MD simulations, and in vitro experiments all indicate that GA interacts with multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL6, 1L1β and Cox2. In CUMS-induced depressive mice, GA ameliorates depressive-like behaviors by inhibiting HMGB1 through modulation of the kynurenine pathway<sup><a href="#CR64" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR64">64</a></sup>. Another study indicates that GA treatment ameliorates CRS-induced anxiety-like behaviors through GLT1 and Per1/2-dependent pathways<sup><a href="#CR65" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR65">65</a></sup>. Preclinical studies have shown that GA possesses safety, economical, and anti-inflammatory properties, making it a potential adjunctive treatment for depression. Clinical studies have shown that the reduction of TNF-α mediates the association between GA treatment and clinical improvement, and it is beneficial for symptom improvement only in patients with high inflammation (baseline CRP &gt; 3 mg/L)<sup><a href="#CR66" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR66">66</a></sup>. A recent study has demonstrated that GA mitigates neuroinflammatory damage caused by necrotizing enterocolitis by inhibiting the HMGB1/TLR4 pathway, protecting the integrity of the blood-brain barrier and reducing pyroptosis in BV2 cells<sup><a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">41</a></sup>. The relationship between TCM compounds and their individual phytochemicals is not a simple superposition or substitution, but rather a synergistic or complementary system formed based on the complexity of disease mechanisms, the multi-target characteristics of drug actions, and clinical needs. Subsequent research needs to validate these through rigorous controlled experiments.</p>
<p id="Par73">Intracerebral injection of LPS induces depressive-like behaviors accompanied by activation of the JNK pathway and increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, while inhibition of JNK alleviates depressive-like behaviors and reduces the levels of TNF-α and IL1β<sup><a href="#CR67" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR67">67</a></sup>. The pathological responses characterized by reduced social exploratory behavior and increased immobility time, induced by the central injection of TNF-α in mice, can be effectively mitigated through prior lateral ventricular injection of the JNK inhibitor DJNKI-1<sup><a href="#CR68" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR68">68</a></sup>. This suggests that the JNK pathway may be a potential target for the treatment of inflammation-related depression. A study has shown that inhibiting the JNK pathway reduces the inflammatory cascade in LPS-activated BV2 cells<sup><a href="#CR45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR45">45</a></sup>. This study also found that the anti-inflammatory effect of GA is associated with the JNK pathway. However, the anti-depressant effects of other phytochemicals in XYS that have neuroinflammatory regulatory properties require further investigation.</p></section><section id="Sec12"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p id="Par74">Neuroinflammation is one of the key mechanisms through which XYS exerts its anti-depressant effects. In XYS, a variety of phytochemicals serve as potential neuroinflammatory modulators, including kaempferol, quercetin, atractylenolide <em>II</em>, z-ligustilide, paeoniflorin, saikosaponin A, atractylenolide <em>III</em>, GA, and liquiritin. The phytochemicals of XYS primarily regulate the core targets TP53, AKT1, TNF, IL6, BCL2, IL1B, ESR1, HSP90AA1, CASP3, MYC, JUN, PTGS2, PPARG, NFKB1, MTOR, CYCS, CCND1, GSK3B and IL10, modulating neuroinflammation to mediate the treatment of depression. The findings of this study hold significant implications for the clinical application of XYS and the development of anti-depressant drugs targeting neuroinflammation.</p></section><section id="Sec13"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Drugs and antibodies</h3>
<p id="Par75">XYS was obtained from the Affiliated Hospital of Gansu University of Chinese Medicine, and the Chinese herbal medicine prescription granules it contains were acquired from Guangdong Yifang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. LPS (≥ 500000 EU/mg, # BS904-10 mg) was from Biosharp (Beijing, China). Glycyrrhizic acid (purity = 99.84%, CAS #: 1405-86-3) was procured from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China) and dissolved in DMSO. Toluidine blue (CAS #: 6586-04-5) was purchased from Macklin (Macklin, China). The primary antibodies specific for Iba1 (# 10904-1-AP), β-actin (# 66009-1-lg), cyclooxygenase 2 (Cox2, # 27308-1-AP), IL-1β (# 26048-1-AP), IL6 (# 26404-1-AP) and TNF-α (# 29652-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). JNK (# AF6318) and p-JNK (Thr183 + Tyr185, # AF3318) were purchased from Affinity Biosciences (Jiangsu, China). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled goat anti-rabbit/mouse (# A21020/A21010) IgG was used as secondary antibody and purchased from Abbkine (Wuhan, China).</p></section><section id="Sec15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Collect the main phytochemicals of XYS and their potential targets</h3>
<p id="Par76">This study focuses on the 16 blood compounds of XYS in plasma identified through the UPLC-MS/MS method<sup><a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>,<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a></sup>. Each compound’s SMILES was downloaded from the PubChem database. The potential targets of XYS phytochemicals were collected from the HERB database (<a href="http://herb.ac.cn/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://herb.ac.cn/</a>)<sup><a href="#CR69" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR69">69</a></sup> and the Swisstargetprediction server (<a href="http://swisstargetprediction.ch/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://swisstargetprediction.ch/</a>)<sup><a href="#CR70" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR70">70</a></sup>. It should be noted that XYS phytochemicals are preferentially retrieved from the HERB database, while those phytochemicals such as atractylenolide <em>I</em>, atractylenolide <em>II</em>, atractylenolide <em>III</em> and Dl-3-n-butylphthalide that cannot be retrieved from the HERB database are predicted through the Swisstargetprediction server. We further utilized WebCSEA (<a href="https://bioinfo.uth.edu/webcsea/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://bioinfo.uth.edu/webcsea/</a>)<sup><a href="#CR71" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR71">71</a></sup> to classify the potential targets of XYS by organ systems and cell types.</p></section><section id="Sec16"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Evaluate the Pharmacological and toxicological parameters of the phytochemicals in XYS</h3>
<p id="Par77">Compounds that comply with Lipinski rule exhibit better oral bioavailability, those that adhere to Pfizer rule are generally non-toxic, and compounds conforming to GSK rule may possess more favorable ADMET profile. The aforementioned three rules of XYS phytochemicals were evaluated using the ADMETlab 3.0 platform (<a href="https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/</a>)<sup><a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a></sup>. We calculated the toxicological parameters of XYS phytochemicals using the Protox II webserver (<a href="https://tox-new.charite.de/protox_II/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://tox-new.charite.de/protox_II/</a>)<sup><a href="#CR72" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR72">72</a></sup>. According to the toxicity rules<sup><a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>,<a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a></sup>, if the acute toxicity is greater than grade 3 and the number of positive toxicity endpoints is fewer than 3, it indicates non-toxicity.</p></section><section id="Sec17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">GSEA</h3>
<p id="Par78">Human brain tissue samples are extremely scarce and are generally obtained from post-mortem autopsy samples and invasive biopsy samples. Compared to brain tissue samples, the collection of peripheral blood is a minimally invasive procedure with no significant health risks to the patient. The <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE98793" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE98793</a> dataset<sup><a href="#CR73" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR73">73</a></sup>, comprising whole blood samples from 64 healthy controls and 128 samples from individuals with MDD, was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Depression is not merely a central nervous system disorder, but a systemic disease characterized by the dysregulation of the central-peripheral system interaction. The changes in the peripheral blood transcriptome are not isolated events, but rather exhibit clear molecular associations with central pathological mechanisms. GSEA was performed to identify MDD-related gene enrichment using the ClusterProfiler R package (version 3.12.0), with a significance threshold set at <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05. For the GSEA results, both the NES and <em>p</em> value were reported.</p></section><section id="Sec18"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Analysis of gene expression profiles in the mouse brain following intracerebral LPS challenge</h3>
<p id="Par79">The gene expression dataset <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE122815" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE122815</a><sup><a href="#CR74" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR74">74</a></sup> (specie: <em>Mus musculus</em>) was downloaded from the GEO database for analysis. These data were obtained from the <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GPL11180" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GPL11180</a> platform (Affymetrix HT MG-430 PM Array Plate), which includes hemisphere brain samples after 4 days of intrastriatal injection of LPS or PBS (<em>n</em> = 3, three biological replicate samples were combined to form a single sample). DEGs were identified using the BioConductor Limma package with cut-offs of adjusted <em>p</em>-value &lt; 0.05 and fold change &gt; 2. We further analyzed the brain cell localization of DEGs using the “Single Cell Expression” module of the AlzData database (<a href="http://www.alzdata.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.alzdata.org/</a>)<sup><a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a></sup>, including neurons, astrocytes, microglia, endothelial cells, and oligodendrocytes. We have paid special attention to the DEGs specifically localized in astrocytes and microglia. We further utilized Venny 2.1 (<a href="https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html</a>) to identify the common genes between XYS and DEGs.</p></section><section id="Sec19"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">GO enrichment analysis for biological process (BP)</h3>
<p id="Par80">GO enrichment analysis for BP was obtained using the ClusterProfiler R package<sup><a href="#CR75" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR75">75</a></sup>, with a corrected <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05 as the threshold for significance. The ratio of the number of genes to the total number of genes annotated in this term is referred to as the rich factor. We have primarily focused on the BPs related to neuroinflammation or apoptotic signaling pathway. Bubble plot presentation of BP enrichment analysis was generated using a web-based tool (<a href="http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.bioinformatics.com.cn</a>).</p></section><section id="Sec20"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Construction of PPI network and screening of core targets</h3>
<p id="Par81">The PPI networks of XYS targets were built using STRING version 12.0 (<a href="https://cn.string-db.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://cn.string-db.org/</a>) with a confidence score threshold of 0.4, and visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.3. The analysis was limited to the organism <em>Homo sapiens</em>. The PPI network comprises nodes, which represent a target protein, and edges, which represent protein-protein interactions. The thickness of the edges represents the combined score. Degree represents the number of other nodes directly connected to a node. A higher degree value indicates greater significance of the node within the network. The Cytoscape plugin, Network Analysis plugin, was employed to determine the degree values<sup><a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a></sup>. In this study, the top 20 target proteins were selected and identified as core targets based on their degree rankings. We further utilized Venny 2.1 to identify the core targets involved in multiple neuroinflammatory BPs.</p></section><section id="Sec21"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Molecular Docking simulations and MD simulations</h3>
<p id="Par82">The molecular docking procedure LeDock (<a href="http://www.lephar.com/software.htm" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.lephar.com/software.htm</a>)<sup><a href="#CR76" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR76">76</a></sup> was used for the automated molecular docking simulations. Briefly, the three-dimensional structures of target proteins were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB database, <a href="http://www.rcsb.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.rcsb.org/</a>)<sup><a href="#CR77" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR77">77</a></sup>, while the three-dimensional SDF format files of XYS phytochemicals were retrieved from the PubChem database. The LePro tool was utilized to process receptor files, including hydrogenation and the acquisition of the binding pocket. The docking score (in kcal/mol) was calculated using the default scoring function. The interaction residues and hydrogen bond lengths between the ligand and receptor were visualized using LigPlot (<a href="https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/software/LigPlus/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/software/LigPlus/</a>)<sup><a href="#CR78" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR78">78</a></sup>. Generally speaking, docking scores &lt; −7 kcal/mol have strong binding affinity, and − 4 &lt; docking scores ≤ −7 kcal/mol have moderate interactions<sup><a href="#CR79" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR79">79</a>,<a href="#CR80" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR80">80</a></sup>. Ultimately, the optimal docking conformation, characterized by the lowest docking score, was selected for subsequent MD simulation analysis. The MD simulations were carried out with the GROMACS 2024.1 package. The ligand parameters were obtained from the General Amber Force Field (GAFF2), and the charges were derived using the AM1-BCC method. AMBER ff14sb_OL15 protein force field together with the TIP3P water model was used. The production MD simulations were run at 300 K for 100 ns with a time step of 2 fs under constant volume and temperature. The stability of the complex structure was evaluated using the RMSD.</p></section><section id="Sec22"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Animals and treatments</h3>
<p id="Par83">The 8-week-old male C57/BL/6J mice (22–24 g, SPF grade, SCXK(Su)2020-0009, <em>n</em> = 43) were purchased from Huachuang Sino Pharmaceutical Technology Company (Jiangsu, China), and the animal certification number was B202407030457. All mice were housed and maintained in the Medicine Animal Center of University of South China (the use license number is SYXK(Xiang)2020-0002), and the experimental procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of University of South China (No. 2023-36).</p>
<p id="Par84">The XYS, which is derived from the Chinese herbal medicine prescription granules, were freshly dissolved in boiling water and stirred thoroughly before application. The recommended dosage of XYS for adult human in clinical applications is 147.57 mg/kg/day. Based on the mouse equivalent dose being 9.1 times that of humans<sup><a href="#CR81" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR81">81</a>,<a href="#CR82" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR82">82</a></sup>, the gavage dose of XYS is 1342.9 mg/kg/day. Mice were first randomly divided into two groups, with the XYS group (<em>n</em> = 23, 1342.9 mg/kg/day) receiving XYS via oral gavage and the CON group (<em>n</em> = 20) receiving an equivalent volume of water, for a duration of two weeks. The LPS-induced mouse model is a typical inflammatory animal model of depression<sup><a href="#CR53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR53">53</a></sup>. During the last 3 days of the gavage treatment, LPS (0.83 mg/kg/day)<sup><a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>,<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a>,<a href="#CR54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR54">54</a></sup> or an equivalent volume of normal saline was intraperitoneally injected while continuing the gavage. Starting from day 12, the mice were divided into four groups: CON group (<em>n</em> = 10), XYS group (<em>n</em> = 12), LPS group (<em>n</em> = 10), and XYS + LPS group (<em>n</em> = 11). Twenty-four hours after the last LPS injection, all mice underwent depression-related behavioral tests. Detailed experimental procedures provided in Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>A. Mice were sacrificed to collect brain and liver samples for further analysis. Isoflurane was used for mouse anesthesia, and Pentobarbital was used for mouse euthanasia.</p></section><section id="Sec23"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Behavioral tests</h3>
<section id="Sec24"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">OFT</h4>
<p id="Par85">All mice were individually tested in an open-field apparatus consisting of a white square base (45 × 45 cm) with white walls (45 cm in height). A single mouse was carefully placed in the center of the chamber, and all spontaneous activities were recorded for 5 min using a video-computerized tracking system. Immediately following each experiment, the boxes were sanitized with 75% alcohol. The total distance traveled in the OFT was used as an index of locomotor activity and depression/anxiety-like behavior.</p></section></section><section id="Sec25"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">EPM</h3>
<p id="Par86">The EPM experiment was conducted using a plus-maze apparatus that comprised two open arms, two closed arms, and a central platform. Both the open and closed arms measured 50 × 10 cm, with the closed arms featuring baffles on either side. Mice were placed individually in the central area, 50 cm above the ground, and their movements were recorded for 5 min. Following each experiment, the enclosures were promptly cleaned using 75% alcohol. Locomotion was monitored and tracked through an automated system. The durations spent in the closed arms, open arms, and central platform were documented.</p>
<section id="Sec26"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">MBT</h4>
<p id="Par87">We assessed anxiety-related behaviors in mice through MBT. To conduct the test, prepare a cage with dimensions of 40 × 25 × 20 cm, which should be lined with 5 cm of corn cob bedding. Arrange 20 glass marbles, each with a diameter of 2.5 cm, in a “4 × 5” configuration within the cage. Introduce a single mouse into the cage and record the number of marbles buried by the mouse within a 10 min observation period. A marble is identified buried when at least two-thirds of its surface is covered by the corn cob bedding.</p></section><section id="Sec27"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">FST</h4>
<p id="Par88">We used the immobility time in the FST to assess the despair behavior of the animals. The mice were placed individually in a Plexiglas cylinder (50 cm in height × 30 cm in diameter) filled with 30 cm water (25 °C). Immobility was defined as no movement at all or only minor movements necessary to keep the nose above the water versus mobility, which was defined by swimming and struggling behaviors. Each test session lasted for 6 min, with the final 4 min scored for immobility.</p></section><section id="Sec28"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Sucrose preference test (SPT)</h4>
<p id="Par89">As a classic behavioral experiment for assessing anhedonia, SPT comprises two phases: training and testing. During the training phase, without altering the normal diet of the mice, an additional 1.5% sucrose solution was provided at irregular intervals 2–3 days before the SPT. In the testing phase, the mice were deprived of water and food for 24 h, and then both sugar water and normal drinking water were offered, but no food was provided. The consumption of sugar water over 24 h was recorded. Sucrose preference was calculated using the following formula: sucrose preference (%) = sucrose consumption/(water consumption + sucrose consumption) × 100%.</p></section><section id="Sec29"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Measurement of liver function</h4>
<p id="Par90">Serum was obtained from the blood samples by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min and stored at 80 °C. Serum AST (# E-BC-K236-M) and ALT (# E-BC-K235-M) levels were tested by using corresponding assay kits from Elabscience Biotech (Wuhan, China). The detection was performed strictly according to the kit instructions.</p></section><section id="Sec30"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">HE staining</h4>
<p id="Par91">We employed HE staining to assess histopathological damage in liver tissues. The liver tissues from both the control group and the XYS group were excised post-surgery and subsequently rinsed with pre-cooled PBS. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China) for 24 h, routine paraffin embedding and section were performed. Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining Kit (Biosharp, China) was utilized for HE staining. All morphological images in this study were taken using an Olympus optical microscope (Olympus BX60, Tokyo, Japan).</p></section><section id="Sec31"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">IHC staining and Nissl staining</h4>
<p id="Par92">Microglia in the PFC were visualized using Iba1 (# 10904-1-AP, 1:200) IHC staining. The IHC staining procedure was conducted in accordance with our previous studies<sup><a href="#CR83" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR83">83</a>,<a href="#CR84" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR84">84</a></sup>. The IHC universal type two-step detection kit (PV-9000) and diaminobenzidine (DAB) coloration (ZLI-9018) were obtained from Zhongshan Jinqiao (Beijing, China). Nissl staining of a 30 μm coronal section with toluidine blue solution (0.1% toluidine blue in 0.1% sodium carbonate, pH = 11.1) was used. All images were captured by an Olympus optical microscope (Olympus BX60, Tokyo, Japan). Microglia in a healthy brain are in a relatively round body, longer, thinner and resting state (resting cells). In the states initially activated after brain injury, they rapidly proliferate, cell bodies enlarge and processes retract or become thicker. Therefore, this study selected the perimeter and area of the cell bodies to reflect the activation of microglia<sup><a href="#CR85" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR85">85</a></sup>. The analysis of the perimeter and area of microglial cell bodies, along with the quantification of Nissl-stained positive neurons in the PFC, was conducted using ImageJ software (<a href="https://imagej.net/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://imagej.net/</a>).</p></section><section id="Sec32"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Western blotting</h4>
<p id="Par93">The protein concentrations of the PFC tissues or cell samples were measured using a bicinchoninic acid kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Western blotting was conducted as previously described<sup><a href="#CR83" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR83">83</a>,<a href="#CR84" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR84">84</a></sup>. The PVDF membranes (0.45 μm) were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Signals were visualized with an enhanced chemiluminescence imaging system (SCG-W2000, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). ImageJ software was used to analyze the results of the Western blotting.</p></section><section id="Sec33"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Cell culture and CCK8 assay</h4>
<p id="Par94">The immortalized mouse BV2 microglial cell line, exhibiting morphological and molecular characteristics similar to primary microglial, was purchased from the Boster Biological Technology (product number: XBS0421-0017, Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin at 37 ℃ a humidified incubator under a 95%/5% (v/v) mixture of air and CO<sub>2</sub>. We established an in vitro cell model of neuroinflammation by treating BV2 cells with LPS (1 µg/ml) for 24 h<sup><a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">42</a>,<a href="#CR43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR43">43</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par95">Cell viability was assessed indirectly using a CCK-8 assay. BV2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a suitable density. GA is a phytochemical found in <em>Radix Glycyrrhizae Praeparata</em>, classified by the FDA as Generally Recognized As Safe as a food additive. Various concentrations of GA (0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 µM) were applied to treat cells for 24 h. After 24 h exposure, the medium was replaced with 100 µL of fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent (# C0038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 60 min at 37 °C. Optical densities of CCK8 were measured by a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA) at 450 nm.</p></section><section id="Sec34"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h4>
<p id="Par96">All data were expressed as the means ± standard error of mean (SEM). The differences between groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA analysis and Tukey’s multiple comparison using SPSS 19.0 statistical software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Data visualization was performed using Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). The significance level was defined as <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05.</p></section></section></section><section id="Sec35"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h2>
