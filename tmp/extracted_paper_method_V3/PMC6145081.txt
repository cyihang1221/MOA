</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ABS1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="P1">One objective in developing adverse outcome pathways (AOPs) is to connect biological changes that are relevant to risk assessors (i.e., fecundity) to molecular and cellular-level alterations that might be detectable at earlier stages of a chemical exposure. Here, we examined biochemical responses of fathead minnows (Pimephales promelas) to inform an AOP relevant to spironolactone’s activation of the androgen receptor, as well as explore other biological impacts possibly unrelated to this receptor. Liquid chromatography with high resolution mass spectrometry (LC-MS) was used to measure changes in endogenous polar metabolites in livers of male and female fish that were exposed to five water concentrations of spironolactone (0, 0.05, 0.5, 5, or 50μgL<sup>−1</sup>) for 21days. Metabolite profiles were affected at the two highest concentrations (5 and 50μgL<sup>−1</sup>), but not in the lower-level exposures, which agreed with earlier reported results of reduced female fecundity and plasma vitellogenin (VTG) levels. We then applied partial least squares regression to assess whether metabolite alterations covaried with changes in fecundity, VTG gene expression and protein concentrations, and plasma 17β-estradiol and testosterone concentrations. Metabolite profiles significantly covaried with all measured endpoints in females, but only with plasma testosterone in males. Fecundity reductions occurred in parallel with changes in metabolites important in osmoregulation (e.g., betaine), membrane transport (e.g., l-carnitine), and biosynthesis of carnitine (e.g., methionine) and VTG (e.g., glutamate). Based on a network analysis program (i.e., mummichog), spironolactone also affected amino acid, tryptophan, and fatty acid metabolism. Thus, by identifying possible key events related to changes in biochemical pathways, this approach built upon an established AOP describing spironolactone’s androgenic properties and highlighted broader implications potentially unrelated to androgen receptor activation, which could form a basis for the development of an AOP network.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Fathead minnow, Metabolomics, Mineralocorticoid, Reproduction</p></section></section><section id="S1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="P2">To improve understanding and prediction of contaminant toxicity, the adverse outcome pathway (AOP) concept was developed to identify a series of key events that relate a molecular initiating event (e.g., receptor activation) elicited by a chemical exposure to adverse outcomes relevant to risk assessors (<a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">Ankley et al., 2010</a>; <a href="#R53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R53">Villeneuve et al., 2014</a>). This can improve the efficiency of risk assessment programs, for example, by predicting adverse outcomes of a contaminant based on its ability to trigger molecular and biochemical changes linked to a given molecular initiating event in an established AOP. Although this approach can be effective for predicting toxic outcomes in many cases, some limitations exist. For instance, AOPs are inherently pathway specific and cannot predict toxic effects due to molecular perturbations that are outside the key events that define it (<a href="#R53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R53">Villeneuve et al., 2014</a>). Thus, when a contaminant acts via several toxicological pathways, consideration needs to be given to the potential involvement of multiple AOPs that may be integrated into larger AOP networks and help to better understand interactions among the various pathway perturbations (<a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">Knapen et al., 2015</a>). However, the full list of applicable AOPs may not be known <em>a priori.</em> Further analyses (e.g., data-mining and case-studies) may be required to identify those molecular initiating events and AOPs that might co-occur with exposure to a particular chemical (<a href="#R53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R53">Villeneuve et al., 2014</a>).</p>
<p id="P3">Metabolomics represents an effective approach for not only identifying the presence of additional AOPs that may be occurring in parallel with an already assigned AOP, but also for providing physiological support and expanding knowledge of those AOPs relevant to a particular exposure. For example, decreased vitellogenin (VTG) synthesis is a biomarker of endocrine disruption and a key event in several AOPs, including those relating reproductive dysfunction to androgen or estrogen receptor interactions (AOP:23 and 30, respectively; <a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">Adverse Outcome Pathway Wiki, 2016a</a>, <a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">Adverse Outcome Pathway Wiki, 2016b</a>). Compounds that affect VTG synthesis have also been shown, via metabolomics, to elicit changes in endogenous metabolites important to synthesis of this lipoprotein (e.g., glutamate; <a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">Ekman et al., 2011</a>). Thus, metabolomics has the potential to inform and provide additional physiological support for AOPs that incorporate this particular key event, including the possible identification of previously undefined key events. Indeed, metabolomics can measure changes in endogenous metabolites involved in a wide variety of biochemical pathways, and associate those changes with exposure to specific chemicals (e.g., <a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">Bundy et al., 2007</a>, <a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">Davis et al., 2016</a>) and also to adverse biological outcomes (e.g., reduced earthworm fecundity; <a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">Åslund et al., 2013</a>, <a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">Guo et al., 2009</a>). Thus, examination of those pathways perturbed by a chemical exposure may be useful for identifying a range of impacts and any potential receptor interactions. This may subsequently point toward additional molecular initiating events and previously unsuspected AOPs that may be relevant to a particular chemical and may help forecast toxicity of contaminants acting via numerous pathways.</p>
<p id="P4">To demonstrate how metabolomics can enhance understanding of a contaminant’s toxicological effects and potentially inform AOP development for those contaminants that might perturb multiple toxicological pathways, we measured endogenous metabolite responses to spironolactone exposure. Spironolactone is a widely prescribed pharmaceutical that interacts with several nuclear steroidal receptors (<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">Creusot et al., 2014</a>, <a href="#R36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R36">Macikova et al., 2014</a>) and has been detected downstream of wastewater treatment plants, particularly those receiving influent from hospitals and pharmaceutical manufacturers (<a href="#R36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R36">Macikova et al., 2014</a>). Spironolactone is a known mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist in humans and is a frequently prescribed diuretic (<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">Doggrell and Brown, 2001</a>). Other evidence indicates that it may have implications for aquatic biota, as it can interact with mineralocorticoid receptors in fish (<a href="#R49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R49">Sturm et al., 2005</a>). In addition, spironolactone is an antagonist of androgen receptors in humans (<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">Doggrell and Brown, 2001</a>); however, results from fish-based studies have shown that spironolactone can act as an androgen receptor agonist in fish and result in anal fin elongation and decreased hepatic VTG mRNA in females (<a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">Howell et al., 1994</a>, <a href="#R42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R42">Raut et al., 2011</a>). Spironolactone exposure also leads to modifications of progesterone and glucocorticoid receptor signaling in human cell lines(<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">Creusot et al., 2014</a>), which may be due to its rapid conversion to secondary metabolites(i.e., 7α-thiomethylspironolactone and canrenone) that interact with multiple receptors (<a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35">Los et al., 1994</a>).</p>
<p id="P5">The broad array of evidence demonstrating spironolactone’s potential to interact with multiple receptors suggests that several AOPs may be needed to better understand its impacts on fish. In fact, those impacts have begun to be elucidated by the recent application of the androgen receptor activation AOP (<a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">Adverse Outcome Pathway Wiki, 2016a</a>). Specifically, <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al. (2013)</a> showed that female fathead minnows (<em>Pimephales promelas</em>) exposed for 21-days exhibited declines in fecundity, and plasma VTG and steroid concentrations consistent with what would be expected for a chemical operating through this particular AOP. Furthermore, biochemical and fecundity results for spironolactone were similar to those observed for the model androgen receptor agonist 17β-trenbolone (i.e., <a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">Ankley et al., 2003</a>). Although <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al. (2013)</a> provided convincing evidence of spironolactone’s androgenic properties, as a result of their focus on key events associated with the androgen receptor activation AOP, possible impacts on other pathways and potential need for additional AOPs were not assessed. Metabolomics offers an approach for identifying these other impacts of spironolactone, and, more generally, may help to identify AOPs relevant for predicting biological effects of other contaminants that act on various receptors (e.g., progesterone, mineralocorticoid, and glucocorticoid receptors) and can reach biologically-relevant concentrations in aquatic ecosystems (e.g., <a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">Creusot et al., 2014</a>, <a href="#R44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R44">Runnalls et al., 2010</a>).</p>
<p id="P6">Here, we applied a metabolomics-based approach to identify changes in endogenous metabolites in fathead minnows from the previous study by <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al. (2013)</a>, helping to further develop and assess the androgen receptor AOP. Building on results of that analysis, we first confirmed that spironolactone exposure altered profiles of endogenous metabolites in fathead minnow livers. Then, we related those alterations to changes in biological endpoints (i.e., fecundity, VTG mRNA in liver tissue, and concentrations of VTG, 17β-estradiol [E2], and testosterone in plasma) that were sensitive to spironolactone exposure and linked to an AOP based on androgen receptor activation. Finally, we employed a metabolite network-analysis approach based on untargeted metabolite changes to investigate additional altered biochemical pathways that may reflect the involvement of additional AOPs initiated by spironolactone exposure, but potentially unrelated to androgen receptor activity.</p></section><section id="S2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Experimental design</h3>
<p id="P7">To assess endogenous metabolite responses to spironolactone exposure and to link those changes to reproduction related endpoints (e.g., fecundity and VTG production), we conducted a 21-day study that followed established protocols (<a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">Ankley et al., 2001</a>). As reported by <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al. (2013)</a>, two pairs of sexually-mature male and female fathead minnows were placed in a 20-L aquarium that contained 10 L of Lake Superior water (UV-treated and filtered) and was continuously renewed in a flow-through system. Aquaria were divided by a porous, plastic screen, with each side containing a single breeding pair and spawning substrate. After a 14-day acclimation period, fish that had successfully reproduced were allocated to one of five treatment groups, four exposed to different concentrations of spironolactone (0.05, 0.5, 5, or 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup>) and one to control water (Lake Superior). Five aquaria were used for each concentration, yielding 10 pairs of fish per treatment. Concentrations of spironolactone in test aquaria were verified using liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC–MS; <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al., 2013</a>). Throughout the study, fish were maintained at 25 ± 1 °C and under a 16:8 h light:dark photoperiod, and fed twice daily with frozen brine shrimp. Fecundity of each breeding pair (i.e., numbers of spawned eggs and fertile eggs produced) was monitored daily and summed for the entire 21-day exposure. Additional details on water quality measurements, analytical techniques for measuring chemical concentrations in aquaria water, and experimental design were reported by <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al. (2013)</a>.</p>
<p id="P8">At the end of the 21-day exposure, fish were anesthetized in tricaine methane-sulfonate(MS-222; 100 mg L<sup>−1</sup> buffered with 200 mg L<sup>−1</sup> NaHCO<sub>3</sub>), weighed, and dissected. Blood (∼20–50 μL) was collected from the caudal vasculature, centrifuged, and stored at −80 °C until analyzed for testosterone, E2, and VTG concentrations using established methods (see <a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29">Jensen et al., 2001</a>, <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al., 2013</a> for details). After blood collection, the liver was dissected and a sample from females, but not males, was allocated for RNA extraction. <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al. (2013)</a> used quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction to quantify relative abundances of mRNA transcripts for VTG protein in livers. For analysis of endogenous metabolites, a liver sample was collected from both males and females, transferred to a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube containing a stainless steel ball bearing (3 mm), flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until extracted.</p></section><section id="S4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Processing of metabolomics samples</h3>
<p id="P9">To profile changes in relative abundances of endogenous metabolites, liver samples were extracted in a 96-well plate format using established protocols for a dual phase extraction (see <a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">Davis et al., 2013</a> for additional details). A 440 μL aliquot of the polar extract was subsequently collected from each sample. An additional aliquot (ca. 75–100 μL) was then collected from the remaining polar extract for each sample, pooled, and split into multiple quality control (QC) samples for high resolution LC–MS/MS analysis. The QC samples and polar extracts were vacuum dried overnight and stored at −80 °C. Prior to analysis, polar extracts and QC samples were reconstituted in 100 μL of methanol:water (50:50), after which the samples were allowed to sit for 10 min and then vortexed at 500 RPM for 10 min. Samples were allowed to sit again for 10 min and then vortexed a final time for 10 min (500 RPM). To remove particulates, the reconstituted samples were transferred to a prewetted 96-well PVDF filter plate (0.45 μm pore size) and centrifuged at 1000 × <em>g</em> for 5 min (20 °C). The filtrate was transferred to a 96-well plate with 300 μL glass inserts and briefly centrifuged to remove air bubbles (1 min at 1000 × <em>g</em>). Samples were stored in a refrigerated tray (4 °C) during the LC–MS/MS analysis. Quality control samples were analyzed at the beginning, end, and after every tenth sample of the analytical run.</p></section><section id="S5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Acquisition parameters for mass spectrometry</h3>
<p id="P10">Relative intensities of endogenous metabolite features were first assessed using high-resolution LC–MS (full-scan mode), with a follow-up tandem LC–MS/MS fragmentation analysis to aid in metabolite annotation. These analyses were performed using an Accela 1250 UHPLC system with an Open Accela Autosampler (Thermo Fisher Scientific), coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) via a heated electrospray ionization source (HESI-II) operating in positive (+ESI) and negative (-ESI) modes. A Syncronis aQ C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm × 1.7 μm; Thermo Fisher) was maintained at 50 °C and operated at a flow rate of 500 μL min<sup>−1</sup>. A 3 μL sample injection was used with an elution gradient consisting of ultrapure water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B), each containing 0.1% formic acid. The gradient started at 100% solvent A (0 min), and declined to 80% solvent A by 5 min, 40% solvent A by 6.5 min, and 0% solvent A by 9.5 min. Prior to the injection of the next sample, the column was rinsed for 4 min at 100% solvent B and then re-equilibrated for 5 min at 100% solvent A. The following parameters were used during the mass spectrometry analyses: probe heater temperature, 350 °C; sheath gas, 50; auxiliary gas, 10; sweep gas, 2.38; capillary temperature, 320 °C; S-lens RF level, 35; and spray voltage, 3.5 kV. The <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> scan range was set to 60–850 and the resolution was 70,000. The automatic gain control target was set at 1 × 10<sup>6</sup> and maximum injection time was 120 ms. For the data-dependent MS–MS (Top 5), the resolution was set at 17,500 and the HCD normalized collision energy was set at 40% with a stepped normalized collision energy of 50%. The automatic gain control target was set at 1 × 10<sup>5</sup> and maximum injection time was 60 ms.</p></section><section id="S6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. Data processing</h3>
<p id="P11">All raw data were processed using SIEVE software (v2.1, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The small molecule component extraction signal detection algorithm was used for peak detection and integration. For a detected peak to be included in the final dataset, it had to exhibit a percent coefficient of variation (CV) of &lt;30% in the QC samples and had to be present in ≥80% of samples in at least one exposure group. Prior to multivariate and univariate analyses, we identified and removed duplicate records in datasets collected using the positive and negative electrospray ionization modes. All remaining metabolite features were normalized to unit total integrated intensity for each ion mode individually. Then, the data from the positive and negative ionization modes were combined and divided by 2 to renormalize to unit total integrated intensity.</p>
<p id="P12">Detected peaks of interest were putatively annotated (level 2 metabolite identification according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (<a href="#R50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R50">Sumner et al., 2007</a>)) by searching the Human Metabolome Database (<a href="http://www.hmdb.ca/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.hmdb.ca</a>) and LipidMaps (<a href="http://www.lipidmaps.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.lipidmaps.org</a>) using accurate mass of the peak with a mass error tolerance of 3 ppm. When possible, metabolite identifications were further assigned by matching MS/MS spectra with those recorded in the public database Metlin (<a href="http://www.metlin.scripps.edu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.metlin.scripps.edu</a>) and MZCloud (<a href="http://www.mzcloud.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.mzcloud.org</a>), or by matching MS/MS spectra to <em>in silico</em> MS/MS spectra that were generated for possible candidates.</p></section><section id="S7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Statistical analyses</h3>
<p id="P13">After data processing, 1177 peaks that met QC criteria were detected in livers from male and female fish. We expect these peaks represented endogenous metabolites because they were detected in all treatment groups, including controls. To determine whether any change in endogenous metabolite profiles attributed to spironolactone exposure may be partly due to technical variability, we first used principal component analysis (PCA; SIMCA v 13.0, Umetrics Inc.) to compare the variability in metabolite profiles from fish and the variability of the pooled QC samples. After verifying that technical variation was negligible (see Results; <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Fig. S1</a>), subsequent PCA models excluded QC samples and assessed whether exposure altered overall profiles of polar metabolites in fish. Males and females were analyzed separately, metabolite data were unit-variance scaled and mean-centered, and statistical outliers were identified based on preliminary PCA comparisons and a Hotelling’s <em>T</em><sup><em>2</em></sup> test (95% confidence limit). To assess whether average PCA score values differed among exposure groups, a separate one-way ANOVA with posthoc Tukey’s test (R v3.2.2, R Core Team) were run for first and second component scores.</p>
<p id="P14">To evaluate how changes in endogenous metabolite profiles covaried with endpoints related to reproductive function in these fish, we used partial least squares (PLS) regression (SIMCA v 13.0, Umetrics Inc.). This framework can simultaneously compare multiple X and multiple Y variables where the number of variables exceeds the number of replicates or when the variables possess collinearity (<a href="#R56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R56">Wold et al., 2001</a>). Specifically, PLS-regression was used to evaluate whether changes in female metabolites significantly covaried with changes in fecundity, VTG mRNA in liver tissue, or concentrations of VTG, E2, and testosterone in plasma. For males, we only compared changes to plasma VTG, E2, and testosterone. Metabolite data were unit variance-scaled and mean-centered, and reproduction related endpoints were unit variance-scaled, mean-centered, and log<sub>10</sub>(x + 1) transformed. All PLS models were validated using cross-validation, whereby the full dataset was divided into seven randomly distributed subsets and one of the seven data subsets was left out to build a PLS model (i.e., leave-one-out cross validation). To evaluate whether the model predicted those variables better than random chance, we then statistically compared the respective residuals of the measured values of that subset to those values predicted based on the PLS model (CV-ANOVA &lt;0.05; <a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">Eriksson et al., 2008</a>). The best-fitting model was then identified by repeating this cross-validation in an iterative fashion based on subsequently generated data subsets (1000 permutations). Their predictive ability was assessed using cumulative Q<sup>2</sup>(Q<sup>2</sup>cum; <a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">Eriksson et al., 2006</a>), which assesses how well the model predicts bootstrapped data while also controlling for model overfit. To further assess the fit of the validated PLS models, we then used the best-fitting model to predict biological endpoint values for each fish used in the cross-validation, which were statistically compared to the measured values using simple linear regression (r<sup>2</sup><sub>adj</sub>: adjusted r<sup>2</sup>).</p>
<p id="P15">In addition to assessing how overall metabolite profiles were affected, we determined which specific metabolites significantly changed among treatment groups using two separate analyses. First, to assess whether the relative abundance of a detected feature differed across exposure groups, we applied a one-way ANOVA and post-hoc pairwise <em>t</em>-tests (R v3.2.2) to each metabolite feature. A false-discovery rate (FDR) correction (i.e., q-value) was also applied (<a href="#R48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R48">Strimmer, 2008</a>). Second, we identified metabolites that significantly covaried with fecundity based on the PLS-regression model. We focused on fecundity because it is an apical measurement relevant to risk assessment that integrates changes in other measured endpoints. For this, we utilized variable importance in the projection (VIP) values, which indicates the relative importance of an X-variable (i.e., metabolite) for the Y-variable (i.e., fecundity) in a PLS-regression model (<a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">Eriksson et al., 2006</a>). Features with VIP values ≥ 1.0 are considered important and explain a substantial proportion of the variability in fecundity. Because fecundity significantly declined at higher concentrations of spironolactone, we indirectly assessed the direction and magnitude of the relationship between fecundity and biologically-relevant metabolite features (i.e., VIP ≥ 1.0) by examining the one-way ANOVA results and percent change calculations for each metabolite feature.</p>
<p id="P16">In order to provide a broader understanding of spironolactone effects, we also explored how the exposures may have altered biochemical pathways other than those directly related to reproductive function by using a network analysis approach (i.e., <em>mummichog</em>, v1.0.5; <a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34">Li et al., 2013</a>). This software can help elucidate those pathways significantly impacted in a treatment group relative to a control group (<a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">Hoffman et al., 2014</a>, <a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34">Li et al., 2013</a>). Significantly impacted biochemical pathways are those exhibiting an adjusted p-value &lt;0.05. For this comparison, we focused on endogenous metabolite features that significantly changed (pairwise <em>t</em>-test q-value &lt;0.05) in females exposed to 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> of spironolactone, as it would represent the greatest potential impact of the chemical exposure. To meet assumptions of the network approach, this comparison was conducted separately for positive and negative ionization modes. Detailed descriptions of the overall network approach can be found elsewhere (<a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34">Li et al., 2013</a>).</p></section></section><section id="S8"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results</h2>
<section id="S9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Summary of previously reported biological responses</h3>
<p id="P17">Results of the current study, which focused on the endogenous metabolite responses to spironolactone exposure, build upon previously published work that assessed the response of endpoints related to reproduction measured in these same fish (<a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al., 2013</a>). As reported by <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al. (2013)</a>, females exposed to the two highest concentrations (5 and 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup>) exhibited significant declines in plasma VTG concentrations and VTG mRNA expression in liver tissue compared to controls. These endpoints were not significantly different from controls in the two lowest exposure concentrations (0.05 and 0.5 μg L<sup>−1</sup>). Similarly, fecundity was significantly lower in females exposed to 5 and 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> of spironolactone, but no change was detected for the other exposure groups. Levels of plasma E2 and testosterone in females significantly declined at a spironolactone concentration of 0.5 and 5 μg L<sup>−1</sup>, and were lowest in females exposed to 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> of spironolactone. In males, plasma E2 and VTG concentrations were not significantly affected by spironolactone exposure at any concentration; however, plasma testosterone declined at the highest test concentration (50 μg L<sup>−1</sup>). Thus, females exposed to spironolactone exhibited reductions in several measures of reproductive endocrine function, largely at the two highest concentrations; however, exposure of males reduced only plasma testosterone levels, and only in the highest treatment group (50 μg L<sup>−1</sup>) (<a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al., 2013</a>).</p></section><section id="S10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Comparing metabolite profiles across exposure groups</h3>
<p id="P18">To assess whether overall metabolite profiles of the fathead minnows were significantly impacted by exposure to the five concentrations of spironolactone (0, 0.05, 0.5, 5, and 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup>), responses were initially assessed with PCA and subsequent one-way ANOVA. Preliminary PCA comparisons and a Hotelling’s <em>T</em><sup><em>2</em></sup> test (95% confidence limit) detected a single statistical outlier (1 male from the control group), which was removed from all subsequent analyses. Furthermore, technical variation was found to be nominal as results from a separate preliminary PCA showed that variability in the pooled QC samples was substantially lower than variability measured in either female or male fish (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Fig. S1A</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">B</a>). Based on the final PCA comparisons that excluded pooled QC samples, metabolite profiles of females significantly differed along the second (ANOVA: <em>F</em><sub><em>4,49</em></sub> = 50.41, p-value &lt;0.001), but not the first component (ANOVA: <em>F</em><sub><em>4,49</em></sub> = 0.886, p-value = 0.48) of the PCA model (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1A</a>). Pairwise comparisons indicated that differences along the second component were largely due to metabolite profiles of females from the 5 and 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> exposure groups being statistically different from each other, and from the other groups (0, 0.05, and 0.5 μg L<sup>−1</sup>; Tukey’s test, p &lt; 0.05). For males, metabolite profiles were also significantly different along the second component of the PCA model (ANOVA: <em>F</em><sub><em>4,48</em></sub> = 17.88, p-value &lt;0.001), such that males from the 5 and 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> exposure groups were statistically different from each other and from other exposure groups (Tukey’s test, p &lt; 0.05; <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1B</a>). Males did not significantly differ along the first component (ANOVA: <em>F</em><sub><em>4,48</em></sub> = 0.523, p-value = 0.719). Thus, spironolactone exposure altered overall metabolite profiles of males and females, but only for the two highest concentrations, as metabolite profiles measured in individuals from the two lower concentrations were similar to the control group.</p>

For instance, exposure to spironolactone significantly altered relative abundances of 289 features from females (ANOVA q-value &lt;0.05). Subsequent pairwise comparisons indicate that this was largely due to impacts at the 5 and 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> concentrations (137 and 245 significant features, respectively; pairwise <em>t</em>-test, q &lt; 0.05), as no metabolite feature from females exposed to the two lowest concentrations was significantly different from the controls (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>). Thus, spironolactone exposure elicited significant changes in a number of metabolite features; however, those impacts were only at the two highest concentrations of the chemical and were more prevalent in females than males.</p></section><section id="S11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3. Impacted biochemical pathways in females</h3>
<p id="P20">In addition to identifying changes in the relative intensities of individual metabolites, we applied a network analysis approach (i.e., <em>mummichog</em>) to identify biochemical pathways in females that were significantly altered by the 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> exposure. Based on permutation testing that mapped significantly changing metabolite features in females (pairwise <em>t</em>-test q-value &lt;0.05) onto known biochemical pathways, we found that 33 pathways were significantly impacted by spironolactone exposure when comparing features detected using the positive ionization mode. Forty-one pathways were impacted when comparing features from the negative ionization mode (<a href="#T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>, <em>mummichog</em> adjusted p-value &lt;0.05). However, there was considerable overlap in these lists, as 25 pathways were identified as significantly impacted in both comparisons. For those metabolites detected in positive ionization mode, the five biochemical pathways with the lowest adjusted p-value were tryptophan metabolism (<em>mummichog</em> adjusted p-value = 0.0007), glycine, serine, alanine, and threonine metabolism (0.0007), xenobiotic metabolism (0.0007), arginine and proline metabolism (0.0008), and the urea cycle (0.0008). When comparing features detected in negative ionization mode, several pathways had equally low adjusted p-values. These eight pathways were glycosphingolipidmetabolism (adjusted p-value = 0.0003), purine metabolism (0.0003), sialic acid metabolism (0.0004), glycosphingolipid biosynthesis (0.0004), urea cycle (0.0004), methionine and cysteine metabolism (0.0004), glycero-phospholipid metabolism (0.0004), and nitrogen metabolism (0.0004; <a href="#T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). Thus, the application of this network approach helped to elucidate a variety of biochemical pathways in females that were significantly altered by exposure to 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> of spironolactone.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/T1/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="S12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.4. Comparing metabolite profiles to endpoints related to reproductive function</h3>
<p id="P21">Based on the PLS regression, changes in metabolite profiles significantly covaried with changes in several reproductive endpoints (plasma E2, testosterone, VTG, VTG mRNA, and fecundity). The strength of those relationships varied between sexes and across endpoints. For instance, metabolite changes in males significantly covaried with changes in plasma testosterone (Q<sup>2</sup>cum = 0.277; CV-ANOVA &lt; 0.05), but only exhibited a weak linear relationship between measured levels and those predicted based on the cross-validated PLS-regression model (p-value &lt;0.001, r<sub>adj</sub><sup>2</sup> = 0.284; <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>; <a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>). The PLS-regression models examining covariance of plasma VTG or E2 in males with changes in their metabolite profile did not validate (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>). In contrast to males, changes in all five reproductive endpoints in females were significantly related to metabolite profile changes (CV-ANOVA &lt;0.001; <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>), with the strength of those relationships, as measured by Q<sup>2</sup>cum, ranging from 0.374 to 0.726 (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Figs. 3A–D</a> and <a href="#F4" class="usa-link">4</a>; <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>). The model relating metabolites and plasma VTG in females exhibited the strongest relationship (Q<sup>2</sup>cum = 0.726; <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>), and showed significant agreement between predicted and measured values of plasma VTG (p-value &lt;0.001; r<sub>adj</sub><sup>2</sup> = 0.477, <a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3B</a>). The PLS-regression relating female fecundity and metabolite changes also exhibited a relatively strong relationship (Q<sup>2</sup>cum, = 0.547; <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>) with a significant linear relationship between predicted and measured values of fecundity (p-value &lt;0.001; r<sub>adj</sub><sup>2</sup> = 0.471; <a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>). Although the model relating metabolites and VTG mRNA exhibited comparable strength as models describing fecundity and plasma VTG (Q<sup>2</sup>cum = 0.654), it poorly predicted VTG mRNA levels (p-value &lt;0.001; r<sub>adj</sub><sup>2</sup> = 0.272), partly due to overestimation in the control group (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3A</a>). Lastly, models describing plasma E2 and testosterone exhibited a weaker relationship with metabolite changes (Q<sup>2</sup>cum = 0.495 and 0.374, respectively; <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>), but their ability to predict steroid levels was comparable to other models (p-value &lt;0.001; r<sub>adj</sub><sup>2</sup> = 0.468 and 0.353, respectively; <a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig.3C–D</a>). The combination of such evidence suggests that changes in metabolite profiles were significantly covarying with changes in a variety of reproductive endpoints comprising the androgen receptor activation AOP in females, including fecundity and plasma VTG (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>, <a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>). The strength of those relationships varied and was generally weaker in males (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>).</p>

<p id="P22">We also used results of the PLS-regression analyses to discern those hepatic metabolites that significantly covaried with fecundity changes at the individual fish level. Specifically, we selected the 420 metabolite features that had VIP values ≥ 1.0, but were only able to putatively annotate 41 of these features, partly due to limitations of publicly available fragmentation libraries (<a href="#T2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). For instance, several metabolites associated with carnitine biosynthesis and fatty acid oxidationwere significantly related to fecundity changes (<span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine VIP: 1.73, acetylcarnitine: 1.63, propionylcarnitine: 1.37, (±) hexanoylcarnitine: 1.27, 2-methylbutyrylcarnitine: 1.10, and methionine: 1.04; <a href="#T2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). This relationship was accompanied by concomitant changes in the abundances of these metabolites at the highest concentrations of spironolactone, although the overall pattern of those alterations differed among metabolites (<a href="#T2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a> and <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5A–F</a>). Relative to controls, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine and propionylcarnitine significantly decreased at both the 5 and 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> concentrations (pairwise <em>t</em>-test, q &lt; 0.05; <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5A and D</a>). Although (±) hexanoylcarnitine was marginally lower at the 5 μg L<sup>−1</sup> level (pairwise <em>t</em>-test, q &lt; 0.10), it was significantly lower only at the 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> concentration (q &lt; 0.05; <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5B</a>). In contrast, acetylcarnitine significantly decreased at 5 μg L<sup>−1</sup> (pairwise <em>t</em>-test, q &lt; 0.05), but was only marginally lower at the 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> concentration (q &lt; 0.10; <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5C</a>). The abundance of 2-methylbutyrylcarnitine was marginally lower at the highest concentration (pairwise <em>t</em>-test, q &lt; 0.10; <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5E</a>), whereas methionine increased with exposure concentration (q &lt; 0.05; <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5F</a>). Despite some variability in the overall responses, results indicate that relative intensities of these particular metabolites were significantly covarying with female fecundity at the individual fish level, and were generally lower in the groups of females exhibiting decreased fecundity at higher levels of spironolactone exposure.</p>

Discussion</h2>
<section id="S15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.1. Androgen receptor AOP informed by linking metabolite and reproductive endpoints</h3>
<p id="P25">In this study, we identified effects of a 21-day spironolactone exposure on polar metabolite profiles in fathead minnow livers and linked those changes to declines in fecundity and other reproductive-related endpoints that were previously reported in a companion study (i.e., <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al., 2013</a>). This builds upon earlier evidence of the androgenic properties of spironolactone in fish (i.e., <a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">Howell et al., 1994</a>, <a href="#R42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R42">Raut et al., 2011</a>), as metabolite changes in this study correlated with reproductive endpoints measured in females (VTG mRNA expression, plasma VTG and steroids, and fecundity), whereas changes in endogenous metabolites only weakly correlated with changes in plasma testosterone levels in males. This stronger relationship in females is possibly related to the direct functional role of the liver in the biosynthesis of VTG (e.g., <a href="#R39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R39">Parks et al., 1999</a>), which is important in female reproduction. This direct relationship is less apparent in males, which may attenuate the strength of the relationship between hepatic metabolite changes and alterations of other reproduction-related endpoints measured in this study. Also, in agreement with earlier measurements of reproductive endpoints (i.e., <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al., 2013</a>), metabolite profiles (as analyzed by PCA) and relative intensities of individual metabolites (as analyzed by ANOVA) were significantly altered at 5 and 50 μg L<sup>−1</sup> of exposure, but not at the lowest concentrations. However, metabolite and reproductive endpoint responses did diverge for males exposed to 5 μg L<sup>−1</sup> of spironolactone, whereby metabolite profiles were affected despite the lack of a similar response in male reproductive endpoints measured by <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al. (2013)</a>. This inconsistency suggests that changes in endogenous metabolites could be indicative of impacts not directly related to androgen receptor signaling and reproductive function. Nonetheless, we found metabolite responses that were consistent with exposure to an androgen receptor agonist.</p>
<p id="P26">In addition to strengthening the evidence of spironolactone’s androgenic properties, we provided insight into the mechanistic basis of relevant key events and linkages within the androgen receptor AOP by relating metabolite changes to indicators of reproductive function. For example, reproductive endpoints that significantly covaried with metabolite changes comprise several key events in the androgen receptor AOP described for female fish (AOP 23; <a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">Adverse Outcome Pathway Wiki, 2016a</a>); thus, our results provide further confidence in the scientific robustness of this AOP. In particular, the relative abundance of glutamate in livers (the primary site of VTG synthesis; <a href="#R39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R39">Parks et al., 1999</a>) significantly decreased with exposure and covaried with fecundity declines. Glutamate is, in fact, an important constituent of VTG in fathead minnows (<a href="#R39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R39">Parks et al., 1999</a>). Thus, the significant relationship between hepatic glutamate and fecundity are likely related to concomitant changes in plasma VTG, and are consistent with a spironolactone-induced decline in VTG production (<a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al., 2013</a>). Indeed, VIP results from the PLS-regression comparing plasma VTG and liver metabolite changes found that hepatic glutamate significantly covaried with plasma VTG levels (VIP = 1.72; data not shown). Because this particular endogenous metabolite is an important constituent of VTG, there is a very direct biological link between these two measurements. On the other hand, there are a greater number of intermediate steps that occur between changes in VTG mRNA levels and endogenous metabolites, which may attenuate the strength of their relationship. This may be a contributing factor in the stronger relationship between changes in endogenous metabolites and plasma VTG versus changes in VTG mRNA levels.</p>
<p id="P27">Our approach also identified other endogenous metabolites of potential importance; for example, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine and related metabolites significantly covaried with fathead minnow fecundity. This agrees with earlier studies showing that reductions in <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine can be associated with hyperandrogenism in human females (<a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">Fenkci et al., 2008</a>) and reduced fecundity in other fish species (<a href="#R46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R46">Schreiber et al., 1997</a>; but see <a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">Dzikowski et al., 2001</a>). Furthermore, targeted <em>in vitro</em> inhibition of <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine biosynthesis significantly reduced oocyte maturation in bovine ovaries, likely due to changes in fatty acid oxidation (<a href="#R45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R45">Sanchez-Lazo et al., 2014</a>), while <em>in vitro</em><span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine supplementation improved rates of mouse oocyte maturation, fertilization, and blastocyst development (<a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">Dunning et al., 2011</a>). Thus, considerable biological evidence provides indirect support that our observed decline in hepatic <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine levels may be a contributing factor in decreased female fecundity, especially as <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine is primarily biosynthesized in the liver (<a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">Bøhmer, 1974</a>, <a href="#R52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R52">Vaz and Wanders, 2002</a>). Such results are largely correlative and any conclusion based upon them should be viewed accordingly. Results from other studies have suggested that changes in certain metabolites measured in this current study (e.g., glutamate and <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine) may be related to biological pathways unrelated to androgen receptor signaling (i.e., <a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">Karst et al., 2005</a>, <a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">Grube et al., 2006</a>). Nonetheless, the strong relationships observed among endogenous metabolites and biological endpoints helped elucidate specific metabolites that could be targeted by future studies. Those studies could more fully evaluate metabolite relationships described here and explore whether they should be considered for inclusion as key events in the androgen receptor activation AOP, or whether they are related to other biological pathway changes.</p>
<p id="P28">This study provides further evidence for the potential utility of metabolomics to identify possible biomarkers or key events through various stages of exposure, helping to inform and refine the development of this particular AOP. Despite this possibility, the proportion of detected peaks that were putatively annotated in this study, while comparable to other LC/MS-based metabolomics studies, also demonstrates that metabolite annotation continues to represent a significant challenge for LC–MS-based metabolomics studies (<a href="#R59" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R59">Zhou et al., 2012</a>). In particular, there is a considerable need to expand publically available MS/MSspectral libraries, as there are a large number of endogenous metabolites and few of them have been reliably identified in currently available libraries. Such limitations are largely due to the scarce resources available to develop these libraries. Nonetheless, focusing those resources on metabolite features that have been detected in model organisms or have been found to be differentially affected by chemical exposures may represent a cost-effective means of fully realizing the potential strength of metabolomics for informing AOP discovery and ecotoxicology in general.</p></section><section id="S16"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.2. Potential key events supported by similar responses to other androgen exposures</h3>
<p id="P29">Changes in the relative abundances of metabolites associated with spironolactone exposure largely agree with earlier studies that have assessed fish responses to other androgens. For example, similar to spironolactone, a 21-day exposure of fathead minnows to 17β-trenbolone significantly decreased plasma VTG (<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">Ankley et al., 2003</a>) and correspondingly decreased hepatic glutamate (<a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">Ekman et al., 2011</a>). Consistent with the present study, exposure to 17β-trenbolone also reduced hepatic lactate and glutamine. In addition to changes in metabolite profiles, 17β-trenbolone altered gene expression in fathead minnows (<a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">Dorts et al., 2009</a>), with many of the genes exhibiting functional overlap with changes in biochemical pathways that were observed in this present study with spironolactone. For example, a 4-d 17β-trenbolone exposure of fathead minnows down-regulated genes important for fatty acid and lipid metabolism, and also sphingolipid metabolism in female gonad tissue (<a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">Dorts et al., 2009</a>). Alteration of gene expression related to lipid metabolismand transport have also been reported for fish exposed to other types of androgens (11-ketotestosterone and methyl-testosterone; <a href="#R38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R38">Martyniuk and Denslow, 2012</a>). Such substantial agreement of transcriptomic and metabolomic changes in multiple studies using known androgens provides additional evidence that some of the metabolite alterations observed in the present study may be generalizable responses that could eventually serve as additional key events for diagnosing exposure to and effects of this class of chemicals.</p></section><section id="S17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.3. Metabolite alteration indicates additional potential impacts and AOPs</h3>
<p id="P30">When utilizing an AOP to characterize or predict possible effects of a given chemical, an important question is whether additional effects might be exerted through other AOPs. Such potential is supported by the results of the present study, as spironolactone exposure reduced the ability of fathead minnows to biosynthesize <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine. Specifically, we measured significant decreases in several forms of endogenous metabolites involved with fatty acid oxidation (i.e., <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine, (±) hexanoylcarnitine, acetylcarnitine, propionylcarnitine, and 2-methylbutyrylcarnitine). In addition, we detected significant increases in methionine, a crucial building block in <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine biosynthesis (Bremer, 1983), suggesting that the reduction in <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine biosynthesis may occur during later stages of biosynthesis. Due to the essential role of <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine in many processes, including fatty acid, lipid, and amino acid metabolism (<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">Bremer, 1983</a>), such declines may not only contribute to the reduced fecundity discussed above, but may encompass biological effects that extend beyond those measured by <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">LaLone et al. (2013)</a>. For example, spironolactone exposure may have implications for energy production because <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine deficiencies can increase the reliance of fish on protein metabolism to maintain metabolic rates (<a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">Harpaz, 2005</a>). In the context of other research, results from this current study suggest that spironolactone exposure could potentially interact with other environmental changes, as <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine can ameliorate the negative impacts of environmental stressors on fish, including thermal stress, ammonia toxicity, and oxidative stress. Specifically, cichlids (<em>Pelvicachromis pulcher</em>) fed diets supplemented with <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine exhibited reduced mortality in response to cold shock (<a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">Harpaz et al., 1999</a>). Because of its functional role as an osmolyte, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine also improved resistance to ammonia toxicity in Chinook salmon (<em>Oncorhyncus tshawytscha</em>) injected with ammonium acetate, whereby 33% of fish receiving <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine supplemented diets exhibited ammonia toxicity and only 4% died, in contrast to 98% and 69% of reference fish, respectively (<a href="#R51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R51">Tremblay and Bradley, 1992</a>). Due to its role in membrane transport, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine declines may also affect the susceptibility of exposed fish to other xenobiotics when spironolactone is present in complex chemical mixtures. Under thermal stress, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine supplementation of guppies (Poecilia <em>reticulata</em>) reduced the transport of an anionic xenobiotic (i.e., fluorescein) across membranes of epithelial and gill cells, subsequently reducing its accumulation in liver and kidney tissue (<a href="#R46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R46">Schreiber et al., 1997</a>). Furthermore, oxidative stress declined in fathead minnow cells that were pretreated with <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine and subsequently exposed to hydrogen peroxide (<a href="#R55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R55">Wang et al. In press</a>). Given the variety of physiological functions associated with <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine, this highlights the potential for additional adverse physiological outcomes as a result of these pathway alterations.</p>
<p id="P31">The potential for spironolactone exposure to elicit biological impacts beyond those associated with androgen receptor activation is also supported by other biochemical alterations reported here and their overall agreement with pharmacological effects. For example, results from previous studies have shown that spironolactone can bind to the glucocorticoid and progesterone receptors (<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">Garthwaite and McMahon, 2004</a>, <a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">Fagart et al., 2010</a>). Perturbations of mineralocorticoid receptor signaling may also represent a molecular initiating event through which spironolactone is acting, as it has been shown to be an antagonist of mineralocorticoid receptors in humans (<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">Doggrell and Brown, 2001</a>). In fact, it can display the highest affinity for the mineralocorticoid receptor (<a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43">Rogerson et al., 2004</a>) and alter signaling of this nuclear receptor in mammals at a lower concentration (MR IC50 &lt; AR IC50 &lt; PR EC50 &lt; GR IC50; <a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">Garthwaite and McMahon, 2004</a>, <a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">Fagart et al., 2010</a>). This affinity for the mineralocorticoid receptor has led to its use as a diuretic and to treat hypertension in humans due to the functional importance of the mineralocorticoid receptor for ion transfer and osmoregulation (<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">Doggrell and Brown, 2001</a>). There is also some evidence that spironolactone is an agonist of this receptor in teleost fish (<a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">Pippal et al., 2011</a>, <a href="#R49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R49">Sturm et al., 2005</a>). This points to its activation as a possible molecular initiating event related to spironolactone exposure, with other evidence suggesting possible physiological effects. Several endogenous metabolites (i.e., GABA, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine, and betaine) that were affected by spironolactone exposure are involved with fish osmoregulation (<a href="#R40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R40">Peluso et al., 2000</a>) and may be partly related to agonism of this steroidal receptor. This agrees with another study showing that dosing of killifish (<em>Fundulus heteroclitus</em>) with spironolactone inhibited the expression of genes important for ion transfer across gill membranes, and affected ion concentrations in plasma (<a href="#R47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R47">Scott et al., 2005</a>). In addition, other studies indicate spironolactone can reduce the prevalence of fatty livers in mice fed a diet high in fat and fructose and down-regulate genes involved with lipidogenesis and β-oxidation (<a href="#R54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R54">Wada et al., 2010</a>). In agreement with such findings, the network analysis from the present study indicated overall changes in fatty acid oxidation and β-oxidation in exposed fathead minnows. It also showed that tryptophan metabolism was significantly altered by spironolactone exposure, which aligns with evidence indicating that spironolactone may affect depression in mammals, possibly via changes in mineralocorticoid receptor activation and serotonin levels, a neurotransmitter derived from tryptophan (<a href="#R57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R57">Wu et al., 2013</a>, <a href="#R58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R58">Young et al., 2003</a>).</p></section><section id="S18"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.4. Predicting effects via alteration of multiple receptors requires an AOP network approach</h3>
<p id="P32">Results of our metabolomics-based approach and previous pharmacological studies contribute to the mounting evidence that the development of AOP networks may be required to effectively forecast net impacts of compounds that alter multiple molecular pathways. For instance, an AOP network approach (<em>sensu</em><a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">Knapen et al., 2015</a>) helped to predict overall changes in fathead minnow gluconeogenesis, immune system function, and androgen receptor activation due to several molecular initiating events related to beclomethasone dipropionate exposure (<a href="#R37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R37">Margiotta-Casaluci et al., 2016</a>). As evidence suggests that spironolactone similarly interacts with a range of nuclear receptors, an AOP network that integrates impacts via a number of molecular changes may similarly improve understanding of how aquatic biota respond to similar types of compounds. Such a possibility is supported by the range of endogenous metabolite changes reported here and the functional overlap of some of the receptors impacted by spironolactone, including the potential importance of mineralocorticoid and glucocorticoid receptors in gill permeability and osmoregulation (<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">Kelly and Chasiotis 2011</a>). Although spironolactone exposure may elicit interactive effects that may require an AOP network approach, the relative importance of these various pathway changes may be largely concentration-dependent. For instance, mineralocorticoid and androgen receptor signaling in mammals can be altered at a substantially lower concentration of spironolactone (as measured by IC50 or EC50) than changes in progesterone and glucocorticoid signaling (e.g., <a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">Garthwaite and McMahon, 2004</a>, <a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">Fagart et al., 2010</a>). Thus, exposure to lower concentrations of spironolactone may elicit effects primarily via alteration of mineralocorticoid and androgen receptors, with the effects related to progesterone and glucocorticoid receptors increasing in importance at higher exposure levels. These dose-dependent interactions suggest the potential complexity and need for AOP networks to integrate dynamic relationships when forecasting the overall effects of compounds that alter multiple molecular pathways.</p>
<p id="P33">Overall results from this current study highlight how the application of metabolomics within an AOP framework may help to elucidate other pathway alterations and associated effects that may be occurring in parallel with androgen receptor activation and could be explored in the future. Consideration of these additional alterations may provide a more complete understanding of the overall effects associated with contaminants that act upon multiple receptors, and could be targeted for inclusion in a broader AOP network focused on these nuclear receptors. The relative importance of the various molecular changes within such a network could be assessed by future studies that utilize the more recently developed compounds (e.g., eplerenone) that exhibit greater selectivity for the mineralocorticoid receptor and reduced side effects associated with androgen receptor binding (<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">Garthwaite and McMahon, 2004</a>, <a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">Fagart et al., 2010</a>). These and other types of studies (e.g., time-course experiments) may help to refine the AOPs focused on these particular nuclear receptors, partition the relative importance of these molecular pathways in a broader AOP network, and evaluate the utility of applying changes in those endogenous metabolites measured in this current study as indicators of exposure to similar classes of compounds. Understanding those relationships and how they interact with other factors may be particularly important in complex multi-stressor environments where co-occurring environmental stressors related to energy regulation, ammonia toxicity, and osmotic gradients could potentially exacerbate responses directly attributable to contaminant exposure.</p></section></section><section id="S19"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">5. Conclusions</h2>
<p id="P34">Results from this study provide an example of how high-resolution mass spectrometry and metabolomics can complement existing knowledge of an established AOP and help identify potential molecular indicators of a biological perturbation. For example, parallel analysis of reproductive endpoint measurements and endogenous metabolites in fathead minnow livers identified several metabolites (i.e., glutamate and <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine) that significantly covaried with fecundity and other endpoints that have been used as biomarkers of exposure for a variety of endocrine disrupting compounds (i.e., plasma VTG levels; <a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">Ankley et al., 2003</a>, <a href="#R39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R39">Parks et al., 1999</a>). This comparison also helped substantiate aspects of the physiological basis of the AOP centered on androgen receptor activation, as these endpoints have been previously recognized as key events that link this AOP’s molecular initiating event (i.e., activation of androgen receptor) to an adverse outcome (i.e., decreased fecundity). Furthermore, we identified alterations in other endogenous metabolites (i.e., glutamine and lactate) that have been similarly affected by known androgenic compounds (e.g., <a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">Ekman et al., 2011</a>), which supports the potential for applying pathway-based metabolomics to identify generalizable molecular changes that may help signal exposure to a range of androgenic compounds.</p>
<p id="P35">In addition to highlighting potential molecular changes that could be tied to the androgen receptor AOP, examination of biochemical pathways that were significantly affected by spironolactone helped generate testable hypotheses for additional biological impacts. Future studies or AOP development could target such effects to help determine if they could be more generally applied to other compounds similar to spironolactone, and be linked together under a broader AOP network-based approach. In particular, metabolite analysis identified changes in several endogenous metabolites important for osmoregulation and membrane transport, indicating that spironolactone may increase the susceptibility of exposed fish to changes in osmotic gradients and nitrogen concentrations, two environmental variables known to vary at outflows of wastewater treatment plants. In addition to identifying this impact that points toward possible involvement of an AOP based on mineralocorticoidreceptor signaling, other evidence points to impacts that could not to be tied any specific receptor activation, but may have comparable implications for aquatic biota. Results from the metabolite analysis suggest the potential for spironolactone to exacerbate exposure effects for other contaminants, as we observed declines in an endogenous metabolite (i.e., <span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-carnitine) important for maintaining membrane integrity, reducing xenobiotic transport across cell membranes, and protecting against oxidative stress. This suggests that spironolactone could have interactive effects when found in combination with other contaminants. Similar integrative studies, which utilize multivariate analysis of pathway-based metabolomic and reproductive endpoint data, could help build on such results by evaluating the robustness of relationships described here and contribute to mounting evidence of metabolomics’ utility for AOP development and risk management (e.g., <a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">Åslund et al., 2013</a>, <a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">Davis et al., 2016</a>, <a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">Guo et al., 2009</a>).</p></section><section id="SM1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
