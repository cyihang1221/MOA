</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abs0010"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Geranylgeranoic acid (GGA) was developed as a preventative agent against second primary hepatoma, and was reported to induce cell death in human hepatoma cells via Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated pyroptosis. We recently reported that GGA is enzymatically biosynthesized from mevalonic acid in human hepatoma-derived HuH-7 cells and that endogenous GGA is found in most rat organs including the liver. An unbiased metabolomics analysis of ice-cold 50% acetonitrile extracts from control and GGA-treated cells was performed in this study to characterize the intracellular metabolic changes in GGA-induced pyroptosis and to analyze their relationship with the mechanism of GGA-induced cell death. The total positive ion chromatograms of the cellular extracts in ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry were apparently unchanged after GGA treatment, but an orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis score plot clearly discriminated the intracellular metabolite profiles of GGA-treated cells from that of control cells. S-plot analysis revealed 15 potential biomarkers up-regulated by 24-h GGA treatment according to their variable importance in the projection value of more than 1, and the subsequent metabolomics analysis identified nine of these metabolites as a group of lysophospholipids containing lysophosphatidylcholine with C16:0, C20:4, or C20:3 fatty acids. The possible roles of these lysophospholipids in GGA-induced pyroptosis are discussed.</p>

</ul></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0035">In 1995, we discovered a group of "acyclic retinoids" without a cyclic structure, which have ligand activity on retinoid receptors [<a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1">1</a>]. At the beginning of our research, we found that acyclic retinoids do indeed act as ligands for nuclear retinoid receptors and have differentiation-inducing effects such as enhanced albumin production in some human hepatoma cell lines [<a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1">1</a>,<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">2</a>]. Furthermore, acyclic retinoid significantly induced neural differentiation of the human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y, as did the natural active retinoid all-<em>trans</em>-retinoic acid (ATRA) [<a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">3</a>].</p>
<p id="p0040">Geranylgeranoic acid (GGA or all-<em>trans</em>-3,7,11,15-tetramethyl-2,6,10,14-hexadecatetraenoic acid) and its 4,5-didehydro derivative (peretinoin or all-<em>trans</em>-3,7,11,15-tetramethyl-2,4,6,10,14-hexadecapentaenoic acid) are the most well-studied acyclic retinoids so far as a preventive agent for second primary hepatoma (SPH) [<a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4">4</a>,<a href="#bib5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib5">5</a>]. Indeed, peretinoin was shown to have the potential to prevent SPH in randomized, placebo-controlled phase II/III clinical trials [<a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6">6</a>,<a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7">7</a>]. Using human hepatoma-derived cell lines, we found that both GGA and peretinoin induced tumor-specific cell death, which may be involved in the suppression of SPH [<a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8">8</a>,<a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9">9</a>]. We have reported that GGA induces cell death in human hepatoma cells via the unfolded protein response (UPR<sup>ER</sup>) and an incomplete response of autophagy [<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">10</a>,<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>]. Most recently, we concluded that GGA induces Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-signaled pyroptosis in human hepatoma cells [<a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12">12</a>].</p>
<p id="p0045">GGA causes activation of caspase-4 via TLR4-mediated UPR<sup>ER</sup>, resulting in cleavage of gasdermin D (GSDMD) and the translocation of its N-terminal fragment to the plasma membrane leading to the formation of the GSDMD pore [<a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12">12</a>]. Another TLR4 signaling causes increased reactive oxygen species production in the mitochondria, leading to the transfer of NF-κB to the nucleus and priming the NLRP3 inflammasome. Then, the influx of extracellular Ca<sup>2+</sup> causes inflammasome activation, which in turn activates caspase-1, leading to extensive cleavage of GSDMD and completing pyroptosis [<a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12">12</a>]. Given that autophagy can inactivate the inflammasome [<a href="#bib13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib13">13</a>], we believe that GGA-induced incomplete response of autophagy [<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">10</a>] may support the activation of caspase-1 by avoiding the inactivation of the inflammasome.</p>
<p id="p0050">Although we have gained some understanding of the signal transduction pathways in GGA-induced cell death of human hepatoma cells, we still have only limited knowledge of the changes in intracellular metabolites of GGA-treated cells. We previously reported that during GGA-induced cell death, GGA shifts the energy state of human hepatoma-derived HuH-7 cells from aerobic glycolysis to mitochondrial respiration via up-regulation of ‘TP53-induced glycolysis regulatory phosphatase’ (TIGAR) and ‘synthesis of cytochrome C oxidase 2’ (SCO2) proteins [<a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">14</a>]. In fact, we confirmed that GGA induced a time-dependent increase in the cellular contents of fructose 6-phosphate and decreased fructose 1,6-diphosphate. Furthermore, a preliminary metabolomics analysis revealed that GGA rapidly induced spermine accumulation [<a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">14</a>].</p>
<p id="p0055">In the present study, we decided to further perform the metabolomics analysis of HuH-7 cells to search for metabolites that respond rapidly and reliably to GGA. As a result, an orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) score plot distinguished GGA-treated cells from control cells, and S-plot analysis revealed that GGA induced the up-regulation of several lysophospholipid (LPL) species. The possible roles of the up-regulated LPLs in GGA-induced pyroptosis are discussed.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="sec2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Cell culture</h3>
<p id="p0060">HuH-7 cells were obtained from RIKEN BioResource Center (Tsukuba, Japan), and cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (D-MEM; Wako, Osaka, Japan) containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Hyclone, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).</p></section><section id="sec2.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. GGA treatment</h3>
<p id="p0065">The cells were inoculated in 9-cm dishes, at a density of 2.5 × 10<sup>6</sup> cells/dish and cultured with D-MEM containing 5% FBS for 2 days. Thereafter, the medium was replaced with FBS-free D-MEM 1 day before GGA addition to efficiently induce cell death in 24 h [<a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8">8</a>]. Since the LD<sub>50</sub> of GGA for HuH-7 cells is in the range of 5–10 μM [<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">10</a>,<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>], GGA (10 mM in ethanol) was added at a final concentration of 10 μM for 2–24 h. Ethanol was added as a control at 0.1% (v/v).</p></section><section id="sec2.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Extraction of metabolites</h3>
<p id="p0070">The procedure for the preparation of cell extracts has already been described in detail in our paper [<a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">14</a>]. Here, it is briefly presented below. After GGA treatment, the cells were quenched by replacement of the conditioned medium with ice-cold 0.9% (w/v) NaCl. Cell pellets were extracted in ice-cold 50% (v/v) aqueous acetonitrile (LC-MS Chromatosolv®; Fluka, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), lyophilized, dissolved in water and stored frozen at −80°C until analysis.</p>
<p id="p0075">Thawed and filtered samples were analyzed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) and quadrupole time-of-flight type mass spectrometric apparatus (Xevo™ Q-Tof/MS, Nihon Waters).</p></section><section id="sec2.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. UPLC</h3>
<p id="p0080">Reversed-phase chromatographic separation was performed on a 2.1 × 100 mm ACQUITY™ UPLC® BEH C18, 1.7 μm column at 40°C with the mobile phase consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid (HPLC grade, Wako) as solvent A and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid as solvent B. Up to 0.5 min after sample injection, the volume proportion of acetonitrile in eluent was kept at 1% and linearly increased to 95% in 10 min at a constant flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Then, acetonitrile was kept at 95% for 4 min. A 10-μL aliquot of each sample was injected automatically onto the column three times.</p></section><section id="sec2.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Q-Tof/MS</h3>
<p id="p0085">In-line mass spectrometry was performed on a Waters Xevo™ ESI-Q-Tof/MS apparatus, which was calibrated with sodium formate and lock-sprayed with Leu-enkephalin (Nihon Waters) each time, operating in positive ion mode. The operating conditions for Q-Tof/MS have already been described in detail [<a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">14</a>]. 1-Palmitoyl-<em>sn</em>-glycero-3-phosphocholine (synthetic, ≥99%, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as the standard for lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC(16:0)).</p></section><section id="sec2.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Data collection, processing, and multivariate statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="p0090">Raw chromatogram data of UPLC/Q-Tof/MS were initially converted to NetCDF (network common data file) formatted by Databridge using MarkerLynx® software (Waters). The peak width at 50% height was set to 3 s. Other parameters were all in default settings. Detected and matched peaks with retention time and <em>m/z</em> and their corresponding intensities were electronically exported to an Excel table.</p>
<p id="p0095">Principal component analysis (PCA) and OPLS-DA models were constructed to reproduce the differences in metabolites between GGA-treated and ethanol-treated control cells, using MarkerLynx® XS software. Potential biomarkers for GGA-treated cells were selected according to their variable importance in the projection (VIP) value of more than 1 (&gt;1) in the S-plot.</p></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results</h2>

Kinetics of LPL up-regulation induced by GGA treatment</h3>
<p id="p0120"><em>S-plot analysis:</em> On the basis of the combination of retention time in UPLC and the observed accurate mass, the LPLs presented in <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a> were traced on time-course S plots (<a href="#appsec1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S1</a>). LPC(20:4) (④) and LPE(20:4) (⑤) were detected in the upper-right region of the first quadrant of the S-plot already at 2 h after GGA treatment and were continuously detected at almost the same position at 4 and 8 h. However, LPC(16:0) (②) and LPC(16:1) (⑨) appeared in the upper-right corner of the S plot only after 2 h, and LPC(20:3) (①) and LPC(OH20:3) (③) appeared in the upper-right part of the S plot only after 8 h.</p>
<p id="p0125"><em>Semi-quantitative analysis:</em> A calibration curve for LPC(16:0) was prepared by UPLC-Q-Tof/MS using 1-palmitoyl-<em>sn</em>-glycero-3-phosphocholine under the same conditions as in the metabolomics analysis. The mass signal intensity of the peak area increased linearly from 80 to 20,000 in the concentration range of LPC(16:0) from 0.3 to 40 pmoles (<a href="#appsec1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S2</a>). Therefore, we targeted eight LPLs in <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a> for semi-quantitative analysis using their respective retention times and exact masses, assuming that the mass signal intensity was linear with the concentration of LPLs injected.</p>
<p id="p0130">As shown in <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>A, LPC(20:4) (④) and LPE(20:4) (⑤) were barely detectable before GGA treatment, but were significantly increased at 2 h after GGA treatment, and then gradually and continuously increased up to 24 h. LPC(20:3) (①) and LPC(OH20:3) (③) were almost undetectable at 0 h, and showed only a slight increase up to 4 h, a sharp increase at 8 h, and a further increase above the two 20:4-containing LPL levels at 24 h.</p>

<figcaption><p>Time-dependent changes in the concentrations of various LPLs in HuH-7 cells after GGA treatment. <strong>A</strong>: Time-dependent changes in LPLs containing polyunsaturated fatty acids (20:3, 20:4). <strong>B</strong>: Time-dependent changes in LPLs containing saturated fatty acids (14:0, 16:0) or monounsaturated fatty acid (16:1).</p></figcaption></figure><p id="p0135">Conversely, LPC(16:0) (②) was present in relatively large amounts even before GGA treatment and showed a transient 1.8-fold increase at 2 h, returning to the original value at 4 h and then gradually increasing to reach 2.2-fold of the basal level at 24 h (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>B). Similarly, LPC(16:1) (⑨) showed a transient increase of 4.1-fold at 2 h, and then gradually increased to 2.1-fold at 24 h. LPE(16:0) (⑦) and LPC(14:0) (⑪) increased 2.5- and 4-fold relative to the basal levels at 24 h, respectively.</p></section></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Discussion</h2>
<p id="p0140">In this paper, we undertook a metabolomics approach to observe the changes in intracellular metabolites of HuH-7 cells after 24-h GGA treatment. To our surprise, out of the top 11 identified biomarkers found to specifically increase in GGA-treated cells, nine were all LPLs. Although this was a completely unexpected result, a semi-quantitative analysis of the temporal variation of the eight LPLs after GGA treatment showed that LPC and LPE containing polyunsaturated or saturated fatty acids were already increased at 2 h and then further increased up to 24 h.</p>
<p id="p0145">In general, the increase in intracellular LPLs could be due to either an increase in LPLs production, a decrease in their degradation, or both (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>) [<a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a>,<a href="#bib16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib16">16</a>]. Here, we first considered the possibility of increased synthesis of LPLs: Because GGA-induced cell death is a type of inflammatory cell death with the cytosolic calcium surges [<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>,<a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12">12</a>] and nuclear translocation of cytosolic NF-κB [<a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12">12</a>], activation of phospholipase A2 (PLA2), which is well known to be activated by calcium [<a href="#bib17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib17">17</a>] and transactivated by NF-κB [<a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18">18</a>], was expected. However, because PLA2 is an enzyme known to release arachidonic acid in the <em>sn</em>-2 position and contributes to the production of eicosanoids [<a href="#bib19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib19">19</a>], it is highly unlikely that the activated PLA2 enhances the production of LPC(20:4) and LPE(20:4). The same is true for lecithin cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT), which transfers the fatty acid in the <em>sn</em>-2 position of PC to cholesterol [<a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20">20</a>]. Lipase C (LIPC) is a hydrolytic enzyme that liberates fatty acids in the <em>sn</em>-1 position [<a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21">21</a>]. When LIPC is activated, LPLs containing the most abundant 18:2 and 18:1 should increase. UPLC/Q-Tof/MS tracing using the exact mass of each LPL on the protonated assumption detected some LPCs and LPEs containing C18-series fatty acids, but all of them except LPC(18:1) showed a gradual, but not dramatic, increase after GGA addition (<a href="#appsec1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S3</a>). From these perspectives, GGA-induced up-regulation of the LPLs cannot be explained by the activation of any of the three enzymes, PLA2, LCAT, and LIPC.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="fig4" title="Fig. 4"><h3 class="obj_head">Fig. 4.</h3>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=8626837_gr4.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/17b4/8626837/d7e9778b83e3/gr4.jpg" loading="lazy" height="502" width="688" alt="Fig. 4"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/fig4/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>Enzymes involved in the increase and decrease in intracellular lysophospholipids in human hepatocytes. PLs, phospholipids; LPLs, lysophospholipids; LPAs, lysophosphatidic acids; LIPC, lipase C; LCAT, lecithin cholesterol acyltransferase; PLA2, phospholipase A2; LPCAT, lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase; ENPP2, ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 2; ATX, autotaxin; LPLD, lysophospholipase D.</p></figcaption></figure><p id="p0150">Next, we considered the suppression of the decreasing system of LPLs. There are two possible mechanisms: the suppression of LPC acyltransferase (LPCAT) and the suppression of ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 2 (ENPP2) (see <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>). It has been reported that the expression of either LPCAT1 or ENPP2 is increased and some LPCs are decreased in diverse human tumors including hepatoma [<a href="#bib22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib22">22</a>], melanoma [<a href="#bib23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib23">23</a>], and acute myeloid leukemia [<a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24">24</a>]. LPCAT catalyzes the reacylation of LPLs at the <em>sn</em>-2 position. LPCAT3, which is abundant in the liver, prefers 18:2- or 20:4-acyl CoA as a donor of fatty acids to LPC and LPE [<a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a>]. Therefore, LPLs, which are increased by inhibition of LPCAT, would contain mostly saturated fatty acids. Conversely, ENPP2 acts on any LPL such as LPC and LPE, to produce LPA [<a href="#bib16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib16">16</a>]. Therefore, when ENPP2 is suppressed, LPC and LPE containing saturated or unsaturated fatty acids would increase.</p>
<p id="p0155">On the basis of the above considerations, it is reasonable to speculate that the GGA-induced increase in multiple LPLs could be mainly due to the down-regulation of ENPP2. In other words, if ENPP2 was suppressed by GGA, it could well explain GGA-induced increase in intracellular LPLs such as LPC(16:0), LPC(20:4), LPE(16:0), and LPE(20:4). Of note, ENPP2 expression has recently been considered to be very important in hepatoma development [<a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25">25</a>], including replication of hepatitis B virus [<a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26">26</a>] and hepatitis C virus [<a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27">27</a>]. Moreover, high ENPP2 expression in hepatoma is detected in patients with histological grade II/III [<a href="#bib28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib28">28</a>]. Therefore, ENPP2 is expected to be a promising target for hepatoma suppression, and the possibility of reducing ENPP2 activity by GGA will pave the way for future hepatoma prevention.</p>
<p id="p0160">Finally, we discuss the role of the GGA-induced increases in the LPLs identified in the present study in GGA-induced pyroptosis. We reported that GGA-induced cell death is very similar to the well-known lipotoxicity caused by saturated fatty acids [<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>]. The GGA-induced UPR<sup>ER</sup> is inhibited by oleate co-treatment, which is similar to the UPR<sup>ER</sup> induced by saturated fatty acids [<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>]. Of note, Kakisaka et al. reported that palmitate-mediated lipotoxicity in HuH-7 cells is associated with UPR<sup>ER</sup>, which is caused by LPC(16:0) [<a href="#bib29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib29">29</a>]. In fact, they showed that palmitate is not required to induce UPR<sup>ER</sup> and lipotoxicity in HuH-7 cells if only LPC(16:0) is added to the medium [<a href="#bib29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib29">29</a>]. Interestingly, the unbiased metabolomics analysis in the present study showed that LPC(16:0) was transiently increased 2 h after the addition of GGA to HuH-7 cells. Hence, it can be explained that intracellular LPC(16:0) increased by GGA may induce UPR<sup>ER</sup>, resulting in eventual cell death. In addition, a previous report showing that LPCs cause pro-inflammatory changes by activating TLR4 [<a href="#bib30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib30">30</a>] and a recent report demonstrating that LPC induces pyroptosis [<a href="#bib31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib31">31</a>] both strongly support the idea that the increase in a group of LPLs by GGA forms part of the mechanism for pyroptotic cell death via TLR4 signaling through UPR<sup>ER</sup>.</p>
<p id="p0165">In conclusion, we performed an unbiased metabolomics analysis in GGA-treated cells, which revealed GGA-induced up-regulation of several LPLs including LPC(20:4), LPE(20:4), LPC(16:0), and LPE(16:0). The possible mechanism by which GGA increases these LPLs and the involvement of LPLs in GGA-induced pyroptosis were discussed. LPLs are a class of lipids that are not only localized inside cells but also secreted outside cells. In particular, the majority of plasma LPLs are thought to be secreted by the liver [<a href="#bib32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib32">32</a>]. Therefore, analyzing the profile and concentration of LPLs in the blood during clinical hepatoma treatment may allow monitoring of cell death in hepatoma cells.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Data availability</h2>
