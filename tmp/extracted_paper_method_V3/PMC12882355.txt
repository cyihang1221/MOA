<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Background</h3>
<p id="Par1">Return to sport (RTS) after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) remains a critical issue in sports medicine. Despite improvements in rehabilitation, many patients fail to return to their preinjury level of activity. Previous studies have primarily focused on clinical and biomechanical factors that influence RTS; however, the impact of intrinsic physiological changes, particularly urinary proteomic profiles, has not yet been fully explored. This study aimed to identify potential urinary protein biomarkers associated with RTS in ACLR patients.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p id="Par2">A total of 30 ACLR patients, at least 9 months post-surgery, were recruited. Patients were divided into RTS and non-RTS groups based on their ability to RTS, recovery to preinjury Tegner levels, and a leg symmetry index (LSI) of ≥ 85% on the single-leg hop (SLH) test. Furthermore, urine samples were analyzed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry to identify differentially expressed proteins associated with RTS. Potential biomarkers and mechanism associated with RTS were identified by series of bioinformatics and machine learning methods such as pathway enrichment methods, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), receiver operating characteristic curve and correlation analysis.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p id="Par3">Significant differences in knee muscle characteristics, including limb circumferences and isokinetic strength, were observed between the RTS and Non-RTS groups. A total of 3433 proteins were identified, with 20 upregulated and 58 downregulated in the RTS group. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses revealed key pathways including regulation of actin cytoskeleton, calcium ion binding, and Pathways related to inflammation such as IL-17 signaling pathway and neutrophil extracellular trap formation. Five proteins, including Glypican-3 (GPC3), Phosphatidylinositol transfer protein alpha isoform (PIPNA), Rap guanine nucleotide exchange factor 3 (RPGF3), Histone H1.5 (H15), and Small proline-rich protein 3 (SPRR3), were identified as potential biomarkers for evaluating RTS.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h3>
<p id="Par4">Muscle function was the primary factor influencing RTS after ACLR. The study revealed proteomic differences between those who RTS and those who do not. The identified potential biomarkers, such as GPC3, PIPNA, RPGF3, H15, and SPRR3, may serve as candidate targets to guide interventions designed to improve RTS outcomes following ACLR.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Trial registration</h3>
<p id="Par5">This study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200061779) on 02/07/2022.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, Return to sport, Proteomics, Urine, Muscle strength</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Background</h2>
<p id="Par23">Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury is one of the most prevalent musculoskeletal diseases. It has an incidence of approximately 17.5 per 100,000 person-years, constituting about 50% of all knee ligament injuries [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>, <a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>]. Following ACL reconstruction (ACLR) and rehabilitation, a primary objective is to restore knee stability and functional capacity, enabling patients to return to sports (RTS) [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>]. Notably, it is recommended to postpone RTS until at least 9 months after ACLR to reduce the re-injury risk [<a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>]. Even so, approximately 45% to 60% of patients with ACLR are unable to return to their preinjury level of activity [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>]. Factors influencing the likelihood of RTS can be categorized into five main criteria: psychological function, time to RTS, strength, functional performance, and risk factors such as age, sex, and combined injuries [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>–<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>]. To date, the test battery used to evaluate RTS following ACLR remains under continuous improvement.</p>
<p id="Par24">In addition to the clinical factors mentioned above, several studies have also demonstrated that internal physiological changes can affect sports performance in ACLR patients. Slater et al. [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>] reported that unilateral quadriceps strength was significantly correlated with aerobic fitness, and that aerobic fitness was reduced in ACLR patients compared to healthy subjects. Iris Leister et al. [<a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>] found that muscular fatigue led to a demonstrable deterioration in functional performance in healthy subjects, resulting in a statistically significant reduction in jumping distance of approximately 10.5 cm in either leg in the SLH. Dauty et al. [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>] also found that ACLR patients with arthrofibrosis exhibited a significantly lower quadriceps limb symmetry index (LSI) at 4, 7, and 12 postoperative months compared to the control group. Additionally, a significant and durable quadriceps muscle weakness occurred after arthrofibrosis, primarily due to an arthrogenic muscle inhibition. Further investigations are still required to confirm the possibility and elucidate the potential mechanisms of internal physiological changes after ACLR.</p>
<p id="Par25">Mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics is used to comprehensively study the composition, functions, interactions, and connections of all proteins under specific physiological or pathological conditions from a holistic perspective [<a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>]. Proteomics has also been exploited in the field of ACL injury and reconstruction. Several studies have investigated the early biological changes that occur after ACL tears, and these investigations may lead to improved methods for predicting clinical outcomes and the risk of future morbidities. Brophy et al. [<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>] collected synovial fluid from 105 patients with acute ACL injury, and they found that patient sex, injury pattern, and location of bone bruises were associated with the composition of the proteomics of synovial fluid in ACL tears. Jacobs et al. [<a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>] explored serum proteomic profiles between 6- and 14-days post-ACL injury, concluding that insulin-like growth factor binding protein 5 may have important implications for skeletal muscle atrophy after ACL injury. In addition to the acute phase of ACL injury, proteomic characteristics in the subacute phase have also been explored. Periostin was regarded as a synovial fluid biomarker of subacute ACL injury, setting it apart from osteoarthritis (OA); it contributed to the initiation of joint degeneration and may offer a window of opportunity to intervene in the early stages of post-traumatic OA [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. While the current study mainly focused on the proteomic features after ACL injury, there is a lack of literature regarding the proteomic profiles after ACLR. Currently, synovial fluid and serum are commonly used samples for proteomic analysis in ACL-related studies. However, to the best of our knowledge, the urine proteomic response after ACL injury and reconstruction has not been performed. Urine is an ideal source for disease-associated proteomic studies because urine collection is non-invasive and relatively simple, and urinary proteins could serve as biomarkers to reflect the status of the entire body under various physiological changes and disease processes [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>].</p>
<p id="Par26">Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate intrinsic factors associated with RTS after ACLR by urinary proteomics. We hypothesized that specific proteins would be differentially expressed in patients with RTS compared to those without it.</p>
<p id="Par27">This study could provide a reference for finding potential non-invasive biomarkers that could assist clinicians in objectively assessing recovery status, optimizing rehabilitation strategies, and guiding safe RTS decisions for patients following ACLR.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Participants</h3>
<p id="Par28">Participants were recruited from the National Institute of Sports Medicine in China. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Sport (No. 102772022RT041). This study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200061779) on 02/07/2022. Before participation, all participants were introduced to detailed procedures and signed the informed consent form. Inclusion criteria for the study were: (1) aged 18–60 years, (2) unilateral ACL injury with/without meniscus damage determined by clinical exam (positive anterior drawer test, Lachman test and pivot shift test) that was corroborated using magnetic resonance imaging, (3) underwent ACLR surgery with semitendinosus and gracilis hamstrings tendon (HT) autograft, (4) no history of trauma or surgery on the contralateral knee joint. Exclusion criteria were as follows:1) previous knee surgery, 2) concomitant ligamentous injuries (such as posterior cruciate ligament injury, medial or lateral collateral ligament injury) and chondral damage, 3) a clinical history of any inflammatory disorder, 4) patients complicated with metabolic disease, cerebrovascular disease, neurodegenerative disorders and urinary system dysfunction, (5) history of drug or alcohol abuse, (6) pregnant or nursing (lactating) women.</p></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study design</h3>
<p id="Par29">ACL reconstruction was performed with an HT autograft by one of two experienced knee surgeons at the National Institute of Sports Medicine in China. There was no heterogeneity existed in graft selection during surgery. After surgery, all participants followed the same standardized rehabilitation protocol offered by the clinic. Progressive knee range-of-motion exercises and partial weight-bearing with crutches were initiated as tolerated the day after ACLR. In the first two weeks following surgery, patients were instructed to reduce knee swelling, bear weight as tolerated, and perform range-of-motion exercises for the knee. Over the course of three weeks, wall squats, a stationary bike, and straight-leg raises were introduced, followed by progressive blocks of strength, power, and plyometric exercises at approximately 5 weeks. After 3 months, the rehabilitation progressed to jogging once there was no evidence of swelling. The range of motion, lower muscle strength, and proprioceptive sensation should be continuously improved. After 6 months, patients were allowed to return to sports, including kicking, dribbling, and changing direction. Patients were encouraged to increase the intensity of their training gradually. For this study, clinical assessments and urine collection were conducted approximately 9 months after ACLR. In this study, the criteria for RTS include being greater than 9 months postoperatively, recovering to a preinjury level of Tegner, and an SLH test LSI of 85% or greater. Patients were grouped according to whether they returned to sport (RTS group) or not (Non-RTS group).</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Clinical data acquisition</h3>
<p id="Par30">Urine samples were collected from each participant at the time of study inclusion. Then, participants underwent clinical tests, including subjective evaluations (International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC), Lysholm, and Tegner), KT-2000 measurement, limb circumference measurement, isokinetic strength testing, and the SLH test.</p>
<section id="Sec6"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Limb circumference</h4>
<p id="Par31">Bilateral lower limb circumferences were measured with a flexible ruler without elasticity. Limb circumference was measured at 15 cm, 10 cm, and 5 cm from the patella upper margin, the knee joint, and 15 cm below the lower edge of the patella, respectively. Index of atrophy = (unaffected limb circumference - affected limb circumference)/unaffected limb circumference.</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Isokinetic muscle strength</h4>
<p id="Par32">Measurements of concentric isokinetic strength of the knee extensor and flexor muscles were performed at a speed of 60°/s (5 repetitions) and 180°/s (10 repetitions) from a range of 0° to 90° of knee flexion of both limbs with a isokinetic dynamometer (Biodex Medical System, New York, USA). The peak torque (PT), the relative peak torque, and total power (TW) were recorded. The relative peak torque was calculated by normalizing the peak torque to body mass (PT/BW). LSI was calculated for both quadriceps and hamstrings PT by expressing the injured limb relative to the unaffected limb. The hamstrings: quadriceps ratio (H/Q) was calculated by expressing hamstrings torque relative to quadriceps torque.</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Single-Leg hop for distance</h4>
<p id="Par33">The patient stood on one foot, jumped forward as far as possible, and landed on the same foot. If the landing resulted in loss of balance, the trial was repeated until the landing was controlled. The hop distance was measured from the starting line (with the toe touching the line) to the toe of the foot on landing and recorded using a tape measure. All participants were given two practice trials and then performed three measured trials on each leg, with the non-operated leg first. The averaged value for each leg was recorded. For hop distance, an LSI was calculated as the operated leg/the non-operated leg × 100%.</p></section></section><section id="Sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Urine proteomics</h3>
<p id="Par34">Urine was collected from each participant at the time of study inclusion. Subsequently, urine samples were collected, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until used. Frozen samples were thawed at room temperature, and 10 mL of each patient’s urine was taken for proteomic analysis. The urine samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Then, the protein was precipitated with three times the volume of pre-cooled acetone for 4 h at −20 °C. The precipitates were solubilized in 8 M urea and sonicated. Protein was digested using standard filter-aided sample preparation. Enzymatic digestion with trypsin was performed, and the resulting peptides were analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). LC analysis was performed using the Ultimate 3000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), with one injection per sample for all tested samples. The resulting peptides were analyzed by an Orbitrap Exploris 480 Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Protein identification and quantification were performed using Proteome Discoverer (PD, version 2.5, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Then, data processing and analysis were performed in an R environment.</p></section><section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Biomarker selection and validation</h3>
<p id="Par35">Differentially expressed proteins between the RTS and Non-RTS groups were first identified using a two-sample t-test (|Log₂ fold-change (FC)|&gt; 1, <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05). 20 of the highest significantly up-regulated and 20 of the highest significantly down-regulated proteins were selected for subsequent modeling. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was then applied to these candidate proteins using the R package glmnet (v4.1–4). A 10-fold cross-validation procedure was used to determine the optimal regularization parameter (λ) that minimized the mean cross-validated error. Proteins with non-zero regression coefficients at this optimal λ were regarded as LASSO-selected feature proteins. Subsequently, Spearman correlation analyses were performed between the relative abundance of proteins (The top 20 up- and top 20 down-regulated proteins) and differential clinical indexes. Proteins that exhibited significant correlations (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) with at least one clinical index were intersected with the LASSO-selected features to obtain a subset of clinically relevant proteins. The discriminative ability of each intersected protein was further evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Proteins with area under the curve (AUC) &gt; 0.8 were defined as candidate biomarkers with clinical relevance [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>].</p></section><section id="Sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="Par36">The proteomic raw data files were analyzed using Proteome Discoverer (PD, Thermo Fisher Scientific, version 2.5) for protein identification and quantification; with the Mascot algorithm. The database search was performed against the human UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot FASTA database (released in 2023, containing 20,423 entries). Trypsin and Lys-C were selected as specific proteolytic enzymes, and up to two missed cleavages were allowed. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, whereas methionine oxidation and protein N-terminal acetylation were defined as variable modifications. Both the peptide-spectrum match (PSM) false discovery rate (FDR) and the protein-level FDR were controlled below 1%.</p>
<p id="Par37">Quantitative results were exported from PD and imported into Perseus (version 1.6.14.0) for downstream statistical analysis. Label-free quantification intensity values were Log₂-transformed, and missing values were imputed from a normal distribution (width = 0.3, downshift = 1.8) to simulate signals from low-abundance proteins near the detection limit [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>]. The significantly different proteins were determined by upregulated fold-change (FC) ≥ 2 or downregulated FC ≤ 0.5, and the <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05 of an independent-sample t-test.</p>
<p id="Par38">The UniProt (<a href="https://www.uniprot.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.uniprot.org/</a>) was used for protein identification. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was performed using DAVID (<a href="https://david.ncifcrf.gov/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://david.ncifcrf.gov/</a>), and Gene Ontology (GO) annotations (<a href="http://geneontology.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://geneontology.org/</a>) were used for the functional annotation of proteins. Differences in continuous clinical variables with a normal distribution were evaluated using the independent sample t-test. In contrast, differences in variables with non-normal distributions were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Comparison between the two groups’ nominal variables was tested with the Chi-squared test. The means ± standard deviations or count (%) of clinical variables were calculated. Correlation analyses were conducted between urine proteomic data and clinical measures. All data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0 (Chicago, IL, USA), with a statistical significance level set at <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05.</p></section></section><section id="Sec12"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>

<div class="tw-foot p"><div class="fn" id="_fn_p29"><p><em>VAS </em>Visual analog scale,<em> IKDC </em>International knee documentation committee,<em> ACL-RSI </em>ACL-return to sport after injury score,<em> RTS </em>Return to sport</p></div></div></section></section><section id="Sec15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Isokinetic muscle strength</h3>
<p id="Par43">The RTS group demonstrated significantly superior muscle performance compared with the Non-RTS group across multiple isokinetic parameters. Specifically, on the affected side, the PT of the knee extensors at 60°/s and 180°/s were markedly higher than those in the Non-RTS group (126.17 ± 38.30 N.m vs. 80.83 ± 34.21 N.m, <em>P</em> = 0.002; 100.10 ± 34.13 N.m vs. 69.04 ± 33.71 N.m, <em>P</em> = 0.018). The PT/BW of knee extensors at 60°/s and 180°/s of the RTS group were also higher than those in the Non-RTS group (154.36 ± 39.92% vs. 105.99 ± 35.78%, <em>P</em> = 0.002; 125.84 ± 39.64% vs. 89.63 ± 34.81%, <em>P</em> = 0.014). And the TW of the extensors at 60°/s and 180°/s was also higher (638.66 ± 213.22 J vs. 425.04 ± 127.28 J, <em>P</em> = 0.006; 1084.93 ± 360.97 J vs. 726.78 ± 345.47 J, <em>P</em> = 0.013). Furthermore, LSI for extension at 60°/s and 180°/s were significantly greater in the RTS group (0.85 ± 0.35 vs. 0.58 ± 0.20, <em>P</em> = 0.017; 0.94 ± 0.28 vs. 0.72 ± 0.25, <em>P</em> = 0.028;), while the H/Q ratio at 60°/s of the RTS group were significantly less than the non-RTS group (0.56 ± 0.18 vs. 0.84 ± 0.28 J, <em>P</em> = 0.003). These findings collectively suggested that patients who successfully returned to sport exhibited more complete recovery of quadriceps strength, endurance, and limb symmetry following ACL reconstruction. The detailed results were shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>.</p>

<p id="Par45">In total, 25,908 peptides, corresponding to 3433 proteins, were identified. An unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) revealed significant differences between the proteomes of the proteomes of RTS and Non-RTS groups, highlighting the high heterogeneity among urine samples (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>A). Compared with the Non-RTS group, 20 proteins were upregulated (FC &gt; 2, <em>P</em> value &lt; 0.05), and 58 were downregulated in the RTS group (FC &lt; 0.5, <em>P</em> value &lt; 0.05). <p id="Par60">The biomarker screening flow chart was shown in the Fig. <a href="#Fig8" class="usa-link">8</a>. Among the 3433 quantified urinary proteins, 78 were differentially expressed between the RTS and Non-RTS groups (|log₂FC| &gt; 1, <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05). The top 20 up- and 20 down-regulated proteins were entered into the LASSO model. LASSO regression with repeated cross-validation identified 13 proteins contributing most to RTS discrimination. Correlation analysis revealed that 34 proteins were significantly associated with postoperative clinical functional performance. The intersection between the LASSO-selected and clinically correlated proteins yielded 10 overlapping proteins. ROC analyses of these proteins demonstrated high discriminative performance for RTS status: H15 (AUC = 0.827, <em>P</em> = 0.002), SPRR3 (AUC = 0.831, <em>P</em> = 0.002), RPGF3 (AUC = 0.844, <em>P</em> = 0.001), PIPNA (AUC = 0.876, <em>P</em>&lt;0.001), and GPC3 (AUC = 0.840, <em>P</em> = 0.002) (Fig. <a href="#Fig9" class="usa-link">9</a>). These 5 proteins were therefore defined as potential biomarkers with clinical relevance for functional recovery following ACL reconstruction.</p>

<p id="Par63">The aim of this study was to investigate intrinsic factors associated with RTS after ACLR by urinary proteomics. Objective clinical assessments revealed significant differences in knee muscle characteristics between the RTS and non-RTS groups. Moreover, 78 proteins exhibited differential expression levels between the two groups. The results suggested correlations between specific proteins and clinical assessments, which could facilitate the mechanistic elucidations of factors affecting the performance of motor function and serve as ideal therapeutic targets to promote RTS in patients with ACLR.</p>
<p id="Par64">There were significant differences in ACL-RSI scores and muscle function (as measured by limb circumference and isokinetic muscle strength) between the two groups. The result of ACL-RSI in this study (RTS group: 73.45, non-RTS group: 57.45, <em>P</em> = 0.041) was similar with previous studies. Chmielewski et al. [<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>] suggested those with less kinesiophobia consistently displayed greater performance on the return to sports participation after ACLR. Zwolski et al. [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>] demonstrated that patients with greater self-reported fear of injury were more likely to have decreased functional testing scores and reduced strength. Furthermore, they were 13 times more likely to suffer a second ACL injury within the first 24 months after ACLR. Previous studies have also reported somewhat similar findings, indicating that poor quadriceps strength is associated with poor RTS performance. Kitaguchi et al. [<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>] found that preoperative quadriceps index (QI) could be a predictor of RTS at one year following ACLR, with a preoperative QI cut-off of 66%. N.M. Furthermore, Nuala Crotty et al. [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>] found a significant relationship between ACLR-limb isokinetic extensor strength and single-leg drop jump height (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.001, r<sup>2</sup> = 0.29) at 9 months after ACLR.</p>
<p id="Par65">KEGG analysis indicated that hyperinflammation and regulation of actin cytoskeleton was likely to affect motor functional performance of ACLR patients.</p>
<p id="Par66">The inflammation-related pathways of the “IL-17 signaling pathway” and “Neutrophil extracellular trap formation” were identified in the present study, indicating that hyperinflammation also affects ACLR motor function performance.</p>
<p id="Par67">IL-17 signaling plays a critical role in mediating inflammatory responses by inducing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and CCL20, while simultaneously triggering endoplasmic reticulum stress and reactive oxygen species generation in myoblasts; these processes collectively promote immune cell recruitment and amplify both local and systemic inflammation [<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>]. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are one of the neutrophil tactics for restricting and removing external infections [<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>]. Previous experiments have shown that inhibiting the production of NETs can effectively alleviate arthritic conditions and reduce synovial inflammation [<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>]. The cytoskeleton refers to the three-dimensional network structure composed of proteins, including microtubules, microfilaments, and intermediate filaments, present in eukaryotic cells. Its primary function is to determine the cell’s shape as a supportive structure and establish a uniform matrix for the spatial arrangement of various organelles, macromolecular polymers, and living macromolecules within the cell. Additionally, it provides the force necessary for the cellular activities [<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>]. The dynamic characteristics of the cytoskeleton such as assembly and disassembly of MTs and MFs, are also regulated by calcium ions [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>].</p>
<p id="Par68">The GO enrichment analysis indicated that the RTS phase following ACLR was accompanied by distinct molecular adaptations. RTS after ACLR might be characterized by simultaneous activation of tissue-regenerative signaling pathways and selective downregulation of immune and extracellular structural responses. Upregulated proteins were predominantly enriched in developmental and regulatory pathways such as the Wnt, planar cell polarity, and smoothened signaling pathway. The Wnt signaling pathway is activated in this phase and is essential for skeletal muscle regeneration; canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling promotes satellite cell activation and myoblast differentiation, which underpin the maintenance of muscle mass and enhancement of muscle strength [<a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>, <a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a>]. In contrast, significant downregulation of processes related to keratinocyte differentiation, epidermis development, and antimicrobial as well as antifungal defense responses suggested a suppression of epidermal and immune-related protein expression during the later recovery phase. This suppression may serve to shift the local environment from a predominantly inflammatory state toward one that favors tissue regeneration [<a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>]. Moreover, analysis of molecular function and cellular components revealed a biphasic modulation of extracellular matrix constituents, with a selective increase in structural proteins such as collagen-containing matrix components and a broad decrease in proteins involved in ion binding (such as calcium ion binding, calcium-dependent protein binding), structural molecule activity, and extracellular exosome formation. Calcium ion triggers muscle contraction and its removal relaxation [<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>]. The troponin-tropomyosin system plays a crucial role in the regulatory mechanism of striated muscle contraction [<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>]. Abnormalities, including dystrophinopathy-associated changes in voltage-gated sodium channels, calcium release channels, calcium pumps, and calcium-binding proteins, have been demonstrated in patient biopsy specimens and genetic animal models of dystrophinopathy [<a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>].</p>
<p id="Par69">LASSO regression and correlation analyses revealed 5 overlapping proteins (GPC3, PIPNA, RPGF3, H15, and SPRR3) that were potential key targets of RTS following ACLR. In the RTS group following ACLR, the significant upregulation of GPC3, PIPNA, and RPGF3 underscored an enhanced anabolic and regenerative signaling environment. Elevated GPC3, a heparan sulfate proteoglycan, has been linked to increased lean mass and muscle strength, suggesting its vital role in promoting muscle protein synthesis and remodeling [<a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a>]. Lynch et al. [<a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>]found that compared to healthy control lines, the myocytes from all amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) lines showed downregulation of GPC3. ALS is a fatal neuromuscular disease in which patients gradually become paralyzed due to loss of motor function. Although direct literature on PIPNA is limited, its annotation as a nuclear or adaptor protein that binds phosphatidylinositol phosphates indicates its potential to regulate PI signaling cascades—including the Akt–mTOR axis—which are essential for actin cytoskeletal remodeling, calcium flux regulation, and muscle repair [<a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a>]. RPGF3, a growth factor-related protein akin to PDGF family members, may drive vascular remodeling and fibroblast activation necessary for paracrine support during muscle regeneration [<a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>]. Together, these upregulated proteins appear to facilitate an environment conducive to muscle hypertrophy, enhanced repair, and optimal functional recovery.</p>
<p id="Par70">Conversely, the downregulation of SPRR3 and H15 in the RTS cohort suggested attenuation of pathways associated with cellular stress and maladaptive remodeling. SPRR3 is both members of the Small Proline-Rich Protein family, which is known for its significant roles in skin barrier formation and responses to mechanical and environmental stress [<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a>]. The excessive expression of SPRR3 is associated with fibrosis and inflammatory responses, reflecting their broader roles in tissue response to injury and stress [<a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a>]. For instance, in cardiac tissue, SPRR3 has been shown to regulate integrin and growth factor signaling, which are crucial pathways in the development of fibrosis following myocardial infarction [<a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a>]. Reduced expression of SPRR3 after ACLR likely helps to decrease adverse fibrotic and inflammatory processes, thereby enhancing muscle repair efficiency. H15, likely reflecting extracellular histone H1 variants such as H1.5, is typically released during nuclear stress and cell damage; its reduction may indicate diminished tissue injury and improved chromatin stability during recovery [<a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">39</a>]. This coordinated downregulation indicates a favorable shift in the muscle microenvironment that supports efficient regeneration and functional restoration post-ACLR. These biomarker dynamics reveal a critical balance between regenerative stimulation and stress attenuation that underpins effective muscle recovery. These above-mentioned proteins could be used as potential targets to explain the factors affecting muscle function following ACLR. However, further verification is still needed.</p>
<p id="Par71">This study comprehensively explored the influencing factors of RTS following ACLR and found that muscle function was the main factor affecting motor performance in patients after ACLR. The urine proteomic further revealed that the potential primary pathophysiological mechanisms underlying decreased muscle function in ACLR patients were reactive myofibrosis, chronic inflammation, and proteasomal proteolytic system. However, the present study had certain limitations. First, the sample size was limited, and it was essential to validate the findings in a larger sample population. Second, while urine offered a non-invasive and easily accessible medium for biomarker analysis, some tissue-specific or joint microenvironment–related changes might not be captured through urinary proteomics alone. Third, although all participants underwent ACL reconstruction using the same graft type and a standardized rehabilitation protocol, the study did not specifically control for the timing of surgery relative to the injury. The interval between injury and surgery could have influenced the recovery process. Moreover, the actual rehabilitation progress completed by each patient during recovery was not fully recorded, which may have introduced individual variability in rehabilitation outcomes. Fourth, the present analysis was based on a single urine collection time point without perioperative or longitudinal sampling, limiting the ability to capture temporal dynamics or infer causality. However, this time point corresponds to the standard clinical evaluation for return-to-sport assessment. Finally, this study represents an exploratory, discovery-phase study without healthy control samples, restricting the distinction between recovery-related and normal physiological changes. In the future, studies integrating multi-timepoint sampling, inclusion of healthy control cohorts, and combined analysis of urine with synovial fluid, or tissue-specific biomarkers will be essential to validate and extend these findings. Furthermore, targeted verification using independent cohorts combined with additional in vitro and in vivo experiments will be essential to confirm the diagnostic potential and mechanistic relevance of the potential biomarkers.</p></section><section id="Sec26"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p id="Par72">Muscle function is the primary factor affecting the RTS at 9 months after ACLR. There are some proteomic differences in ACLR patients who achieve RTS versus those who do not. Several differentially expressed proteins, including GPC3, PIPNA, RPGF3, H15, and SPRR3, are associated with clinical function. These proteins may serve as potential biomarkers to monitor rehabilitation progress, identify patients at risk of delayed RTS, and guide personalized interventions. Collectively, this study provides a mechanistic link between proteomic changes and post-ACLR functional recovery, offering a foundation for more targeted, evidence-based strategies to enhance RTS outcomes.</p></section><section id="glossary1" class="glossary"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Abbreviations</h2>
