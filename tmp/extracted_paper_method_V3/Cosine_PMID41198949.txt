>Methods   Our model operates on the principle of interpreting each peak not as a unique fragment, but as an assembly of rAEs. This allows for a granular breakdown of each fragment into its constituent atom-types. Figure  4 b demonstrates our method of translating spectral data into structural information using a transformer model 68 , 69 . Peaks are assigned a logRank, a logarithmic intensity measure ranging from 1 to 7 (see Fig.  4 a). This system, adapted from the work of Cao and Guler 19 , is designed to minimize parameter counts and prevent overfitting, offering a more refined approach than traditional intensity rankings. Inputs to the transformer model consist of  m / z  values, intensity indicators (’ i ’), and their corresponding logRanks.   The heart of the model is the transformer decoder, which takes the encoded context and predicts output probabilities for each input token, essentially ’decoding’ the tokens that carry both mass and intensity information into the target data. The target data in our model is structured to capture the relationship between fragment ions and molecular content. Specifically, it is represented by sequences of blocks of rAEs–groupings of atom-types that constitute the observed fragment ions–followed by a special token <mol>, which marks the transition to representing the overall molecular structure. Each block of rAEs on the left side of <mol> depicts atom-types from individual fragments, treated as separate tokens, while the rAEs to the right of <mol> indicate the atom-types comprising the molecule itself, also treated as individual tokens. Since rAEs represent disconnected substructures, permuted variants (e.g., [O].[N] vs. [N].[O]) correspond to the same underlying chemical structure. To ensure consistency and reproducibility, all rAEs-groupings are sorted alphabetically before being passed to the model. Although the self-attention mechanism in transformers is inherently permutation invariant, we still apply positional encodings, as prior studies have shown that incorporating positional information–as a training-time convention–improves learning stability and model performance, even for order-invariant inputs 70 – 72 .   In the absence of a molecular ion peak in the training data, the structural content of the molecular ion can still be accurately represented by deriving its rAEs directly from the molecular structure. These rAEs are equivalent, in terms of atom-type content, to those that would be obtained from the molecular ion fragment if it had been observed or correctly predicted. Our transformer model does not depend on the presence of a molecular ion peak. Instead, it learns peak-to-fragment relationships via contextual embeddings over the entire spectrum. This means the model does not require a strict one-to-one correspondence between individual peaks and their source fragments. Rather, it integrates contextual evidence from all observed peaks to infer the most probable molecular atom-types.   Such structured tokenization aligns with our hypothesis that the molecular content can be deduced from the rAEs-groupings generated from spectral peaks. As a final step, the generated rAE blocks undergo post-processing-aggregated, decomposed, and organized into a set to construct atom-type content of a given mass spectrum. The PyTorch machine learning library was used for constructing and training the transformer model 73 . Model training setup and hyperparameters are summarized in the Supplementary Methods. As a side note, while methods such as MolForge 53  exist for reconstructing molecules from atomic environments, direct reassembly from predicted rAEs remains infeasible in our setting. This is because EI-induced bond dissociations generate fragment-derived rAEs not present in the intact molecule, inflating the prediction space with spurious atom environments and hindering accurate reconstruction.
