<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Background</h3>
<p id="Par1">Saliva-derived small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) are emerging as potential biomarkers for coronary artery disease (CAD). Early identification of these biomarkers is essential for effective management and improved patient outcomes. Our study aimed to isolate and characterize sEVs from saliva to identify non-invasive protein signatures in younger CAD patients.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p id="Par2">Saliva sEVs were obtained from 20 CAD patients aged 18–65 years, and 20 healthy controls matched for age and gender. The saliva exosome or sEVs isolation was performed using differential ultracentrifugation and sucrose density gradient methods, and we characterized the sEVs using transmission electron microscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and nanoparticle tracking analysis. Western blotting was done with exosome markers including Anti-Flotilin-1, Anti-TSG-101, and Anti-CD63. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified through label-free LC–MS/MS Orbitrap and data was analyzed using Proteome Discoverer 3.0 and statistical analysis using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Protein–protein interaction network, gene ontology, and pathways enrichment analysis were performed.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p id="Par3">We identified 506 proteins using label-free LC–MS/MS proteomic approaches, with 18 significant DEPs. Notable upregulated proteins included mainly Cystatin-S (CST1/CST2/CST4), Protein S100, alpha-amylase, and Gelsolin (GSN), while downregulated proteins included Serum albumin (ALB) and Apolipoprotein A1 (APOA1). These proteins are linked to inflammation and salivary secretions largely.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h3>
<p id="Par4">For the first time, we present unique saliva sEVs protein signatures associated with CAD. Validation in larger cohorts may establish Cystatin S as a potential diagnostic biomarker for CAD.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Graphical Abstract</h3>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=12505727_12014_2025_9541_Figa_HTML.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/59a2/12505727/a74018d64b9c/12014_2025_9541_Figa_HTML.jpg" loading="lazy" id="MO6" height="944" width="708" alt="graphic file with name 12014_2025_9541_Figa_HTML.jpg"></a></p></section><section id="sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h3>
<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12014-025-09541-9.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Coronary artery disease, Exosome, Small extracellular vesicles, Saliva, Biomarker, Cystatin</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par6">Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the leading cause of mortality worldwide, including in India. It is a chronic inflammatory disease with a long asymptomatic phase characterized by the development of fatty fibrous deposits in the arteries, leading to angina, myocardial infarction, and death [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>]. According to the World Health Organization, four out of every five patients die from myocardial infarction every year, and India accounts for one-fifth of global deaths due to heart disease [<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>]. Indians are known to have the highest CAD rates, and conventional risk factors do not fully explain the increased risk [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>]. CAD problems can onset at an early age, and its rapid progression is commonly observed in younger age groups. CAD is caused by the accumulation of fatty-fibrous deposits, including calcium and other substances, inside the innermost layer of the arteries, or intima. Over time, the arteries get blocked, restricting blood flow and oxygen to the heart. This leads to conditions such as acute coronary syndrome-myocardial infarction (ACS-MI), heart attack, and mortality [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>, <a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>]. Hypertension, smoking, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, physical inactivity, age, and poor diet are the risk factors. Several cardiac biomarkers are commonly used for various heart-related conditions, including high-sensitive C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), Creatine Phosphokinase, high-sensitive Cardiac Troponin I, B-type Natriuretic Peptide, Procalcitonin, Albumin, Globulin, Cystatin C, Lipoprotein-associated phospholipase 2, and Apolipoproteins [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>, <a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>]. Further testing is needed to assess their clinical utility and understand the pathophysiology. The prognosis and diagnosis of early fatty-fibrous lesions in arteries in asymptomatic individuals are essential for the detection, treatment, and prevention of progression, as well as for better management of patients at an early stage before it becomes severe [<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>, <a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>].</p>
<p id="Par7">Several studies have examined the association between plasma proteins (without isolating exosomes) and cardiovascular death in patients with chronic coronary heart disease. One notable retrospective study [<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>] investigated the relationship between levels of multiple proteins and mortality over a follow-up period of 3–12 years in individuals with chronic CAD. The researchers utilized the OLINK Proximity Extension Assay, identifying 18 protein biomarkers linked to cardiovascular-related deaths. Another study analyzed plasma samples from patients with CAD using quantitative proteomics. This analysis involved selected and multiple reaction monitoring techniques, using samples from 18 patients and controls in the discovery cohort [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>]. The study observed proteins apolipoprotein C-II and chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 7 were associated with the severity of CAD. Elevated levels of vinculin were reported in a quantitative plasma profiling study focused on the manifestations of atherosclerosis [<a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>]. Another study compared plasma proteomics from 20 acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients with 10 healthy controls. It identified glutamic oxaloacetic transaminases as potential markers alongside selected protein signatures such as creatine kinase-MB, myoglobin, and both cytoplasmic and mitochondrial aspartate aminotransferases [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>]. Similarly, a 2D-DIGE gel-based technique revealed haptoglobin as a biomarker for AMI in a sample of 30 patients [<a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>]. Serum proteomics profiling has been studied to predict CAD in symptomatic coronary angiography and another on premature carotid atherosclerosis [<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>, <a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>].</p>
<p id="Par8">Human saliva is a valuable diagnostic medium for clinical applications due to its accessibility and non-invasiveness [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. Saliva offers several advantages for biomarker discovery compared to blood and CSF, as it can be easily collected in large amounts without risk to patients. Saliva contains 99% water and 1% other substances including electrolytes, mucus, white blood cells, antimicrobial agents, and extracellular vesicles. Salivary exosomes or small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) play a significant role in cell-to-cell communication and transport of proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>, <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>]. In CAD, these exosomes, abundantly released by various cells, especially endothelial cells, are excreted in saliva through salivary glands. Saliva exosomes carry targeted disease-related information and stable biomarkers, resistant to proteolytic degradations, and contain relevant proteins for diagnostic purposes [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>].</p>
<p id="Par9">To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to report DEPs signatures from saliva exosomes in CAD compared to healthy controls. The comparative proteomic profiling of clinically diagnosed CAD patients and healthy controls using high throughput mass spectrometry will reveal changes in the expression levels of proteins from saliva sEVs. We believe that saliva sEVs have the potential to serve as a non-invasive liquid biopsy for diagnosing CAD at an early stage. The DEPs in CAD will help as a diagnostic biomarker and contribute to understanding the pathophysiology, progression, and therapeutics [<a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>, <a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>–<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>]. Therefore, our study aims to identify the protein signature in saliva sEVs by performing high throughput label-free LC–MS/MS mass spectrometry. Currently, there are no published studies that emphasize the role of saliva sEVs in CAD. The saliva sEVs hold the potential for acting as a non-invasive liquid biopsy for CAD diagnosis. We have discussed the role of differentially identified proteins and signaling pathways.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Material and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Research subjects</h3>
<p id="Par10">Our study was approved with reference number IEC/425/07.06.19, RP-21/2019 by the Institutional Ethics Committee at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, New Delhi, India. The guidelines of ICH-GCP (International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use—Good Clinical Practice) were followed. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants following the Helsinki Declaration. Our study was designed to focus on identifying early protein signature biomarkers for CAD diagnosis from saliva-sEVs proteomics compared to healthy controls. To achieve this, we included a total of forty participants with diagnosed CAD (n = 20) and healthy controls (n = 20) matched for age and gender.</p>
<p id="Par11">Saliva samples were collected from patients diagnosed by the physician having CAD and healthy controls from 2019 to 2021. All recruited patients were evaluated through patient history, blood tests, Electrocardiogram (ECG), Computed Tomography (CT) or Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans, and physical examinations. The included patients were of (1) age: 18–65 years, (2) both male and female, (3) having Acute myocardial infarction (AMI)/ past MI, and CAD, documented as per Angiography (≥ 50% narrowing in at least one of the major coronary arteries), and (4) Positive stress test or cardiac MRI or CT angiography. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age less than 18 years or more than 65 years, (2) chronic inflammatory disease, (3) Carotid artery disease or stroke and peripheral artery disease, (4) malignancy, (5) non-atherosclerotic myocardial ischemia (coronary embolism or vasculitis), (6) patients with a previous history of heart, renal, or any other recent surgery in the past 3 months. Blood samples were collected for testing lipid profile at the time of saliva collection. During sample collection, we conducted a lipid profile test and monitored basic clinical parameters of blood pressure, weight, and height. We gathered data on hypertension, diabetes, alcohol consumption, statin drug use, as well as fasting lipid profile measurements such as high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), total cholesterol, and triglycerides. The methodology and results are summarized in a graphical abstract (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>).</p>

<p id="Par12">A standard operating procedure was followed for the collection and storage of unstimulated saliva from participants. We collected 2 mL of unstipulated saliva from each participant. The samples were collected preferably in the morning between 8:00 am and 12:00 pm. Participants were asked to refrain from eating, drinking, or smoking for at least one hour before saliva collection. After rinsing their mouths with deionized water, participants were instructed to sit upright in a chair and to avoid speaking or swallowing. They were then asked to allow saliva to collect on the floor of their mouths and to spit the liquid into a sterile tube that was kept on ice. A minimum of 2 mL of saliva was collected from each participant. The salivary flow rate was recorded by measuring the volume of saliva collected and the time taken for the collection. Only clear saliva samples of good quality with a pH greater than 6.0 were used. Participants with oral infections, autoimmune disorders, or oral malignancies were excluded from saliva collection.</p>
<p id="Par13">After sequential centrifugation at 2000<em>g</em> for 10 min and 15,000<em>g</em> for 10 min at 4 °C, 1 mL clear supernatant was filtered through a pre-sterilized PES membrane syringe 0.22 µm filter. Collected filtrate into ultra-clear tubes for ultracentrifugation at 120,000<em>g</em> for 2 h at 4 °C using Optima Max Instrument fixed angle rotors (Beckman Coulter, Thermo Scientific). The upper solution was discarded, leaving the 100-150ul at the bottom as micro-vesicles. The isolated sEVs were further ultracentrifuged using swing rotors with sucrose density gradient steps at 110,000<em>g</em> for 2 h at 4 °C. The saliva-sEVs thus collected were further utilized for their characterization and proteomics.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">1D SDS PAGE and western blotting</h3>
<p id="Par14">Total protein was obtained using TCA-Acetone precipitation and protein was quantified using Pierce BCA Protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific). 20 µg of each sample were dissolved in 2× Laemmli sample buffer and 12% SDS-PAGE mini gel was run. The See Blue Plus2 pre-stained protein standard was used to track protein migration. The gels were run first at 60 V for 30 min and at 100 V for 120 min until the blue dye reached the end. The resulting gels were stained with Colloidal Coomassie Stain. Dry western blotting transfer was performed by transferring protein onto the polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (PVDF, from Invitrogen). A sandwich setup was created with an anode, filter paper, membrane, gel, filter paper, and cathode in the transfer buffer, and the process was run at 25 V for 25 min. The membrane was blocked using a 5% non-fat milk solution (Biobasic) in TBST buffer for 30 min on a rocking platform. The PVDF membranes were then probed separately with exosome marker primary antibodies mouse anti-human TSG101 (Abcam, # ab30871), mouse anti-human flotillin-1 (Abcam, # ab41927), anti-CD63 (Invitrogen, 10628D), control anti-GAPDH (ITT5052, G Biosciences), and atherosclerotic marker anti-APOA1 (ab64308, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking at dilutions ranging from 1:1000 to 1:2000. The membranes were washed three times in TBST for 10 min each. Appropriate secondary Anti-Rabbit and Anti-mouse IgG H&amp;L (ab205718, Abcam Cambridge, UK) were used. These antibodies were diluted from 1:5000 to 1:10,000 and allowed to incubate for one hour. The membranes were washed three times in TBST for 10 min each. The signal was detected and developed by exposure to Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) plus Western blotting detection reagents (Amersham Biosciences, USA). Images were captured using the Azure Systems 400 (Azure Biosystems, California, USA).</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)</h3>
<p id="Par15">The concentration and size distribution of saliva-sEVs were measured using Zeta View NTA (Particle Metrix software version 8.05). The samples were diluted 1:1000 fold in PBS, injected, and viewed under 11 different positions at temperature − 28 °C, pH 7.0 with a 405-nm laser. Camera settings ranged from 12 to 14 and the rest of the auto-settings in default mode.</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Zeta potential</h3>
<p id="Par16">The Malvern zeta sizer software was used for checking the stability of saliva-sEVs particles in suspension. 1 µL of isolated saliva sEVs sample was diluted in 1 mL of distilled water and analyzed using NanoSightNTA software.</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM)</h3>
<p id="Par17">The size and morphology of saliva-sEVs were assessed using TEM and SEM. The 10 uL from freshly isolated saliva-sEVs was fixed, washed, and incubated with formvar-coated copper grids (200 mesh size) for 5 min. The samples were viewed under the Tecnai G2-20 microscope (Fei company, The Netherlands). A 100 uL solution of the samples diluted in distilled water was mounted on a glass cover slip and dried under a desiccator. Sputter coating with gold particles was applied to the samples. The samples were viewed under the SEM Zeiss Evo-18 vs 5.09 instrument and analyzed using SMART-SEM software.</p></section><section id="Sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Label-free protein quantitation using mass spectrometry</h3>
<p id="Par18">Label-free quantitation of mass spectrometry protein analysis was conducted in pooled triplicate groups and included technical repeats. Protein samples were dissolved in lysis buffer (8 M Urea, 3 M Thiourea, and 4% CHAPS) and added 1X protease inhibitor cocktail (Promega). Equal amounts of protein (200ug each) from pooled sEVs samples were diluted in 50 mM Ammonium bicarbonate (pH ≥ 8) with urea concentration ≤ 1 M. The proteins were reduced with 20 mM DTT (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature and alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The trypsin (Promega) to protein ratio was 1:20, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. The enzymatic reaction was stopped using a final concentration of 1% Formic acid in samples. Finally, the samples were desalted using Pierce’s Peptide Desalting Spin Columns (Thermo Scientific). The samples were dried under SpeedVac and stored at -20 °C until LC–MS/MS.</p>
<p id="Par19">We utilized an Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometry platform (Thermo Scientific) for analyzing peptide fractions with precision. Sample peptides were introduced using an Easy nano-LC 1000 system (Thermo Scientific) and a meticulous loading process onto trapping and analytical columns. The column was heated to 45 °C using an Agile SLEEVE column oven. Elution was performed for 125 min with non-linear gradients of mobile phase A (water and 0.1% formic acid) to mobile phase B (acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 250 nL/minute, followed by a 15-min wash reaching 99% mobile phase B. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent mode with the number of dependent scans 20 with multi-notch synchronous precursor selection MS3. The LC–MS/MS analysis was performed in biological triplicates and technical repeats. Data analysis was performed using Proteome Discoverer software version 3.0 and Metaboanalyst 6.0. MS2 spectra for Ion trap-detected higher energy collisional dissociation (HCD) spectra were searched using SequestHT against the Uniprot human proteome database appended to the list of common contaminants. The parameters were set as enzyme trypsin, precursor mass tolerance 10 ppm, fragment mass tolerance 0.6 Da, 2 missed cleavage allowed, peptide length 6–144 amino acids, static modification Carbamidomethyl + 57.021 Da (C), oxidation of methionine. Regression settings with non-linear regression and course parameter tuning were used. Decoy proteins and contaminant proteins were removed from the list of all the proteins identified before further analysis.</p></section><section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Bioinformatics and enrichment analysis for the identified proteins</h3>
<p id="Par20">Gene functional enrichment analysis, gene ontology (GO), and pathways were obtained for saliva-sEV DEPs after identification. The terms corresponding to biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) were analyzed using the web-accessible program DAVID (The Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery; <a href="https://david.ncifcrf.gov/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://david.ncifcrf.gov/</a>) [<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>]. Additionally, pathways enrichment from Shiny GO online platform; <a href="http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/</a> [<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>] was carried out. We compiled the main pathways related to CAD together and obtained pathways information from KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) [<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>]. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed for proteins that were ≥ twofold up-regulated and down-regulated using STRING version 11.5; (<a href="https://string-db.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://string-db.org</a>)) with a significance level of <em>p</em>-value &lt; 0.001.</p></section><section id="Sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="Par21">Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8 Software (San Diego, CA). Descriptive statistics were used to compare CAD patients and healthy control groups based on demographics, age, gender, blood pressure, body mass index (BMI), smoking, obesity, and fasting glucose levels. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess variable distribution. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using the student <em>t</em>-test for independent variables. Non-normally distributed data was represented as median and interquartile range. For the baseline data of patients, the classified variables were displayed in numbers and presented as percentages. Correlations were determined using the Spearman-Rho test, and logistic regression analysis was performed to find associations among variables. Mixed effect models were used to analyze the significance of identified proteins. A statistical significance level of <em>p</em>-value &lt; 0.05 was considered for all tests.</p></section></section><section id="Sec12"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="Sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Characteristics of participants</h3>
<p id="Par22">Patients recently diagnosed with CAD (n = 20) and healthy controls (n = 20) aged 18–65 years were included. The mean age of CAD patients was 47.6 ± 9.3 years, and age-gender matched control group (47.1 ± 9.2 years; Pearson correlation coefficient (r) = 0.98, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001) had 75% males in both groups. The patients with CAD had various conditions such as myocardial infarctions (MI) affecting the coronary arteries, including anterior wall myocardial infarctions (AWMI), left anterior descending artery (LAD) involvement, and single, double, or triple vessel disease, with moderate to severe left ventricle dysfunction. Further, these patients had opted for surgical or medical treatment based on their conditions. Study patients were inclusive of risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, and smoking, as previously described [<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>]. The baseline characteristics of the patients are presented in Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>.</p>

<p id="Par23">Two samples each from patient and healthy control saliva-sEVs were used to standardize NTA, western blotting, and electron microscopy experiments. After isolating saliva-sEVs, we used NTA to determine their size distribution and concentration [<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>]. We found that patients had a comparatively greater concentration of sEVs than controls, with an average of 3.1 × 10<sup>10</sup> particles/mL for patients and 1.3 × 10<sup>10</sup> particles/mL for controls. The mean diameter of the patient’s sEVs was 166 ± 62 nm, while the mean diameter of control sEVs was 157 ± 80 nm. The healthy control and patient characterization results are represented in Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>a (i &amp; ii, respectively). In Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>b, we depicted the apparent zeta potential for control saliva sEVs as + 34 ± 3.3 mV. The sEVs demonstrated initial stability along with suitable dispersion capabilities. They had rapid coagulation or flocculation-like properties. We used TEM and SEM (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>c) to characterize the shape, size, and overall morphology of the saliva-sEVs, which corroborated the presence of sEVs with diameters ranging from 50 to 130 nm. We observed round-shaped morphology in SEM for control samples, while CAD patients showed mixed shapes and morphology [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>]. The sizes in NTA were observed to be larger than that of TEM due to the method being fundamentally based on the Brownian motion of analyte particles and inclusive of solvation shells. Further, the saliva-sEVs were characterized using western blotting showing the presence of exosome markers TSG 101, Flotillin 1, and CD63 (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>d)]. Also, the presence of APOA1 has been observed in both patients and controls. Taken together, these results confirm the isolation of sEVs from saliva samples.</p>

<p id="Par24">After characterizing the sample, we isolated proteins from saliva-derived sEVs. Upon quantification, we found that 2 mL of saliva collected from patients can yield approximately 0.3 mL of usable saliva-sEVs after undergoing density gradient centrifugation processing. This can result in an estimated protein yield of 0.3–1 mg, as determined using the BCA protein assay kit and Nanodrop instrument. The SDS-PAGE gel observed differences in protein bands, as shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>e.</p>
<p id="Par25">We have identified a total of 506 protein entries from saliva-sEVs of CAD patients and healthy controls using label-free quantitation LC–MS/MS. These proteins were identified with a false discovery rate (FDR &lt; 1%) at 95% confidence. Although identified in three biological repeats, 131 proteins did not present any abundance-based data in CAD and control. Using the Scaling Mode parameter after normalization of grouped protein samples from the Reporter Ions Quantifier node and the Precursor Ions Quantifier node. After the normalization of samples, the nodes scale the abundance values of each sample so that the average of all samples is 100. Then, it calculates all samples up or down relative to 100. The scaling mode parameter values for 123 proteins (grouped samples) and 136 proteins (grouped controls) were observed to have abundances in either of these. But it is not useful for differential expression analysis and may not represent the actual protein expression levels and scaled for a range. We calculated the fold change between control and sample groups after normalization for differential expression. 116 such proteins were observed to fall under this criterion, showing expression data in both the samples and controls. Figure <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>a represents the volcano plot for all the proteins identified, indicating significantly up/down-regulated proteins in CAD. A total of 18 significant DEPs were identified between the sample and controls with ≥ 2 folds, high Sequest HT scores, peptide match &gt; 1, coverage &gt; 10%, and abundance ratio variability of sample and control &gt; 10% (Table <a href="#Tab2" class="usa-link">2</a>). The Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) differentiates between the healthy control groups and CAD groups based on their protein profiles. These form two separate groups (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>b). Grouped samples can be assessed using the groups. The DEPs in the form of a heat map are presented in Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>c. The protein abundances were normalized and scaled for clustering parameters. Euclidean distance function and average linking methods were used. The color scale is indicative of expressions based on their z-scores.</p>

<p id="Par26">Functional annotations of CAD sEVs were analyzed for 116 identified proteins, showing abundances in CAD and healthy controls. We were able to obtain results for 109 proteins associated with GO terms with statistically significant (<em>p</em>-value &lt; 0.01). The enrichment analysis of CC revealed that approximately 80% of proteins were localized as extracellular regions released as small vesicles, and rest from membrane, cytoplasm, and other regions. (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>d). BP included proteins involved in mostly immunity, complement coagulation cascade, inflammation, oxygen transport, glycolysis, and oxidative stress. The most enriched terms of MF corresponding to approximately 80% were involved in enzymatic activity such as protease inhibitor, hydrolase, oxidoreductase, and peroxidases. The rest few were involved in antioxidant and antibiotic responses.</p></section><section id="Sec17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">PPI network and pathway enrichment analysis of DEPs</h3>
<p id="Par27">Among 116 identified proteins showing CAD and healthy control group abundances were combined after normalization and checked using the PPI network. We identified results for ninety such proteins that could significantly link to other proteins within identified proteins (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>e, f). Specifically, we focused on 18 proteins that exhibited notable upregulation or downregulation in CAD patients compared to healthy controls. A PPI network map was generated and analyzed using k-means clustering, resulting in three distinct clusters. Notably, the Cystatin proteins formed a small, separate cluster. In contrast, another cluster comprised nine proteins, including ACTB, GSN, APOA1, ALB, LYZ, PRTN3, S100A8, H4C6, and JCHAIN, which were found to interact significantly with each other (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.001). AMY1A and WFDC2 did not present any interactions within the identified proteins. Furthermore, Cystatin S was consistently found to be upregulated in CAD saliva sEVs, as confirmed through all three biological replicates in the mass spectrometry analysis. The mass spectrometry spectra and abundance levels are illustrated in Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>g. Further, refer to Supplementary Data Figures S1–S4. We used the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 online platform to reanalyze the MS spectra files [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>]. Figure <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>g represents the most significant protein-normalized concentrations for CAD in their relative abundance for proteins APOA1, Protein S100, Alpha amylase, gelsolin, Cystatin SA and Cystatin S. From pathway enrichment analysis, we observed that proteins were primarily involved in key pathways, including salivary secretions, pentose phosphate pathway, complement coagulation cascade, cellular mechanism and biosynthesis, cholesterol metabolism, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, platelet activation, fluid shear stress and atherosclerosis, lipid and atherosclerosis pathways (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>). Each of these pathways contained at least four proteins exhibiting notable abundances.</p>

<p id="Par29">Salivary sEVs are crucial for intercellular communication transporting proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids between cells. This transport can influence disease progression. Understanding the mechanisms and structure of exosomes is vital for their use as biomarkers in disease diagnosis, drug delivery, and therapy. Exosomes from diseased cells differ in shape, size, and content compared to those from healthy cells, reflecting changes in cell behavior and physiology. The biochemical content of sEVs varies based on disease pathophysiology, allowing them to be used as potential biomarkers. Saliva-sEVs may be an excellent source of biomarkers that can help diagnose diseases such as CAD. As far as we know, our study is the first to identify biomarkers from sEVs in the saliva of CAD patients. We have characterized the isolated sEVs using electron microscopy techniques. We have highlighted the structural morphological difference in CAD patients as observed by TEM. SEM provided three-dimensional surface topography of sEVs. We obtained the molecular level evidence for the presence of exosomes using Western blotting. Overall distributions of saliva sEVs have been observed using NTA. The stability in distilled water and dispersion were evaluated using zeta potential, indicating that saliva sEVs exhibit rapid coagulation or flocculation-like characteristics.</p>
<p id="Par30">We examined the levels of various proteins in the saliva sEVs of CAD patients, including upregulation of Phosphoglycerate mutase, Cystatin-S (CST1/ CST2/ CST4), Actin, cytoplasmic 1 (ACTB), Histone H4 (HIST1H4L), WAP four-disulfide core domain protein 2 (WFDC2), Lipocalin 1 (Tear Prealbumin; LCN1), Protein S100 and its subtype S100-A8, Immunoglobulin J chain (JCHAIN), Immunoglobulin heavy constant alpha 2 (IGHA2), Gelsolin (GSN), Alpha-amylase (AMY1A), Myeloblastin (PRTN3), and Lysozyme C (LYZ). Compared to healthy controls, Serum albumin (ALB) and Apolipoprotein A1 (APOA1) were found to be downregulated. These findings suggest that saliva sEVs have the potential to serve as early biomarkers for CAD, providing direct disease-state protein information to aid in understanding disease progression and pathophysiology. We specifically focused on younger participants to identify a protein signature as an early biomarker for CAD diagnosis.</p>
<p id="Par31">Some of the proteins we identified are commonly used in blood tests for CAD, while others may offer new insights into early CAD diagnosis. Phosphoglycerate dismutase (PGM) is a glycolytic enzyme that converts 3-phosphoglycerate to 2-phosphoglycerate. It plays a role in the pathways of glycolysis and gluconeogenesis. It is involved in the metabolism of glucose and 2,3-phosphoglycerate. PGAM2 overexpression was reported in the serum of 153 patients with heart failure using the ELISA assay technique [<a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>].</p>
<p id="Par32">Cystatins are cysteine protease inhibitors expressed in numerous cell types that regulate several physiological processes. Cystatin forms mainly including A, B, C, S, and M) are extensively studied. CST1, CST2, and CST4 are thiol protease inhibitors found abundant in CAD patient sEVs. These are mostly expressed in saliva and tears. In clinical practice, serum cystatin C level is an established biomarker for kidney function (filtration) other than serum creatinine level [<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a>]. In a few studies, elevated serum Cystatin levels were observed to be associated with the development of cardiovascular event or mortality [<a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>]. One study has evaluated the association between Cystatin C and coronary plaque phenotype in patients with preserved renal function (eGFR &gt; 60 ml/min/1.73 m<sup>2</sup>) [<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>]. Several epidemiological studies have consistently documented the positive association between serum Cystatin C level and atherosclerotic disease [<a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>–<a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>]. Elevated levels of cystatin C have been associated with proteolytic, vascular remodelling, and endothelial dysfunction. No data has been studied and reported on Cystatin S playing a significant role in CAD or any other disease (Supplementary Data Figure S10). We performed multiple sequence alignments of all the Cystatin subtypes. Cystatin S had the highest sequence coverage and significant abundance in the sample with high sequest score values in all three repeats. However, cystatin C and D were identified from our samples but with low coverage of approximately 10% and low sequence HT scores, one peptide match with a fivefold increase in samples. Cystatin S or SAP1 is a human salivary protein and an inhibitor of several cysteine proteinases and it has high similarity to Cystatin C obtained as differentially expressed from saliva-sEVs in CAD. The obtained elevated levels of Cystatin S, SA, and SN were 6.4, 4.8, and 2 folds in CAD compared to healthy controls, respectively. Gelsolin is a calcium-regulated, actin-modulating protein that binds actin and prevents its exchange by cutting and sealing the actin filament [<a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a>]. This helps regulate the cytoskeleton and plays a role in cell movement, apoptosis, and phagocytosis. This is widely expressed in heart tissues and immune cells. Recent evidence supports that gelsolin is related to atherosclerosis, involving lipid metabolism, inflammation, cell proliferation, migration, and thrombosis [<a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a>, <a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>]. This study has suggested an increased levels in the heart after myocardial infarction and heart failure.</p>
<p id="Par33">Lipocalin 1, also known as Tear Prealbumin protein, belongs to the lipocalin family. This protein is abundant in saliva and tears and functions as an extracellular binding protein. We observed the upregulated expressions of this protein. Similar lipocalin levels have been reported in CAD patients compared to healthy controls, and the levels were increased with disease severity [<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a>]. It plays a role in inflammation. WAP four-disulfide core domain protein, also known as Human Epididymis Protein 4, is a protease inhibitor found in saliva sEVs. A study conducted on the mouse model reported the role of the WAP protein in cardiomyopathy and observed elevated levels of this protein in 87 patients tested [<a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a>]. Another study conducted on this protein from 567 plasma samples of COACH (Coordinating study evaluating outcomes of Advising and Counseling in Heart failure) study observed doubled levels of this protein with HR 1.46 (95% CI, 1.23–1.72, <em>p</em>-value &lt; 0.001) adjusted for risk factors [<a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a>]. Myeloblastin, also known as proteinase 3 is a serine protease inhibitor involved in inflammation. In its active form, it generates superoxide radicals. Previous studies have suggested a protective and anti-inflammatory role in pathologies characterised by infiltration and involvement of T-cells [<a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">39</a>, <a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">40</a>].</p>
<p id="Par34">Lysozyme C, is a proteolytic enzyme found in saliva, tears, and other body fluids that generally helps protect against bacterial invasions. Elevated Lysozyme C levels have been observed in 197 CAD patient’s plasma with various degrees of disease severity and saliva-sEVs (this study) [<a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">41</a>]. The authors have reported the levels using a cut-off value of 1.5 µg/mL, with 86% sensitivity and 93% specificity. Logistic regression analyses were used to test the association of lysozyme C with 250 CAD patients and 250 non-CAD patients. The analyses observed a significant association with CAD independent of C-reactive protein, a known clinical inflammatory biomarker [<a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">42</a>]. Alpha amylase, an enzyme, is found to cause hyperamylasaemia beyond its upper limit (the normal range is typically 30–110 U/L). However, some studies have reported elevated amylase in association with heart failure and other conditions from blood and saliva, but there is no report of CAD [<a href="#CR43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR43">43</a>, <a href="#CR44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR44">44</a>]. Protein S100 is a calcium and zinc-binding protein that plays a prominent role in the regulation of inflammatory processes and immune response. Elevated S00A1 is one of the risk factors of ST-segment elevation, found associated with cardiac function after myocardial infarction [<a href="#CR45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR45">45</a>]. In PROVE IT-TIMI 22 trial the risk of recurrent cardiovascular events increased with an increase in plasma levels of proteins S100A8/A9 [<a href="#CR46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR46">46</a>]. This protein is suggested to play a role in leukocyte activation and is reported associated with thrombosis in acute coronary syndrome. These proteins are largely expressed in inflammatory and autoimmune states, mainly by myeloid cells and vascular cells involved in Toll-like receptor kinase and the receptor for advanced glycation end products. The protein S100A1 was suggested to have elevated plasma levels in patients having acute myocardial infarctions [<a href="#CR45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR45">45</a>, <a href="#CR47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR47">47</a>]. Other proteins such as immunoglobulin heavy constant alpha 2, also known as immunoglobulin A (IgA) heavy chain an antibody that plays an important role in the immune system’s function. Immune system-related cells such as macrophages, mast cells, and T-lymphocytes are associated with atheromatous plaque [<a href="#CR48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR48">48</a>]. Mast cell-activated macrophages lead to an increase in T-cell activations. T-cells mediate mast-cell activation, and proliferation to regulate macrophage activity. Secreted components from these cells such as histamine, cytokines, and chemokines induce coronary artery spasm and acute coronary syndrome [<a href="#CR49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR49">49</a>].</p>
<p id="Par35">We observed the downregulated levels of proteins, serum albumin, and apolipoprotein A1. Serum albumin is the major protein present in plasma. It is highly involved in the binding and transporting calcium, magnesium, zinc, fatty acids, and drugs [<a href="#CR50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR50">50</a>]. It has anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, anti-coagulation, and anti-platelet coagulation activity in cardiovascular disease. The low serum albumin levels are found to be associated with increased risk of cardiac events in CAD [<a href="#CR51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR51">51</a>]. Apolipoprotein A1 protein is involved in the reverse transporting of cholesterol from tissue to the liver for excretion. Low levels of this protein are reported as a risk factor for CAD [<a href="#CR52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR52">52</a>, <a href="#CR53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR53">53</a>]. Low levels of Apo A1 may indicate that the body is not clearing excess cholesterol efficiently. The study discusses the importance of this protein as a marker of early CAD as well as coronary artery obstruction. Apoliporotein A1 and serum albumin proteins are involved in HDL remodeling and glycation. Glycation modifies proteins and lipoproteins in the arterial wall in diabetic patients, accelerating atherosclerosis development [<a href="#CR54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR54">54</a>, <a href="#CR55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR55">55</a>]. However, the association between apolipoproteins and CAD remains controversial and has been relatively underexplored in healthy participants [<a href="#CR56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR56">56</a>].</p>
<p id="Par36">Atherosclerosis and CAD are inflammatory diseases having extensive arterial wall matrix protein degradations. Whereas the cysteine protease cathepsins play a pivotal role in the remodeling of the extracellular matrix and are implicated in plaque development and progression [<a href="#CR57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR57">57</a>, <a href="#CR58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR58">58</a>]. Figure <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a> represents all crucial pathways highlighting the dysregulated protein in the form of their gene IDs identified in CAD patients, pathways such as lipid and atherosclerosis, complement and coagulation, IL-17 signaling pathway, salivary secretions, platelet activation, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation. These proteins in the form of diagnosis and/or prognosis biomarkers were tested in blood and tissue samples from CAD patients. We have identified elevated levels of Cystatin S as a novel marker from non-invasive saliva-sEVs of CAD. This protein is distributed in saliva through salivary glands and plays roles in regulating carbohydrate digestion and absorption in salivary secretions (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>). Evidence and similarity with subtype protein Cystatin C of Cystatin S can be potential biomarkers in early diagnosis. We have significantly identified the elevated levels of this protein repeatedly. This may inhibit the elastase activity, an enzyme involved in the degradation of protein elastin that provides elasticity to the arteries [<a href="#CR59" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR59">59</a>]. It is also involved in the modulation of lipid metabolism. Cystatin may regulate the inflammation in arteries by inhibiting the activity of cysteine proteases, involved in inflammatory response. The imbalance of cysteine protease and its inhibitor cystatin C may favor the proteolysis of the matrix and promote disease pathogenesis. However, the exact role of Cystatin S in the pathophysiology and progression of CAD is unknown and needs to be elucidated further. Based on the cystatin C role majorly reported and associated from blood samples of atherosclerosis. It provides us with the confidence to work on the Cystatin S from saliva sEVs as a potential early diagnosis biomarker for CAD. Cystatin S in saliva sEVs may have greater diagnostic potential than Cystatin C in blood, being more specific to CAD, whereas Cystatin C in blood may be influenced by multiple factors, such as kidney function and inflammation. Salivary sEVs may be more sensitive to changes in CAD, allowing for earlier detection and diagnosis. Compared to healthy controls, the elevated levels of saliva sEVs Cystatin S protein found in CAD patients can potentially become a diagnostic non-invasive biomarker. On significant testing in larger samples, it may be used to screen the individual likelihood of having CAD future risk at an early stage, from routine non-invasive clinical check-ups from saliva sEVs.</p>
<p id="Par37">Ours is the preliminary study conducted for the identification of protein signatures from saliva sEVs for early diagnostic biomarker identification in patients with CAD. The major strength and limitation lie in the methodology used for the isolation of sEVs. We have tested the same from different biofluids [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>]. We have presented the most significant proteins after label-free quantitation high throughput mass spectrometry. However, it does come with some technological limitations compared to the new-generation tandem mass spectrometry [<a href="#CR60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR60">60</a>–<a href="#CR62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR62">62</a>]. While it can detect proteins at low nanogram levels, it may not be as sensitive as newer tandem mass spectrometry instruments. It has a limited dynamic range, making it challenging to quantify a high abundance of protein differences. This may impact the overall number of peptides that can be identified and quantified [<a href="#CR61" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR61">61</a>]. The protein signature presented needs to be validated further. The cohort studies and clinical trials to verify the potential use of DEPs in CAD may be more specific for risk factors and particular disease conditions in future studies.</p></section><section id="Sec19"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="Par38">Saliva sEVs hold significant potential for the early diagnosis and monitoring of diseases. They offer several advantages over traditional blood markers, making them a viable alternative for disease detection. These benefits include being non-invasive, easy to collect, and cost-effective. Its ability to facilitate early diagnosis is crucial in conditions where timely intervention can significantly affect outcomes. In comparison to blood samples, sEVs show reduced pre-analytical variability as they are less susceptible to issues like hemolysis and clotting. This stability enhances diagnostic accuracy while keeping expenses manageable, positioning saliva sEVs as a valuable tool in clinical practice. However, some expertise with sEVs isolation will be required.</p>
<p id="Par39">Our study analyzed the proteomic characteristics of saliva sEVs in patients with CAD. We discussed the DEPs identified and their potential mechanisms related to the disease pathways. Among all the DEPs, Cystatin S may serve as a novel biomarker for the early identification and diagnosis of CAD and may also act as a potential therapeutic target. The saliva sEVs elevated level of this proteolytic protein may be involved in inflammatory response, vascular remodeling and endothelial dysfunction pathways. However, the precise role of Cystatin S in the pathophysiology and progression of CAD requires further investigation. Our study provides valuable insights for the early and accurate identification of CAD using non-invasive sEVs. Our groundwork will support future translational studies focused on non-invasive early diagnosis and risk assessment, facilitating improved management of CAD. Additionally, longitudinal monitoring may provide dynamic insights into the progression of CAD.</p></section><section id="Sec20"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Competing interests</h2>
