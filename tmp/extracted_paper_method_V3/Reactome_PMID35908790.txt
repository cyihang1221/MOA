>Materials and Methods   Animals and sample collection   All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the University of Connecticut Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (A13-059).   Animal procedures have been previously described ( Pillai et al., 2017 ). Briefly, multiparous Western White-faced ewes ( N  = 78) were estrus synchronized using a controlled intravaginal drug release device (Easi-Breed CIDR Sheep Insert, Zoetis, Inc., Parsippany, NJ) and PGF 2α  (Lutalyse, 5 mg/mL; Zoetis Inc.;  Knights et al., 2001 ). Ewes were bred by one of four related Dorset rams. Day 0 of pregnancy was determined by a rump mark with no following remarking. Ewes were moved to 3 × 1 m individual pens at day 20 of gestation and transitioned onto a complete pelleted feed based on National Research Council requirements (100% NRC) over a 7-d period. Ewes were confirmed pregnant via transabdominal ultrasound on day 28.5 ± 0.4 ( Jones et al., 2016 ) and were assigned to a diet and time point of necropsy. Pregnant ewes were fed a restricted-fed (RES; 60% NRC;  n  = 27), control-fed (CON; 100% NRC;  n  = 25), or over-fed diet (OVER; 140% NRC;  n  = 26) based on the NRC requirements for TDN ( National Research Council, 2007 ) starting on day 30.2 ± 0.2 of gestation. A subset of ewes was euthanized at days 45, 90, or 135 of gestation (corresponding with primary myogenesis, secondary myogenesis, and hypertrophy, respectively) by i.v. injection of Beuthanasia-D Special (Merck Animal Health, Summit, NJ). Fetuses were removed, weighed, and fetal tissues were collected. The remaining ewes were allowed to undergo parturition (birth;  n  = 5 to 7 per dietary treatment). Lambs at birth were allowed to nurse for up to 24 h before euthanasia to ensure adequate colostrum intake. Within 24 h of parturition, lambs were weighed and euthanized as described earlier for tissue collection. For the experiments described in this manuscript, a portion of the LM from days 90, 135, and birth was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for proteomics analysis. The ewe number, fetal sex, and litter size distribution have been extensively described in  Pillai et al. (2017) . In brief, this study used 20 ewes at day 90 of gestation (CON = 7, RES = 7, OVER = 6), 19 ewes at day 135 (CON = 6, RES = 6, OVER = 7), and 18 ewes at birth (CON = 5, RES = 7, OVER = 6). At day 90, there were 39 total fetuses (CON = 14, RES = 14, OVER = 11), at day 135, there were 34 total fetuses (CON = 11, RES = 12, OVER = 11), and at birth, there were 35 total offspring (CON = 10, RES = 13, OVER = 12). Because fetal sex was not able to be accurately predicted, the ratio of males to females was not evenly distributed in any treatment within any time point ( Pillai et al., 2017 ). Six singleton fetuses (two per treatment) were collected at day 90 of gestation, four singleton fetuses were collected at day 135 of gestation (CON = 1, RES = 1, OVER = 2), and two singleton offspring were collected at birth (CON = 1, OVER = 1). Fetuses from five triplet litters were collected at day 90 (CON = 2, RES = 2, OVER = 1), from two triplet litters at day 135 (CON = 1, RES = 1), and offspring from four litters were collected at birth (CON = 2, RES = 1, OVER = 1). The remaining litters were all twin litters. Sample preparation   Frozen LM samples (approximately 250 mg) were homogenized using the bead beating technique (TissueLyzer, Qiagen) in protein extraction buffer (50 mM Tris HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0). Protein lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 ×  g  for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through sterile cheesecloth, Halt protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA) was added, and the protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteomic analysis   Sample preparation for multiplexed quantitative proteomic analysis   Purified protein samples were prepared using an adapted filter-aided sample preparation method as described previously ( Wisniewski et al., 2009 ). Briefly, aliquots of lysates (0.1 mg protein) were diluted to a final volume of 300 µL UA buffer (8 M urea, 0.1 M Tris HCl, pH 8.5) and subjected to Cys reduction using 25 mM dithiothreitol in UA buffer at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Afterward, each protein solution was added to a Microcon YM-10 MWCO filter device (MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO) that was preconditioned overnight with 10 mg/mL bovine serum albumin and subsequently washed with 200 μL aliquots of Fisher Optima LC/MS grade water via pipetting and centrifugation at 14,000 ×  g  at 20 °C for 40 min. All following centrifugation steps were performed applying the same centrifugal force. When Cys reduction was complete, the reduced proteins were concentrated using a 40 min centrifugation step, followed by resuspension in 200 μL of UA buffer. The resuspended proteins were mixed via trituration and the device was centrifuged for 40 min. Subsequently, 100 μL of 0.05 M iodoacetamide in UA buffer was added to the concentrate and mixed on a shaker at 37 °C for 15 min to allow carbamidomethylation on all Cys residues. The filters were then centrifuged for 30 min. The resulting concentrate was diluted with 100 μL UB buffer (8 M urea in 0.1 M triethylammonium bicarbonate [TEAB] pH 8.0) and centrifuged for 30 min. This step was repeated and then 40 μL UB buffer was added to resuspend the concentrate, mixed via trituration, and the entire solution was transferred to a new 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. The filter was washed twice more to remove any remaining proteins with 40 μL aliquots of 0.1 M TEAB and both solutions were added to the first UB buffer resuspension. An additional 10 μL of 0.1 M TEAB was added and the filter was placed into a new Microcon receiving tube and centrifuged at 1,000 ×  g  for 3 min. This final solution was pooled with the previous three solutions. The concentrates were subjected to proteolytic digestion by adding Pierce endoproteinase Lys-C (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 1:60 (wt:wt) enzyme:substrate and incubated on a thermal shaker plate at 37 °C for 16 h. The digests were then diluted with 0.1 M TEAB to a final urea concentration of less than 1 M. Sequencing grade, porcine-modified trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI) was added at 1:50 (wt:wt) enzyme:substrate concentration and incubated on a thermal shaker at 37 °C for 6.5 h. Digests were quenched by the addition of concentrated formic acid (Optima LC/MS grade, Fisher Scientific) to yield a final pH 2.5 and desalted on Pierce C18 Peptide Desalting spin columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P/N 89852) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The peptide content was determined by resuspending the desalted and fully dried peptides in 0.1% formic acid in water and then measuring the UV light spectral density at 280 nm using an extinction coefficient of 1.0 of 0.1% (g/L) solution. Tandem mass tag labeling   Tandem mass tag ( TMT ) labeling was conducted from a modified protocol as described previously ( Murphy et al., 2021 ). Desalted peptides were dried completely and then resuspended in 100 μL of 0.1 M TEAB and labeled using TMTsixplex isobaric label reagent kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturer’s instruction. The resulting mixture was desalted using Pierce peptide C18 desalting spin columns (P/N 89852), according to manufacturer’s instructions for TMT labeled peptides and dried using a speedvac concentrator. The labeled peptides were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid and a small aliquot from each reaction was analyzed by LC-MS/MS for labeling efficiency using the MaxQuant software package ( Cox and Mann, 2008 ]), as described previously ( Huang et al., 2017 ). All samples were verified to exceed 98% labeling efficiency. Equal amounts of TMTsixplex-labeled peptides from each of 6 individual proteomic samples were pooled to create 12 unique TMTsixplex sample groups using A280 absorbance by a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative proteomic analysis via liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry   All 12 TMTsixplex sample groups were dried, resuspended, and diluted to a total peptide concentration of 500 ng/µL using 0.1% formic acid in water and subsequently analyzed using an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano ultra-high performance liquid chromatography ( UPLC ) system coupled directly to a Q Exactive HF Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). From each sample group, 1 μL of the sample was injected onto a 25 cm PepMap RSLC C18 Easy-Spray analytical column (100 Å pore, 2 µm particle size, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated over a 300 min reversed-phase UPLC gradient at 300 nL/min flow rate. Peptides were directly eluted into the Q Exactive HF via positive mode electrospray ionization using a capillary voltage of 1.5 kV. A Top 15 data-dependent tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) acquisition method was used and included the following parameters for MS full scans: 120,000 resolution, 375 to 1,400 m/z scan range, 1e6 AGC target, 50 ms maximum ion injection time, and 1 microscan per spectrum. MS/MS scans included the following settings: 30,000 resolution, fixed first mass of 100 m/z, 1e5 AGC target, 100 ms maximum ion injection time, 0.7 m/z isolation window, charge state exclusion “on” for charge states of unassigned, +1, and >+8, normalized collision energy of 33, and 1 microscan per spectrum. Bioinformatics and statistical methods   Raw data files were uploaded into Proteome Discoverer (v2.2, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Sequest HT search engine was used to search raw files against the Uniprot  Ovis aries  reference proteome database (UP000002356, accessed 20180719) using the following parameters: trypsin enzyme specificity with 2 maximum missed cleavages, minimum and maximum peptide lengths of 6 and 144, respectively, static modifications of TMT6plex on peptide N-termini and Lys side chains plus carbamidomethylation on Cys, and dynamic modifications of oxidation on Met, deamidation on Asn and Gln, phosphorylation on Ser, Thr, and Tyr, plus protein N-terminal acetylation. Precursor and fragment mass tolerances of 10 ppm and 0.02 Da were used, respectively. All data were filtered to a strict 1% false discovery rate at the peptide spectrum matches, peptide, and protein levels. TMTsixplex-based quantitation included the following parameters: quantitative value corrections set to “true,” a co-isolation threshold of 50, total peptide amount normalization “on,” and protein ratio calculation was set to “Summed Abundance Based.” Equal mixing amounts within each TMT group were verified by monitoring the median abundance ratio for all conditions relative to the embedded control in each sample group. This median value was verified to be within 1.00 ± 0.03 across all 12 sample groups. Following removal of proteins with greater than 60% of missing values and imputation with the within treatment minimum, data were analyzed using ANOVA in R (version 4.0.5). Tukey post hoc analyses were used to determine the significance of differential protein abundance between treatments. Because of the relatively small sample size and imbalance of litter sizes and fetal sex, these were not included in the analyses. Significance was determined at  P  ≤ 0.05. Gene ontology categories were identified using  UniProt (2021)  and over-represented pathways were identified by Reactome ( https://reactome.org/ ). Results   As previously reported in  Pillai et al. (2017) , RES offspring weighed 18.4% less than CON and 13.1% less than OVER offspring. However, there were no differences detected in the loin-eye area at day 135 of gestation or birth ( Pillai et al., 2017 ). A total of 2,726 proteins were quantified by TMT-based quantitative mass spectrometry. Of those proteins, 312 were differentially expressed as a result of maternal diet ( Figure 1 ). Among the 89 differentially expressed proteins at day 90 of gestation, the top molecular functions included binding, catalytic activity, structural molecule activity, and molecular function regulator ( Figure 2A ). Top biological processes included cellular process, biological regulation, metabolic process, and localization ( Figure 2B ). Using Reactome, over-represented pathways were identified ( Table 1 ). The pathways with the most entities include metabolism of proteins, immune system, and cellular responses to stress/external stimuli. Differentially expressed proteins related to metabolism, development, the immune system, and cellular responses to stress are presented in  Table 2 . Expression of eukaryotic initiation factor ( EIF ) 2 subunit 3, EIF3L, and EIF4G2 were 15.5%, 5.8%, and 13.5%, respectively, less in OVER than CON fetuses ( P  < 0.05). Fetuses from OVER and RES ewes had 8.6% and 7.3% less EIF3B expression, respectively, than CON ( P  < 0.04). 26S proteasome non-ATPase regulatory subunit ( PSMD ) 1, PSMD6, and PSMD14 were 7.1%, 6.1%, and 8.8% less, respectively, in OVER fetuses than CON ( P  < 0.05), while RES fetuses were similar to CON. Expression of proteins involved in protein ubiquitination, including ubiquitin-like modifier-activating enzyme 6 (UBA6) and ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase FAF-X (USP9X) were 5.4% and 5.5% less, respectively, in OVER fetuses than RES fetuses ( P  < 0.04). Calpastatin (CAPNS1) protein expression was 11.8% less in OVER fetuses than CON ( P  = 0.03). Further, proteins involved in the mitogen-activated protein kinase ( MAPK ) signaling pathway were also altered in response to maternal diet at day 90 of gestation. Dual specificity mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (MAP2K1, or MEK1) was 10.6% greater in RES fetuses than CON ( P  = 0.03). Similarly, MAPK1 (ERK2) was 4.9% greater in RES fetuses than OVER fetuses ( P  = 0.04). Calmodulin-dependent calcineurin A subunit alpha isoform (PPP3CA, or calcineurin A) was increased 6.8% in fetuses than OVER fetuses ( P  = 0.04). Thioredoxin (TXN) was 10.2% greater in OVER fetuses than CON ( P  = 0.03).   Figure 1.
