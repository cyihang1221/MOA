</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="nph16528-abs-0001"><h2>Summary</h2>
<ul id="nph16528-list-0001" class="list" style="list-style-type:disc">
<li><p>
<em>Epichloë festucae</em> is an endophytic fungus that forms a symbiotic association with <em>Lolium perenne</em>. Here we analysed how the metabolome of the ryegrass apoplast changed upon infection of this host with sexual and asexual isolates of <em>E. festucae</em>.</p></li>
<li><p>A metabolite fingerprinting approach was used to analyse the metabolite composition of apoplastic wash fluid from uninfected and infected <em>L. perenne</em>. Metabolites enriched or depleted in one or both of these treatments were identified using a set of interactive tools. A genetic approach in combination with tandem MS was used to identify a novel product of a secondary metabolite gene cluster.</p></li>
<li><p>Metabolites likely to be present in the apoplast were identified using <span class="font-variant-small-caps">marvis</span> in combination with the BioCyc and KEGG databases, and an in‐house <em>Epichloë</em> metabolite database. We were able to identify the known endophyte‐specific metabolites, peramine and epichloëcyclins, as well as a large number of unknown markers.</p></li>
<li><p>To determine whether these methods can be applied to the identification of novel <em>Epichloë</em>‐derived metabolites, we deleted a gene encoding a NRPS (<em>lgsA</em>) that is highly expressed <em>in planta</em>. Comparative MS analysis of apoplastic wash fluid from wild‐type‐ vs mutant‐infected plants identified a novel Leu/Ile glycoside metabolite present in the former.</p></li>
</ul>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> apoplast, endophyte, <em>Epichloë</em>, metabolome, symbiosis</p></section></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0001"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>The fungal endophyte <em>Epichloë festucae</em> forms symbiotic associations with temperate grasses of the <em>Festuca</em>, <em>Lolium</em> and <em>Koeleria</em> genera (Leuchtmann <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0035">1994</a>). Hyphae grow in the intercellular (apoplastic) spaces of aerial tissues, systemically colonizing the host leaf sheath, leaf blade, and inflorescences (May <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0038" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0038">2008</a>; Scott <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0056" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0056">2012</a>). Hyphae attach to the host cell wall through an adhesive matrix and cease growing in the later stages of host development, though they remain metabolically active (Tan <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0061" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0061">2001</a>; Christensen &amp; Voissey, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0010">2007</a>). <em>Epichloë</em> species growing <em>in planta</em> produce a wide variety of secondary metabolites (SMs) that protect the host against herbivores (Malinowski &amp; Belesky, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0036" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0036">2000</a>; Clay &amp; Schardl, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0014">2002</a>; Saikkonen <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0051" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0051">2016</a>). The most well characterized <em>Epichloë</em> SMs are the ergot alkaloids, lolines, indole‐diterpenes, and pyrrolopyrazines such as peramine (Young <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0071" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0071">2005</a>; Schardl <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0054" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0054">2013</a>; Berry <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0005">2015</a>). Ergot alkaloids and indole‐diterpenes are anti‐mammalian mycotoxins (Florea <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0023" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0023">2016</a>; Philippe, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0046" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0046">2016</a>), while lolines and peramine protect against insect herbivores (Rowan &amp; Gaynor, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0049" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0049">1986</a>; Wilkinson <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0068" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0068">2000</a>; Tanaka <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0063" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0063">2005</a>; Schardl <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0052" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0052">2007</a>; Pan <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0045">2014</a>).</p>
<p>In addition to these well‐known SMs, <em>Epichloë</em> endophytes are known to produce ribosomally synthesized and post‐translationally modified peptides (RiPPs) derived from the secreted polypeptide GigA (Johnson <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0031">2015</a>). Imperfect 27‐amino‐acid repeats within the GigA sequence are cleaved to release multiple different cyclic RiPPs, which are collectively known as the ‘epichloëcyclins’. The spectrum of epichloëcyclins produced varies between different endophyte strains as a result of differences in encoded GigA sequences; for example, epichloëcyclins A–N have been characterized, but only A–E are present in plants infected with <em>E. festucae</em> strain Fl1 (Johnson <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0031">2015</a>). <em>Epichloë</em> species are also known to protect the host, to varying degrees, from a range of fungal pathogens (Gwinn &amp; Gavin, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0026">1992</a>; Bonos <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0006">2005</a>; Clarke <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0013">2006</a>; Steinebrunner <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0060">2008</a>; Niones &amp; Takemoto, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0043" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0043">2014</a>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0044" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0044">2015</a>; Tian <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0064" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0064">2017</a>). In addition to protection from these biotic stresses, <em>Epichloë</em> endophytes also protect hosts from abiotic stresses such as drought (Arachevaleta <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0002">1989</a>; Hahn <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0027">2007</a>) although the physiological basis of this phenomenon is not known. Collectively, these and other studies have shown that <em>Epichloë</em> endophytes have an important biological role in both natural and agricultural ecosystems (Johnson <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0030">2013</a>).</p>
<p>There are many changes in the host transcriptome upon endophyte symbiosis establishment, including differential expression of genes involved in host hormone production, pathogenesis‐related gene expression, adaptation to biotic stresses, and host cell wall metabolism (Ambrose &amp; Belanger, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0001">2012</a>; Dupont <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0018">2015</a>; Schmid <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0055">2016</a>; Dinkins <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0016">2017</a>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0017" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0017">2019</a>). Given these major changes in the transcriptome it is likely that endophyte symbiosis results in metabolome reprogramming. While transcriptome studies have given us significant insights into how host and endophyte reprogramming might occur (Eaton <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0019">2010</a>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0020">2015</a>; Chujo <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0011">2019</a>), these experiments provide very little insight into the exact metabolite changes that occur within the apoplastic environment where the two organisms interact. While some of the SMs synthesized by <em>Epichloë</em> are known to be mobilized throughout the host plant, presumably facilitating systemic host protection (Koulman <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0033">2007</a>), little is known about their exact composition in the apoplast. The aim of this study was to compare the composition of metabolites in the apoplast wash fluid of <em>Epichloë</em>‐infected vs mock‐infected plants by a nontargeted liquid chromatography‐coupled high‐resolution electrospray ionization mass spectroscopy (LC‐HR‐MS)‐based metabolome analysis. In particular, we set out to identify unknown compounds that could be novel symbiosis‐induced <em>E. festucae</em> or host‐synthesized metabolites that contribute to host protection.</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0002"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="nph16528-sec-0003"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Endophyte inoculations and growth conditions</h3>
<p>Endophyte‐free and common‐toxic endophyte (CTE)‐infected seeds (<em>Lolium perenne</em> cv Samson) were germinated on 3% water agar. Endophyte‐free seedlings were inoculated with <em>E. festucae</em> strains as previously described (Latch &amp; Christensen, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0034" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0034">1985</a>). Plants were grown in root trainers in an environmentally controlled growth room at 22°C with a 16 h : 8 h, light : dark photoperiod (<em>c</em>. 100 µmol m<sup>−2</sup> s<sup>−1</sup>) and at 10 wk post‐inoculation, tested for the presence of the endophyte by immunoblotting (Tanaka <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0063" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0063">2005</a>).</p>
<p>Two‐week‐old seedlings of <em>Triticum aestivum</em> (cv Summit) were infected with <em>Zymoseptoria tritici</em> (WAI321) by syringe infiltration into the second leaf with spores at a concentration of 5 × 10<sup>6</sup> ml<sup>−1</sup>.</p>
<p>
<em>Escherichia coli</em> cultures were grown in lysogeny broth (LB) or on LB agar supplemented with 100 μg ml<sup>−1</sup> ampicillin (Miller, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0039" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0039">1972</a>). <em>Epichloë festucae</em> cultures were grown on 2.4% (w/v) potato‐dextrose (PD) agar or in PD broth (Moon <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0041" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0041">1999</a>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0040">2000</a>).</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0004"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Generation of gene deletion and complementation strains</h3>
<p>Biological materials and primers can be found in Supporting Information Tables <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S1 and S2</a>. Plasmid DNA was extracted using the High Pure Plasmid Isolation Kit (Roche). Fungal DNA was extracted as previously described (Byrd <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0007">1990</a>). Cloning and screening PCR reactions were performed using Q5 High‐Fidelity DNA and One‐<em>Taq</em> DNA polymerases (New England Biolabs (NEB), Ipswich, MA, USA) respectively. Products of PCR reactions were purified using the Wizard<sup>®</sup> SV Gel and PCR Clean‐Up system (Promega). Sequencing reactions were performed using the <span class="font-variant-small-caps">big‐dye terminator</span> v.3.1 Ready Reaction Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and separated using an ABI3730 genetic analyser (Applied Biosystems). Sequence data wer assembled and analysed using <span class="font-variant-small-caps">macvector</span> sequence assembly software, v.12.0.5 (MacVector Inc., Apex, NC, USA).</p>
<p>The <em>lgsA</em> deletion and complementation constructs were assembled using Gibson Assembly (Gibson <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0024" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0024">2009</a>). The <em>lgsA</em> (gene model EfM3.056230) (Schardl <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0054" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0054">2013</a>) replacement construct pKG39 was prepared by recombining a 2.6 kb pAN7‐1 vector backbone (amplified using primers pRS426F/pRS426R from a pAN7‐1 plasmid DNA template), a 1.4 kb hygromycin resistance cassette (primers hphF/hphR; pSF15.15 plasmid DNA template), and 1 and 2.3 kb sequence fragments that flank the <em>lgsA</em> gene in the Fl1 genome (primers KG167/168 and KG169/170, respectively; <em>E. festucae</em> Fl1 genomic DNA template). The Δ<em>lgsA</em> complementation construct pKG40 was prepared using a 5.5 kb pRS426 backbone (primers pRS426F/pRS426R; pRS426 plasmid DNA template) and a 4.2 kb genomic DNA fragment containing <em>lgsA</em> (primers KG167KG170; <em>E. festucae</em> Fl1 genomic DNA template). Plasmids were transformed into chemically competent <em>E. coli</em> DH5α cells and transformants selected using ampicillin (100 μg ml<sup>−1</sup>), and screened by restriction enzyme digestion. Candidate clones were verified by insert sequencing.</p>
<p>The <em>lgsC</em> (gene model EfM3.062310) deletion construct pCE57 was assembled using yeast recombinational cloning (Colot <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0015">2006</a>). For this, 5′ and 3′ sequences flanking the <em>lgsC</em> gene of 1432 and 967 bp respectively, were PCR‐amplified with Phusion<sup>®</sup> polymerase from genomic DNA template using primer sets pRS426‐cpsA‐F/cpsA‐hph‐R and hph‐cpsA‐F/cpsA‐pRS426‐R, which contained sequences that overlap with the yeast vector pRS426 and hph cassette. The P<em>trpC‐hph</em> cassette was amplified with primers hph‐F/hphR. Yeast cells were transformed with the <em>Eco</em>RI/<em>Xho</em>I‐linearized pRS426 backbone together with the <em>lgsC</em> 5′ and 3′ flanks and the P<em>trpC‐hph</em> cassette, as previously described (Gietz &amp; Woods, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0025">2002</a>). Transformants were selected on media lacking uracil, and plasmid DNA isolated and transformed into <em>E. coli</em> to select for plasmids containing the <em>in silico‐</em>predicted sequence of pCE57.</p>
<p>
<em>Epichloë festucae</em> protoplasts (Young <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0071" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0071">2005</a>) were prepared and transformed with 2–3 μg of target DNA, as previously described (Itoh <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0029" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0029">1994</a>). <em>Epichloë festucae lgsA</em> deletion strains were obtained using the Split Marker system (Rahnama <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0047" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0047">2016</a>). Two PCR fragments (primers KG171/SplitR and SplitF/KG172, pKG39 plasmid DNA template) were transformed into Fl1 protoplasts and transformants selected using hygromycin (150 μg ml<sup>−1</sup>). <em>Epichloë festucae lgsC</em> deletion strains were obtained by transforming a PCR‐amplified linear product from pCE57 using primers AR84/AR85. Transformants were subcultured three times onto selection medium and putative <em>lgsA</em> ‘knockouts’ were PCR‐screened using primer sets KG173/174 (1.5 kb knockout and 1.1 kb wild‐type (WT) products), and KG175/10 and KG11/176 (1.2 and 2.3 kb products for 5′ and 3′ flanks where correct integration has occurred). <em>lgsC</em> putative ‘knockouts’ were PCR‐screened using primer sets cps7/cps8 (4 kb knockout and 3.7 kb WT products), and cps7/TC44 and cps8/TC45 (1.56 kb and 2.04 kb products for 5′ and 3′ flanks where correct integration has occurred).</p>
<p>Candidate ‘knockouts’ were confirmed by Southern blot analysis. Genomic DNA restriction enzyme digests (NEB), separated by agarose gel electrophoresis, were transferred to positively charged nylon membranes (Roche) and fixed by UV light cross‐linking in a Cex‐800 UV light cross‐linker (Ultra‐Lum, Claremont, CA, USA) at 254 nm for 2 min. Digoxigenin‐dUTP (DIG) labelling, and hybridization of the pKG39 DNA probe, and nitroblue tetrazolium chloride and 5‐bromo‐4‐chloro‐3‐indolyl‐phosphate (NBT/BCIP) visualization, were performed using the DIG High Prime DNA Labelling and Detection Starter Kit I (Roche). <em>Hin</em>dIII generates hybridizing fragments of 8, 2.6 and 1.8 kb fragments in WT, and 10.9 and 1.8 kb fragments in ‘knockout’ strains. Complementation strains were generated by cotransforming pKG40 and pII99 plasmid DNA into Δ<em>lgsA</em> protoplasts using geneticin (200 μg ml<sup>−1</sup>) selection. Complementation strains were PCR‐screened using the primers KG171/172 (4.2 kb complementation product).</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0005"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Microscopy analyses</h3>
<p>Culture morphology was analysed using an Olympus IX71 inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan), with the filters set for capturing differential interference contrast. Infected pseudostem tissues for Confocal Laser‐Scanning Microscopy (CLSM) were stained as previously described (Becker <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0003">2016</a>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0004">2018</a>). CLSM images were captured using a Leica SP5 DM6000B confocal microscope (488 nm argon and 561 nm DPSS laser, 40× oil immersion objective, NA = 1.3; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0006"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Apoplast extractions for metabolite analysis</h3>
<p>To extract apoplastic fluid, <em>L. perenne</em> leaf and pseudostem tissues were rinsed and submerged in water, and vacuum‐infiltrated at <em>c</em>. 60 mbar for 3 × 10 min cycles. Tissues were lightly blotted dry to remove surface water and placed in 50 ml polypropylene Falcon tubes retrofitted with a plastic sieve wedged above the conical section of the tube. Tubes were centrifuged at 2000 <strong><em>g</em></strong> for 10 min to collect apoplastic wash fluid in the bottom of the tube, which was removed to a separate microtube and centrifuged at 17 000 <strong><em>g</em></strong> for 10 min to remove cellular debris, then analysed or stored at −20°C.</p>
<p>Apoplastic wash fluid from infected and uninfected seedlings of <em>T. aestivum</em> (cv Summit) were harvested at 2 wk post‐infection with <em>Z. tritici</em> (WAI321). The leaves were removed from the plant and submerged in sterile distilled water under vacuum for 5 min. The infiltrated leaves were then dried and placed into the barrel of a 30 ml syringe which in turn was inserted inside a sterile 50 ml centrifuge tube. The leaves were centrifuged at 5000 <strong><em>g</em></strong> for 5 min at 4°C after which the extracted apoplast wash fluid was quickly transferred to a 1.5 ml Eppendorf tube and frozen in liquid nitrogen before being freeze‐dried overnight and stored at −20°C (Solomon &amp; Oliver, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0058" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0058">2001</a>).</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0007"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolite fingerprinting</h3>
<section id="nph16528-sec-0008"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Two‐phase extraction of apoplastic fluids</h4>
<p>The two‐phase extraction method with methyl‐<em>tert</em>‐butyl ether (MTBE), methanol and water (Matyash <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0037" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0037">2008</a>) was adapted from a previous method (Feussner &amp; Feussner, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0021" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0021">2019</a>) and modified for apoplastic wash fluid. Frozen apoplastic wash fluid (−80°C) was thawed on ice, vortexed and 200 µl was added to 150 µl of methanol in a glass vial followed by addition of 500 µl MTBE. The sample was vortexed and shaken for 1 h in the dark and then 120 µl of water was added. Samples were incubated for 10 min for phase separation and centrifuged for 15 min at 800 <strong><em>g</em></strong>. The upper (nonpolar) and lower (polar) phases were separately removed, transferred to new glass vials, and dried under a stream of nitrogen. The metabolites of the polar extraction phase were dissolved in 60 µl of methanol : acetonitrile : water (1 : 1 : 12, v/v/v). The metabolites of the nonpolar extraction phase were dissolved in 60 µl of 80% methanol. All samples were shaken for 15 min and centrifuged for 10 min. From each sample, 50 µl was transferred into a glass microvial, covered with argon and used for metabolite fingerprinting analysis.</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0009"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Data acquisition</h4>
<p>For metabolite fingerprinting analysis an ultraperformance liquid chromatography system coupled to the photo diode array detector eλ (Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA) and the high‐resolution orthogonal time‐of‐flight MS (HR‐MS) LCT Premier (Waters) was used as previously described (Feussner &amp; Feussner, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0021" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0021">2019</a>). Samples of the polar and the nonpolar extraction phases were analysed in the positive and in the negative electrospray ionization (ESI) mode, resulting in four datasets.</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0010"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Data processing and data mining</h4>
<p>Peak selection and alignment for each dataset was done with the software <span class="font-variant-small-caps">markerlynx</span>
<span class="font-variant-small-caps">xs</span> for <span class="font-variant-small-caps">masslynx</span> v.4.1 (Waters). This data deconvolution procedure resulted in four data matrixes of several thousand metabolite features (3258 and 1797 features for the polar extraction phase for positive and negative ESI, respectively; 1750 and 740 features for the nonpolar extraction phase for positive and negative ESI, respectively (Tables <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S3–S6</a>)). The software suite <span class="font-variant-small-caps">marvis</span> (Kaever <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0032">2015</a>; <a href="http://marvis.gobics.de/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://marvis.gobics.de/</a>) was used for further data processing, such as ranking, filtering, adduct correction, merging of datasets as well as for data mining such as clustering and automated database search. Subsequently, ANOVA and Benjamini–Hochberg for multiple testing were applied with the tool <span class="font-variant-small-caps">marvis filter</span> to rank the metabolite features of each of the four data matrices and filter them for a false discovery rates (FDR) of &lt; 0.003. After adduct correction the data matrices were combined. This resulted in a dataset of 203 metabolite features (Tables <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S7, S8</a>). Next, the tool <span class="font-variant-small-caps">marvis‐cluster</span> was used for clustering and visualizing the intensity profiles of the selected features by means of one‐dimensional self‐organizing maps (1D‐SOMs). Finally, <span class="font-variant-small-caps">marvis‐pathway</span> was applied to facilitate the annotation of metabolite features based on accurate mass information. The databases KEGG (<a href="http://www.kegg.jp" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.kegg.jp</a>; Tanabe &amp; Kanehisa, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0062" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0062">2012</a>), BioCyc (<a href="http://biocyc.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://biocyc.org</a>; Caspi <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0008">2012</a>) and an in‐house database specific for SMs of <em>Epichloë</em> (Table <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S9</a>) were used in combination with a framework for metabolite set enrichment analysis to annotate the metabolite features.</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0011"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Verification of the chemical structure of marker compounds</h4>
<p>To confirm or to elucidate the chemical identity of marker metabolites, LC‐HR‐MS/MS analyses were performed by UHPLC LC 1290 Infinity (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled to the 6540 UHD accurate‐mass Q‐TOF LC‐MS instrument with Agilent Dual Jet Stream Technology as ESI source (Agilent Technologies).</p></section></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0012"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Whole‐pseudostem extractions for polar metabolite analysis</h3>
<p>Pseudostem tissues were snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, freeze‐dried, and 50 mg (DW) of each sample was placed into 2 ml impact‐resistant screw‐cap microtubes containing 3 × 3.2 mm diameter stainless steel beads and ground to a powder using a FastPrep<sup>®</sup> FP120 Cell Disrupter System (Thermo Scientific Savant, Waltham, MA, USA). Ground samples were mixed with 1 ml 50% (v/v) methanol and incubated at room temperature in the dark with end‐over‐end rotation at 40 rpm for 1 h. Cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 17 000 <strong><em>g</em></strong> for 10 min, and the supernatant transferred to an amber glass high‐pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC) vial through a 13‐mm‐diameter, 0.45‐µm‐pore polytetrafluoroethylene syringe filter (Jet BioFil, Guangzhou, China). Extracts were then analysed or stored at −20°C.</p>
<p>Whole pseudostem and apoplastic fluid extracts generated for identification of the putative LgsA product were analysed using a Q Exactive Focus hybrid quadrupole‐orbitrap high‐resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Each sample (5 µl injection) was separated on a 2.1 × 150 mm Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 column with 1.8 µm particle size (Agilent Technologies) with a flow rate of 0.2 ml min<sup>−1</sup> and a linear gradient profile using water with 0.1% formic acid as eluent A and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid as eluent B, with time 0 min (<em>T</em>
<sub>0</sub>) at 5% B, <em>T</em>
<sub>1</sub> at 5% B, <em>T</em>
<sub>21</sub> at 95% B, <em>T</em>
<sub>26</sub> at 95% B, <em>T</em>
<sub>27</sub> at 5% B, followed by equilibration to initial conditions over the following 6 min.</p>
<p>Samples (five replicates) were analysed by HPLC‐coupled HR positive ESI MS (LC‐MS) using a capture window of 133–2000 <em>m/z</em>, and the resulting mass spectra were compared using Compound Discoverer 2.1 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to identify features exhibiting highly differential profiles between sample conditions. Mass spectra from samples of mock‐inoculated (uninfected) plants were used as controls for baseline subtraction.</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0013"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Bioinformatics</h3>
<p>The <em>E. festucae</em> WT (Fl1/E894) genome is available at <a href="http://csbio-l.csr.uky.edu/ef894-2011/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://csbio‐l.csr.uky.edu/ef894‐2011/</a> (Schardl <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0054" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0054">2013</a>). The WT axenic culture and <em>in planta</em> transcriptome data used here are available from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under Bioproject PPRJNA447872 (Hassing <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0028">2019</a>).</p></section></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0014"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="nph16528-sec-0015"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Endophyte symbiosis alters the metabolite composition of the host apoplast</h3>
<p>In nature, the SM composition of <em>E. festucae</em>‐infected host tissues varies considerably depending on the <em>E. festucae</em> strain used (Schardl <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0052" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0052">2007</a>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0054" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0054">2013</a>; Young <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0072" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0072">2009</a>). We therefore included two <em>E. festucae</em> strains, Fl1 and CTE, in our analysis. The former is a commonly used laboratory strain that forms a stable association with <em>L. perenne</em> (Leuchtmann <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0035">1994</a>; Scott <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0056" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0056">2012</a>) while the latter is a naturally occurring asexual endophyte of <em>L. perenne</em> (Christensen <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0009">1993</a>). While a range of SMs are known to be synthesized in each of these associations, our knowledge of the biosynthetic capability of Fl1 is much more extensive because of the availability of a complete genome sequence and the relative ease with which mutants can be generated (Scott <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0056" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0056">2012</a>; Schardl <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0054" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0054">2013</a>; Winter <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0069" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0069">2018</a>). To determine how Fl1 and CTE strains affect the host apoplastic metabolome, we extracted apoplastic wash fluid from leaves of mock‐inoculated (Mock), Fl1‐infected, and CTE‐infected <em>L. perenne</em> associations by two‐phase extraction and analysed both extraction phases by a metabolite fingerprinting approach using LC‐HR‐MS in both positive and negative ESI‐modes (Feussner &amp; Feussner, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0021" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0021">2019</a>). This analysis resulted in several thousand metabolite features (Tables <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S3–S6</a>). Filtering features of the four datasets by FDR &lt; 0.003 selected 203 metabolite features. As LC‐ESI‐MS analyses tend to form adducts, these 203 features may represent in total <em>c</em>. 60–80 metabolites, which show strong differences in their intensity profiles between treatments. Principal component analysis of this dataset showed a clear separation on principal component 1 (PC1) for the apoplast metabolome of mock‐treated and FI1‐infected ryegrass, while PC2 separates all three treatments (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S1</a>). Next, the intensity profiles of the selected features were clustered and visualized by means of 1D‐SOMs and organized into four clusters (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-fig-0001" class="usa-link">1a</a>; Table <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S7</a>). Cluster 1 contains 36 metabolite features that are specifically enriched in CTE‐infected samples, cluster 3 contains 106 features that are specifically enriched in Fl1‐infected samples, and cluster 4 contains 42 features that are enriched in both CTE and Fl1‐infected samples. Cluster 2 contains 19 metabolite features that are depleted in the apoplastic wash fluid of endophyte‐infected plants in comparison to the mock‐treated plants.</p>

The databases BioCyc and KEGG, as well as an in‐house database, containing 117 SMs previously described for <em>Epichloë</em> (Table <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S9</a>)<em>,</em> were used to assign putative metabolite identities for these apoplastic features. The <em>E. festucae</em> metabolite peramine was identified in both CTE and Fl1‐infected samples, with a stronger accumulation in the Fl1‐infected samples (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-fig-0001" class="usa-link">1b</a>,<a href="#nph16528-fig-0001" class="usa-link">d</a>). The identity of peramine was confirmed unequivocally by LC‐HR‐MS/MS analysis (Table <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S8</a>; Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S2</a>). A set of epichloëcyclins were detected as double‐charged ions ([M + 2H]<sup>2+</sup>) using positive‐mode ESI on <em>Epichloë</em>‐infected samples (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-fig-0001" class="usa-link">1b</a>,<a href="#nph16528-fig-0001" class="usa-link">c</a>; Table <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S8</a>). Epichloëcyclin A was detected weakly in Fl1‐infected samples and strongly in CTE‐infected samples, whereas epichloëcyclins B–E accumulated exclusively in Fl1‐infected samples (Figs <a href="#nph16528-fig-0001" class="usa-link">1b</a>, <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S3, S4</a>). Sequencing <em>gigA</em> from CTE, which is part of a four‐gene cluster (Eaton <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0019">2010</a>; Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S5</a>a), revealed that this gene encodes a polypeptide that contains two identical epichloëcyclin A repeats (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S5</a>b). In addition to peramine and the epichloëcyclins, several other features representing so far unknown chemical structures were also present exclusively in the apoplastic wash fluid of endophyte‐infected associations (Tables <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S7, S8</a>) including two putative peptides, which are represented by the CTE‐specific cluster 1 (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S6</a>). A putatively annotated Kojibiose‐related metabolite (322.0643 Da), a putatively annotated <em>N</em>‐(hydroxypentyl) acetamide (145.1101 Da) and a compound of 471.1952 Da were found in the FI1‐specific cluster 3 (Table <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S8</a>). Such markers are of considerable interest as they represent potential novel bioactive fungal secondary metabolites.</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0016"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Identification of a novel amino acid glycoside produced by the NRPS‐like protein LgsA</h3>
<p>To determine whether we could connect a novel metabolite identified by the nontargeted apoplast metabolome analysis to an uncharacterized <em>E. festucae</em> gene clusters, we chose to delete a gene (model EfM3.056230) encoding a nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS)‐like protein that is part of a putative SM gene cluster (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2</a>; Eaton <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0020">2015</a>). The genes from this cluster are expressed at very low levels in axenic cultures of <em>E. festucae</em> Fl1, but are dramatically upregulated when <em>E. festucae</em> is growing <em>in planta</em> (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2</a>; Table <a href="#nph16528-sup-0002" class="usa-link">S10</a>; Winter <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0069" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0069">2018</a>; Hassing <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0028">2019</a>). These genes were previously shown to be significantly downregulated in three <em>L. perenne</em> associations containing symbiotically defective <em>E. festucae</em> mutants (Eaton <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0020">2015</a>). We have provisionally named this cluster <em>LGS</em> (leucine/isoleucine glycoside synthesis) based on the identification of the product described below. The NRPS‐encoding gene (<em>lgsA</em>) from this cluster encodes the 1049 amino acid‐long protein LgsA which contains an N‐terminal thiolation (T) domain, a central condensation (C) domain, and a C‐terminal adenylylation (A) domain (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S7</a>). The presence of an A‐domain in LgsA suggests incorporation of an amino acid substrate into any product, but the domain structure of LgsA (T‐C‐A) is atypical compared with previously characterized NRPS and NRPS‐like proteins. The other genes in the <em>LGS</em> cluster are predicted to encode an ABC transporter (<em>lgsD</em>), a haloacid dehydrogenase‐like hydrolase (gene 1), a mitochondrial substrate carrier protein (gene 2), a NADP‐binding oxidoreductase (<em>lgsB</em>), a FAD‐dependent oxidoreductase (gene 3), and a glycosyl transferase (<em>lgsC</em>) (Table <a href="#nph16528-tbl-0001" class="usa-link">1</a>). These putative functions suggested a product containing both amino acid and sugar residues that may be exported into the apoplastic space.</p>

</div></div></section><p>To investigate the function of LgsA in <em>E. festucae</em>, a replacement construct (pKG39) was prepared and two overlapping PCR‐amplified linear fragments of this plasmid were introduced into the genome of <em>E. festucae</em> strain Fl1 by homologous recombination (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S8</a>). PCR screening of <em>c</em>. 20 hygromycin‐resistant transformants identified two candidates – ∆<em>lgsA</em>#2 and ∆<em>lgsA</em>#11 – that had PCR product patterns consistent with targeted replacement events. Southern blot analysis of genomic DNA digests from these transformants probed with a PCR fragment derived from pKG39 confirmed that both candidates contained a single‐copy integration of the replacement construct at the target <em>lgsA</em> gene locus, with no additional ectopic integrations (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S8</a>).</p>
<p>Macroscopic and microscopic analyses revealed that the culture morphologies of both ∆<em>lgsA</em> strains were indistinguishable from the WT (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S9</a>). To determine the host interaction phenotype, WT and ∆<em>lgsA</em> strains were inoculated into <em>L. perenne</em> seedlings and the phenotype of infected plants was examined at 8 wk post‐planting (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S10</a>). The whole plant and cellular phenotypes of plants infected with the ∆<em>lgsA</em> mutants were indistinguishable from WT‐infected plants (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S10</a>). These results were not unexpected given that the likely function of the product of this gene is the synthesis of a bioprotective metabolite, which is typically phenotypically neutral when plants are grown under laboratory conditions in the absence of biotic stress (Tanaka <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0063" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0063">2005</a>; Young <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0071" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0071">2005</a>; Saikia <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0050">2012</a>).</p>
<p>To examine the chemotype of these associations, whole‐pseudostem extracts and apoplastic wash fluid samples were extracted from <em>L. perenne</em> plants infected with WT, ∆<em>lgsA</em> mutants (both ∆<em>lgsA</em>#2 &amp; ∆<em>lgsA</em>#11), or mock‐inoculated plants. Comparison of the LC‐MS data of pseudostem extract samples from Fl1‐infected and ∆<em>lgsA</em>‐infected plants did not identify any significantly different signal abundances. By contrast, comparison of apoplastic wash fluid samples identified a [M + H]+ 472.2022 feature in Fl1‐infected samples that was undetectable in all ∆<em>lgsA</em> apoplast wash fluid samples (Figs <a href="#nph16528-fig-0003" class="usa-link">3a</a>,<a href="#nph16528-fig-0003" class="usa-link">c</a>, <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S11</a>a). Targeted analysis revealed that this metabolite could also be detected in whole‐pseudostem extracts from WT‐infected plants, although signal intensity was an order of magnitude lower than from apoplastic wash fluid samples. The functional genetic link between <em>lgsA</em> and this metabolite of 471.1949 Da (neutral mass) was confirmed by demonstrating that metabolite production could be restored by reintroduction of a WT <em>lgsA</em> sequence into both ∆<em>lgsA</em> strains (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-fig-0003" class="usa-link">3a</a>). The metabolite of 471.1949 Da was absent from apoplastic wash fluid samples from <em>L. perenne</em> infected with <em>E. festucae</em> var <em>lolii</em> CTE.</p>

<p>To determine whether proteins similar to LgsA are encoded by genes in other fungi, a <span class="font-variant-small-caps">tblastn</span> search was performed against the NCBI database using the <em>E. festucae</em> LgsA protein sequence as query. This analysis identified matches to NRPS‐like proteins in <em>Zymoseptoria brevis</em> (60% amino acid identity), <em>Z. tritici</em> (60%), and <em>Ramularia collo‐cygni</em> (68%) (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S7</a>) that share the same unusual T‐C‐A domain structure as LgsA. Homologues to other putative <em>LGS</em> cluster genes were either absent or located at different loci in these <em>Zymoseptoria</em> and <em>Ramularia</em> spp., with the exception of the putative NADP‐binding oxidoreductase (<em>lgsB</em>), which was located immediately adjacent to the <em>lgsA</em> homologues of both <em>Zymoseptoria</em> species (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2</a>).</p>
<p>We therefore compared apoplastic wash fluid samples from wheat plants infected with <em>Z. tritici</em> to uninfected wheat plants by LC‐HR‐MS. These analyses showed that <em>Z. tritici</em>‐infected wheat apoplastic wash fluid samples do not contain the 471 Da metabolite produced by the <em>E. festucae LGS</em> cluster. Data‐dependent LC‐HR‐MS/MS spectra generated from the <em>Z. tritici</em>‐infected wheat apoplast samples were therefore filtered to identify metabolites exhibiting a neutral loss of 162.0523 Da (C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>10</sub>O<sub>5</sub>/hexose‐moiety) or 178.0476 Da (C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>10</sub>O<sub>6</sub>/hexuronic acid‐moiety), which are characteristic features of the <em>Epichoë</em> LGS product. Candidates exhibiting these neutral losses were eliminated if they were also present in the LC‐HR‐MS spectra of uninfected wheat apoplastic wash fluid samples, exhibited a substantially later retention time compared with the 2.21 min <em>E. festucae</em> LGS metabolite, or exhibited elemental compositions or LC‐HR‐MS/MS spectrum features that were not consistent with the properties expected of an LGS‐like product. This left a single [M + H]<sup>+</sup> 308.1334 feature with a retention time of 2.89 min as the sole remaining candidate for a <em>Z. tritici LGS</em> cluster product in this dataset, corresponding to a metabolite with an elemental composition of C<sub>12</sub>H<sub>21</sub>NO<sub>8</sub> (calculated mass, 307.1267 Da; observed mass, 307.1261 Da; Figs <a href="#nph16528-fig-0003" class="usa-link">3b</a>,<a href="#nph16528-fig-0003" class="usa-link">d</a>, <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S12</a>b). Analysis of the LC‐HR‐MS/MS spectrum for this compound shows that the product ion of <em>m/z</em> 146.0811 remaining after the loss of the hexose‐moiety as well as the corresponding fragments of <em>m/z</em> 128.0707 and 100.0759 could derive from a six‐carbon amino acid, although this does not appear to be a Leu/Ile‐moiety (Figs <a href="#nph16528-fig-0003" class="usa-link">3d</a>, <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S11</a>b). Given that the substrate‐specifying ‘A‐domain code’ (Stachelhaus <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0059" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0059">1999</a>) of the <em>Z. tritici</em> LgsA homologue (DGLMYAVILK) has diverged somewhat from that of <em>E. festucae</em> LgsA (DALLYGIMAK) it is possible that these two proteins bind different amino acid substrates.</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0018"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LgsC is required for biosynthesis of the <em>E. festucae</em> LGS product</h3>
<p>Given that the <em>E. festucae LGS</em> cluster has a gene (<em>lgsC</em>; EfM3.062310) encoding a putative glycosyl transferase that is absent from the <em>Z. tritici</em> cluster, we hypothesized that this gene could encode an enzyme catalysing the conjugation of the second monosaccharide present in the 471 Da <em>E. festucae</em> LGS product. We therefore prepared a replacement construct (pCE57) for <em>lgsC</em>, and transformed the linear product of this construct into protoplasts of <em>E. festucae</em>. A PCR screen of hygromycin‐resistant transformants identified four transformants (Δ<em>lgsC</em>#6‐6, #7‐6, #11‐4 and #12‐6) in which the <em>lgsC</em> gene was deleted, with this result confirmed by Southern blot analysis (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S13</a>). Seedlings of <em>L. perenne</em> were infected with <em>E. festucae</em> WT, Δ<em>lgsC</em>#6‐6 and Δ<em>lgsC</em>#12‐6 and, as expected, <em>L. perenne</em> plants infected with the ∆<em>lgsC</em> mutant exhibited the same host interaction phenotype as WT‐infected plants (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-sup-0001" class="usa-link">S14</a>). Whole‐pseudostem extracts and apoplastic wash fluid samples were harvested from three mature plants of each association and analysed by LC‐HR‐MS. Apoplastic wash fluid samples from the ∆<em>lgsC</em> mutants lacked the 471 Da LGS product, demonstrating that LgsC is required for LGS biosynthesis in <em>E. festucae</em> (Fig. <a href="#nph16528-fig-0004" class="usa-link">4</a>). LC‐HR‐MS analysis of apoplastic fluid and pseudostem extracts targeting metabolites with the sum formulas C<sub>12</sub>H<sub>23</sub>NO<sub>8</sub>, C<sub>12</sub>H<sub>21</sub>NO<sub>8</sub>, C<sub>12</sub>H<sub>23</sub>NO<sub>7</sub>, and C<sub>12</sub>H<sub>21</sub>NO<sub>7</sub> revealed that ∆<em>lgsC</em>‐infected material does not appear to accumulate any of these hypothesized monoglycosylated LGS intermediates. However, given that the 471 Da <em>E. festucae</em> LGS product itself was barely detectable in pseudostem extracts, it is possible that a monoglycosylated intermediate that is not exported into the apoplast is present at low levels.</p>

Three plants were analysed per association, with representative examples shown here.</p></figcaption></figure></section></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0019"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p>Establishment of a mutualistic symbiotic association between <em>E. festucae</em> and <em>L. perenne</em> results in a dramatic change to both the host and fungal transcriptome (Dupont <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0018">2015</a>; Winter <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0069" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0069">2018</a>), leading to a reprogramming of plant host and mycosymbiont metabolism. While there are many changes in primary metabolism (Rasmussen <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0048" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0048">2008</a>; Zhang <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0073" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0073">2009</a>; Dupont <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0018">2015</a>), it is the changes to fungal secondary metabolism that best define these associations as many compounds within this group have known bioprotective roles in natural ecosystems (Schardl <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0053" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0053">2012</a>). The preferential expression of genes encoding these alkaloid biosynthetic enzymes in the host plant makes it challenging to identify novel endophyte‐induced metabolites given the complexity of the leaf extract metabolome. However, by focusing on the apoplast, where the endophyte grows, the metabolite complexity is reduced, thereby enabling the identification of novel products. Here we describe for the first time a nontargeted analysis of the <em>L. perenne</em> apoplast metabolome and how it changes upon establishment of a symbiotic interaction with <em>E. festucae</em>.</p>
<p>The metabolite differences between the apoplast metabolome of mock‐infected vs <em>E. festucae</em>‐infected ryegrass is surprisingly uncomplex, with just 203 metabolite features identified distinguishing these two physiological states. This is in contrast to more complex metabolome changes reported for the plant fungal pathogen <em>Verticillium longisporum</em> (Floerl <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0022">2012</a>). Of these features, 184 were shown to be enriched in the endophyte‐infected apoplastic fluid wash with the remaining 19 features corresponding to depletions in endophyte‐infected apoplastic fluid wash. As expected, fungal metabolites known to be preferentially accumulated in the host are among those enriched upon establishment of endophyte symbiosis, including peramine (Tanaka <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0063" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0063">2005</a>), and epichloëcyclins (Johnson <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0031">2015</a>). No lolines were detected in this analysis, as the two <em>E. festucae</em> strains used lack this biosynthetic capability (Schardl <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0054" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0054">2013</a>).</p>
<p>The epichloëcyclins were among the most abundant metabolites synthesized in response to endophyte symbiosis. These are RiPPs generated through proteolytic cleavage and cyclization of linear peptide repeats that comprise the pro‐peptide product of <em>gigA</em>. This gene is one of the most highly expressed in the endophyte–grass symbiotic interaction and is part of a four‐gene cluster (Eaton <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0019">2010</a>). This cluster also includes <em>kexB</em>, which encodes a kexin presumably required for cleavage of the repeats from the pro‐peptide; a gene encoding a protein with a DUF3328 domain, which is also found in UstYa/UstYb, which are proteins required for cyclization of the ustiloxin precursors in <em>Aspergillus flavus</em> (Nagano <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0042" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0042">2016</a>; Ye <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0070">2016</a>); and a gene encoding a hypothetic protein of unknown function. The cyclic nonapeptides epichloëcyclin A, B and C, and the cyclic octapeptides epichloëcylin B and C (Johnson <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0031">2015</a>), were found in the apoplastic wash fluid of ryegrass infected with strain Fl1, whereas only epichloëcyclin A was found in apoplastic wash fluid from CTE‐infected plants. These results are in agreement with the repeat structures of the corresponding GigA‐derived peptides from these <em>E. festucae</em> strains.</p>
<p>Regarding the alkaloids, both <em>E. festucae</em> strains have a functional copy of peramine synthetase, <em>perA</em>, and accumulate peramine in the apoplastic space as expected, given the known systemic distribution of this metabolite within the plant (Koulman <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0033">2007</a>). While metabolites with masses that match indole‐diterpenes known to be synthesized by these strains were detected (Saikia <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0050">2012</a>), the low signal intensity precluded their confirmation by LC‐HR‐MS/MS. The absence of ergot alkaloids, which are known to be synthesized by these strains <em>in planta</em> (Chujo &amp; Scott, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0012">2014</a>), is probably a result of their lack of transport into the apoplast.</p>
<p>As anticipated, there were many metabolites of unknown structure, highlighting the utility of this type of analysis to identify novel endophyte‐bioprotective metabolites. We therefore employed a combined genetics/metabolomics approach using the well‐developed genetic system of <em>E. festucae</em> strain Fl1 (Scott <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0056" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0056">2012</a>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0057" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0057">2018</a>) to demonstrate the utility of this apoplastic wash fluid method for identification of putative novel fungal metabolites. For this analysis we chose to delete the NRPS‐encoding gene <em>lgsA</em>, which is a component of a putative seven‐gene SM cluster that is downregulated in <em>L. perenne</em> associations with three different symbiotically defective <em>E. festucae</em> mutants (Eaton <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0020">2015</a>). All seven genes were found here to be significantly upregulated in the Fl1 <em>in planta</em> transcriptome compared with axenic culture. As expected for a gene encoding a putative bioprotective metabolite (Tanaka <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0063" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0063">2005</a>; Young <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0071" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0071">2005</a>; Saikia <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0050">2012</a>), the axenic culture and plant interaction phenotypes of the <em>E. festucae</em> Δ<em>lgsA</em> mutant were indistinguishable from the WT. A 471 Da metabolite that was absent in apoplastic wash fluid samples from plants infected with the Δ<em>lgsA</em> mutants was identified by LC‐HR‐MS in extracts of apoplastic wash fluid from <em>L. perenne</em> plants infected with WT <em>E. festucae</em> Fl1. The failure to identify this compound in an equivalent comparative analysis of whole‐pseudostem extracts of these same plants highlights the power of working with the much less complex apoplastic metabolome. LC‐HR‐MS/MS analysis suggests this LGS metabolite has a structure containing a Leu/Ile moiety linked to a glycoside constituent. Given the relatively small quantities of apoplastic wash fluid obtained using the method described here, definitive structural determination will probably require development of a high‐volume extraction method or an alternative approach such as overexpressing the cluster genes in a heterologous fungal host to obtain sufficient product for NMR analysis (Van de Bittner <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0065">2018</a>; van Dolleweerd <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0066" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0066">2018</a>). The absence of this 471 Da metabolite in the apoplastic fluid of <em>L. perenne</em> with CTE suggests that the genes are absent, mutated, or not expressed in this strain. However, the presence of the <em>LGS</em> cluster across several <em>Epichloë</em> species suggests this is a widespread SM of this genus (Eaton <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0020">2015</a>).</p>
<p>A comparative synteny analysis of the genomes of other filamentous fungi identified homologues to the NRPS‐encoding <em>lgsA</em> gene in the genomes of the cereal pathogens <em>R. collo‐cygni</em>, <em>Z. brevis</em>, and <em>Z. tritici</em>, with conserved homologues to the putative NADP‐binding oxidoreductase‐encoding gene <em>lgsB</em> located immediately adjacent in the <em>Zymoseptoria</em> genomes. LC‐HR‐MS analysis of extracts of apoplastic wash fluid samples from wheat infected with WT <em>Z. tritici</em> compared with apoplastic wash fluid from uninfected wheat plants identified a 307 Da metabolite that appears to have some structural similarities to the 471 Da metabolite, but with only a single glycosyl constituent. This structure is consistent with the absence of a conserved homologue to the putative glycosyl transferase‐encoding gene <em>lgsC</em> in these species. However, more substantial evidence, such as analysis of a <em>Z. tritici</em> ∆<em>lgsA</em> mutant, will be required to prove the hypothesized link between the <em>Z. tritici LGS</em> cluster and this 307 Da metabolite.</p>
<p>While the accessory gene content of a secondary metabolism cluster can vary considerably between fungal species, the genes encoding synthesis of the basic structural motif for a class of secondary metabolites are typically conserved (Schardl <em>et al.</em>, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0054" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0054">2013</a>). The conservation of <em>lgsA</em> and <em>lgsB</em> between the <em>LGS</em> clusters of <em>Zymoseptoria</em> and <em>Epichloë</em> spp. therefore suggests that LgsA and LgsB catalyse the first reactions in LGS biosynthesis. The presence of a Leu/Ile product ion in the LC‐HR‐MS/MS spectrum of the <em>E. festucae</em> LGS product suggests that the A‐domain of LgsA binds one of these amino acids as substrate. However, NRPS T‐domains typically accept activated amino acyl substrates from an upstream A‐domain (Weissman, <a href="#nph16528-bib-0067" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="nph16528-bib-0067">2015</a>). Given the unusual T‐C‐A structure of LgsA, it is unclear if the LgsA T‐domain could accept an activated Leu/Ile substrate from the downstream A‐domain. An alternative hypothesis would be that the LgsA T‐domain is loaded with an oxidized hexose derivative provided by LgsB. The LgsA C‐domain would then catalyse condensation between the amino acid and sugar moieties, which would be linked by an ester or amide bond depending on whether the T‐domain is loaded with the Leu/Ile or sugar constituent, respectively. LgsC would add the glycosyl group observed in <em>E. festucae</em>, either before or after the LgsA‐catalysed condensation event. The final LGS pathway product would then be exported into the apoplast by a transporter, presumably the putative ABC transporter encoded by <em>lgsD</em> in <em>E. festucae</em>. While not specifically analysed here, other co‐clustered genes in <em>Epichloë</em> and <em>Zymoseptoria</em> spp. may also encode proteins that contribute to LGS biosynthesis in their respective species.</p>
<p>While we do not know what the biological functions are for the compounds identified here, we make two observations: first, the gene clusters that encode the enzymes for the synthesis of these novel SMs appear to be limited to filamentous fungi that infect monocots of the subfamily Pooideae, and second, this gene cluster is dispensable for a mutualistic symbiotic interaction between <em>E. festucae</em> and <em>L. perenne</em>. Whether these products have a role in bioprotection, virulence or some other interaction role remains to be determined. It will be interesting to analyse the host interaction phenotype of a <em>Z. tritici</em> Δ<em>lgsA</em> mutant.</p>
<p>In conclusion, we describe for the first time how the metabolome of <em>L. perenne</em> changes upon establishment of symbiosis with either asexual or sexual isolates of <em>E. festucae</em>. Interestingly, the changes are not as dramatic as observed for fungal pathogen infection by <em>V. longisporum</em>, though the total number of studies in this field are still rather limited. Given the lower complexity of the apoplast metabolome in comparison to the metabolome of green tissues we have demonstrated here the power of a combined genetics/metabolomics approach to identify new metabolites in host apoplastic wash fluids. It will be of considerable interest to determine the structures of other unknown metabolites in this and other fungal–plant associations, and to identify the biological function of these metabolites in these symbioses.</p></section><section id="nph16528-sec-0021"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Author contributions</h2>
