</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>This study describes a multistage methodology to detect minute amounts of tetrodotoxin in fishes, a plan that may be broadened to include other marine organisms. This methodology was applied to porcupinefish (<em>Diodon hystrix</em>) collected in Punta Chiquirín, El Salvador. A three-stage approach along with post-acquisition processing was employed, to wit: (a) Sample screening by selected reaction monitoring (HPLC-MS/MS-SRM) analyses to quickly identify possible toxin presence via a LC/MS/MS API 3200 system with a triple quadrupole; (b) HPLC-HRFTMS-full scan analyses using an ion trap-Orbitrap spectrometer combined with an MZmine 2-enhanced dereplication-like workflow to collect high-resolution mass spectra; and (c) HPLC-HRMS<sup>2</sup> analyses. This is the first time tetrodotoxin has been reported in <em>D. hystrix</em> specimens collected in El Salvador.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> tetrodotoxin, HPLC-HRMS, HPLC-MS/MS-SRM, HPLC-HRMS<sup>2</sup>, <em>Diodon hystrix</em>, MZmine, natural products detection</p></section></section><section id="sec1-toxins-15-00409"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>Tetrodotoxin (TTX) is a powerful neurotoxin with a molecular weight of 319 Da, which is widely distributed in nature [<a href="#B1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-toxins-15-00409">1</a>]. Since the first report of this toxin isolated from the ovaries of a pufferfish by Tahara and Hirata at the beginning of the 20th century, TTX has been found in terrestrial vertebrates [<a href="#B2-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2-toxins-15-00409">2</a>,<a href="#B3-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3-toxins-15-00409">3</a>], invertebrates [<a href="#B4-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4-toxins-15-00409">4</a>,<a href="#B5-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5-toxins-15-00409">5</a>,<a href="#B6-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6-toxins-15-00409">6</a>], bacteria [<a href="#B7-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-toxins-15-00409">7</a>,<a href="#B8-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8-toxins-15-00409">8</a>,<a href="#B9-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-toxins-15-00409">9</a>,<a href="#B10-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10-toxins-15-00409">10</a>], and fishes, including <em>Diodon hystrix</em> [<a href="#B11-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11-toxins-15-00409">11</a>,<a href="#B12-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12-toxins-15-00409">12</a>,<a href="#B13-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13-toxins-15-00409">13</a>], among others [<a href="#B14-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14-toxins-15-00409">14</a>]. Although this toxin has been found in several organs of adult pufferfish, TTX is mostly localized in the skin, muscles, intestines, gonads, kidneys, and liver [<a href="#B15-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15-toxins-15-00409">15</a>]. The true origin of TTX remains controversial. Its production by symbiotic bacteria (endogenous route) or by accumulation through the diet are the most accepted proposals. Moreover, feeding on TTX-bearing microalgae by fish and its consequent accumulation is another explanation [<a href="#B9-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-toxins-15-00409">9</a>,<a href="#B16-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16-toxins-15-00409">16</a>,<a href="#B17-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17-toxins-15-00409">17</a>].</p>
<p>The deadliness of this potent neurotoxin is caused by its capacity to inhibit voltage-gated sodium channels and thus provoke death through respiratory paralysis [<a href="#B1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-toxins-15-00409">1</a>,<a href="#B18-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18-toxins-15-00409">18</a>]. The estimated minimum lethal dose value in humans is approximately 10,000 mouse units, nearly 2 mg [<a href="#B19-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19-toxins-15-00409">19</a>]. The most useful approach to analyzing TTX is the LC/MS technique. A review of the advantages and limitations of this technique to detect and analyze the presence of TTX and its analogs has been recently reported [<a href="#B20-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20-toxins-15-00409">20</a>].</p>
<p>The pufferfish <em>D. hystrix</em> (Linnaeus, 1758, Diodontidae), also known as spot-fin porcupinefish, is characterized by being one of the most predominant shell-crushing predators of mollusks in tropical coastal waters worldwide [<a href="#B21-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21-toxins-15-00409">21</a>]. <em>D. hystrix</em> is distinguished for bearing spines on the caudal peduncle, fins with dark spots, and a broad head, as well as slight differences in the number of fin rays. <em>D. hystrix</em> is widespread in tropical and subtropical waters of the Indian, Atlantic, and Pacific Oceans. It also dwells in the circumtropical eastern Pacific from San Diego, California, to Chile, including the Galapagos Islands, and in the western Atlantic, from Massachusetts and Bermuda, continuing down to the northern waters of the Gulf of Mexico all the way down to Brazil [<a href="#B22-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22-toxins-15-00409">22</a>,<a href="#B23-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23-toxins-15-00409">23</a>].</p>
<p>The characteristic conditions of the high concentration of suspended material, coastal currents, and surface temperatures that range between 26 and 31 °C present in the tropical Pacific Ocean adjacent to El Salvador create a favorable environment for several tropical species. Although <em>D. hystrix</em> is spread throughout the entire coast of El Salvador, from the estuary of Rio Paz (13°44′39″ N, 90°07′58″ W) to Meanguera Island (13°12′4″ N, 97°41′42″ W), it is not commercially fished or consumed by Salvadorans, a distaste that is shared by others in Central America and the Caribbean.</p>
<p>Cases of fish poisoning in Hawaii (USA) [<a href="#B24-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B24-toxins-15-00409">24</a>] and Papua New Guinea [<a href="#B25-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B25-toxins-15-00409">25</a>] have been reported after <em>D. hystrix</em> consumption or following minor injuries caused by stabs from its spines in The Netherlands [<a href="#B26-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B26-toxins-15-00409">26</a>]. TTX occurrence, however, was not confirmed through analytical methods in the forenamed cases. Several studies focusing on TTX detection in <em>D. hystrix</em> have been reported. A study published in 1997 on the neurotoxic and cytotoxic activities of skin secretions from a specimen collected on the northern coast of Sao Paulo State (Brazil) suggested the presence of neurotoxins other than TTX, although the existence of a residual amount of TTX is not discarded [<a href="#B27-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27-toxins-15-00409">27</a>]. As reported in 2014, the presence of TTX was detected and quantified by LC-MS/MS in the liver, but not in the muscle, of specimens from Sabah and Sarawak waters in Malaysia [<a href="#B11-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11-toxins-15-00409">11</a>]. A genotoxicity study of TTX extracted from different organs of <em>D. hystrix</em> from the southeastern Indian coast, reported in 2016, displayed its presence in the eyes, liver, intestines, and gonads. In this study, GC-MS analyses confirmed TTX occurrence in all raw extracts except the eye. Extracts obtained from the skin, gonads, and liver caused 100% mortality when they were tested on Zebra fish [<a href="#B12-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12-toxins-15-00409">12</a>]. More recently, TTX and analogs were detected and quantified by LC-MS /MS in the liver of four <em>D. hystrix</em> specimens collected on the southern coast of Mozambique [<a href="#B13-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13-toxins-15-00409">13</a>]. In contrast, TTX was not detected by LC-MS studies in a <em>D. hystrix</em> specimen collected in Hawaiian waters nor in 42 distinct bacterial strains cultivated from this pufferfish [<a href="#B28-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28-toxins-15-00409">28</a>]. In another study, no significant level of TTX was found in any organs of a <em>D. hystrix</em> specimen collected in Okinawan waters in Japan [<a href="#B29-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29-toxins-15-00409">29</a>]. Additionally, TTX uptake into liver tissue slices of <em>D. hystrix</em> displayed no significant increase in TTX content over time during incubation. This finding suggests a limited ability of this toxin for bioaccumulation [<a href="#B30-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30-toxins-15-00409">30</a>]. These studies seem to indicate that TTX presence in <em>D. hystrix</em> is associated with the geographic distribution of this pufferfish.</p>
<p>Continuing our search for bioactive natural products from different types of marine organisms [<a href="#B31-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31-toxins-15-00409">31</a>,<a href="#B32-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32-toxins-15-00409">32</a>,<a href="#B33-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B33-toxins-15-00409">33</a>], and given the lack of TTX studies of pufferfish from El Salvador, we endeavored to investigate the occurrence of TTX in <em>D. hystrix</em> collected in El Salvador due to the lack of TTX studies in pufferfish from these coasts. This paper reports the occurrence of TTX in extracts from different organs of <em>D. hystrix</em> sampled in Bahia Chiquirín, El Salvador, for the first time. A multistage methodology based on different MS techniques allowed the detection of TTX in minute amounts. These findings have given more insight into the worldwide distribution of TTX in <em>D. hystrix</em>.</p></section><section id="sec2-toxins-15-00409"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Results and Discussion</h2>
<p>Specimens of <em>D. hystrix</em> collected on the South coast of El Salvador were classified by gender (male, female, and undefined), and their organs, i.e., skin, liver, kidneys, flesh, and gonads, were dissected. All specimens collected were analyzed (three females, one male, and two undefined) (<a href="#toxins-15-00409-t001" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>, <a href="#app1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Figures S1 and S2</a>). Upon processing the samples (<a href="#app1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Figure S3</a>), they underwent submission to HPLC-MS/MS-SRM, HPLC-HRFTMS, and HPLC-HRFTMS<sup>2</sup> analysis. The main criteria were that the parent ion and at least one fragment ion of the toxin, as well as its retention time, be discernible [<a href="#B34-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34-toxins-15-00409">34</a>]. Moreover, blank samples were included to rule out false positives.</p>

In order to corroborate the presence of the toxin, additional experimental approaches were employed, including high-resolution parent ion separation and two high-resolution stages.</p>

HPLC-HRFTMS</h3>
<p>HPLC-HRFTMS, operating in Selected Ion Monitoring (SIM) and positive ionization modes (ESI-(+)), was the second method used to detect TTX in <em>D. hystrix.</em> The method was corroborated by a standard solution, shown in <a href="#app1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Figure S6</a>. The sample analyses evidenced the occurrence of TTX in both the female skin (FS) sample (<a href="#toxins-15-00409-f002" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a> and <a href="#app1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Figures S7–S9</a>) and the male liver (ML) sample.</p>

As a result, no other analog of TTX was detected in either sample.</p></section><section id="sec2dot3-toxins-15-00409"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Post-Acquisition Data Analysis by MZmine</h3>
<p>After the presence of TTX had been established by HPLC-HRFTMS in the <em>D. hystrix</em> samples, we provided a workflow allowing the automatization of toxin data analysis/dereplication. Laboratories wishing to automatize natural product detection should validate their data-analysis protocols. For data processing and metabolite detection, MZmine (V.2) has been extensively utilized in metabolomics and natural product dereplication, along with LTQ Orbitrap hybrid mass spectrometry [<a href="#B44-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B44-toxins-15-00409">44</a>,<a href="#B45-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B45-toxins-15-00409">45</a>,<a href="#B46-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B46-toxins-15-00409">46</a>,<a href="#B47-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B47-toxins-15-00409">47</a>,<a href="#B48-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B48-toxins-15-00409">48</a>,<a href="#B49-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B49-toxins-15-00409">49</a>]. The data analysis workflow software used here is shown in <a href="#sec4-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Section 4</a>.</p>
<p>The automatized mode was employed to process and analyze the Orbitrap HPLC-HRFTMS files. After treating the FS sample raw file with Xcalibur<sup>TM</sup>, a peak attributed to TTX emerged at 8.1 min. The identification of TTX in the FS sample was achieved with the help of existing high-resolution MS, isotope pattern, and Ring/Double Bond Equivalent (DBE) available in online databases (<a href="#toxins-15-00409-f003" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>). We concluded that by using this scheme, it was possible to routinely automatize toxin detection and ease data processing.</p>

HPLC-HRFTMS<sup>2</sup>

<p>Toxin presence in <em>D. histrix</em> was corroborated by multiple-stage mass spectrometry in the LU sample. The method was corroborated, as before, by using a TTX standard solution (<a href="#app1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Figure S11</a>). To optimize the signal-to-noise ratio, parameters were configured by utilizing a standard solution (see <a href="#sec4-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Section 4</a>). The stock solution showed two distinct transitions, suggesting the appropriateness of this method for TTX detection (<a href="#app1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Figure S11</a>).</p>
<p>The analyzed fragment ions found in LU displayed molecular formulas that matched those reported in the literature [<a href="#B34-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34-toxins-15-00409">34</a>]. The parent ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 320.1088 (C<sub>11</sub>H<sub>18</sub>N<sub>3</sub>O<sub>8</sub><sup>+</sup>) yielded distinctive fragment ions at the following <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values: 302.0983 (C<sub>11</sub>H<sub>16</sub>N<sub>3</sub>O<sub>7</sub><sup>+</sup>, Δ<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> = 0.03 ppm) and 162.0656 Da (C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>8</sub>N<sub>3</sub>O<sup>+</sup>, ∆<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> = 3.64 ppm). The Ring/Double Bond Equivalent (DBE) value changed from 4.5 to 5.5 for the fragment at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 302.0983, suggesting the loss of a water molecule accompanied by an adjustment on the ring system and resulting in the formation of an extra 5-member ring [9-O-4-4a-8a] (<a href="#toxins-15-00409-f004" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>a) [<a href="#B40-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B40-toxins-15-00409">40</a>]. On the other hand, the DBE value change from 5.5 to 6.5 for the fragment at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 162.0662 implied a significant modification in the molecular structure, suggesting the break of several bonds and the ensuing formation of a heterocyclic aromatic structure (<a href="#toxins-15-00409-f004" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>b). Therefore, based on the experimental evidence obtained through HPLC-HRFTMS<sup>2</sup> experiments, the presence of TTX in the LU sample was also confirmed.</p>

Conclusions</h2>
<p>A trace detection methodology by MS techniques was proposed and applied to detect TTX in specimens of porcupinefish (<em>D. hystrix</em>) collected in Punta Chiquirín, El Salvador. The same extraction scheme for the toxin was used in all the samples, and an extra purification step was included in the HPLC-HRMS<sup>2</sup> method to avoid matrix interference. Several techniques, such as HPLC-MS/MS-SRM, HPLC-HRFTMS, and HPLC-HRFTMS<sup>2,</sup> were employed in conjunction with post-processing data using MZmine 2. The approach outlined here can be adjusted to study the occurrence of TTX in various other marine organisms with an economic interest.</p>
<p>The toxin was detected in the gonads (FG) and skin (FS) of the female specimens, in the liver of the male (ML) specimen, and in the liver (LU) and muscles (MU) of the undefined specimens (<a href="#toxins-15-00409-t002" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). The quantification of TTX could not be determined in any of the samples, most likely due to toxin levels falling below the limit of quantification (LOQ).</p>
<p>Although TTX had previously been detected in <em>D. hystrix</em>, this is the first time that this toxin, distributed throughout the different organs of the fish, has been found in specimens collected in El Salvador. Hence, the consumption of <em>D. hystrix</em> collected in the region constitutes a potential threat of food poisoning. The traces of TTX detected in specimens collected in Salvadoran waters contrast with the quantifiable amounts reported from specimens collected on Malaysian, South East Indian, and Mozambican coasts and with nondetection in Hawaiian and Okinawan waters. <em>D. hystrix</em> represents another case where the concentration of TTX depends on the place of collection.</p>
<p>Extending the methodology proposed here to specimens collected on other coasts of El Salvador would contribute to mapping the prevalence of TTX in the country. We encourage further research into both TTX quantification and the geographical distribution of <em>D. hystrix</em> and other pufferfishes in Salvadoran waters.</p></section><section id="sec4-toxins-15-00409"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec4dot1-toxins-15-00409"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.1. General</h3>
<p>All the reagents used for sample preparation, desalting, and solvents for MS analysis were of the highest quality available. Reference tetrodotoxin (TTX) material was supplied through a collaboration with the Spanish Company Laboratorios Dr. Esteve S.A. (Barcelona, Spain). The certificate of analysis of the supplied sample showed a purity of 97.1%.</p></section><section id="sec4dot2-toxins-15-00409"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.2. Specimen Collection</h3>
<p>Specimens of female (3), male (1), and undefined gender (2) of <em>D. hystrix</em> were collected by artisanal fishermen using gillnets on 1 October 2014 at Punta Chiquirín, Department of La Unión, El Salvador (13°17′31.01″ N, 87°47′03.60″ W) (<a href="#toxins-15-00409-f005" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>).</p>

Sample Preparation</h3>
<p>Sample preparations were carried out through the methodology shown in <a href="#toxins-15-00409-f007" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>. After defrosting the organs, the tissues were extracted twice with 40 mL of CH<sub>3</sub>OH/H<sub>2</sub>O 1:4 (1% CH<sub>3</sub>COOH) in an ultrasonic bath at 55 Hz (5 min) and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min. The organic solvent was evaporated from the supernatant under reduced pressure to afford an aqueous phase (aqueous fraction 1) that was extracted twice with 25 mL of CH<sub>2</sub>Cl<sub>2</sub>. After removing the CH<sub>2</sub>Cl<sub>2</sub> under reduced pressure, the resulting aqueous fraction (aqueous fraction 2) was ultra-filtered (Amilcon<sup>®</sup> system) with two membrane filters of 100,000 Da and 10,000 Da, and the filtrate was submitted to fine Bio Gel P-2 column chromatography (pH = 5.5) for desalting (<a href="#app1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Figure S3</a>). The Bio Gel P-2 column was eluted first with H<sub>2</sub>O to remove the salts and then with 0.5 mL of AcOH (0.03 mol/L) to obtain a toxin-enriched fraction (Fraction G). Finally, the samples were lyophilized before undergoing MS analyses [<a href="#B50-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B50-toxins-15-00409">50</a>].</p>

<p>The stock solution was prepared by dissolving the TTX standard in CH<sub>3</sub>COOH (1 mM). Then, a 9-point calibration curve was prepared by serial dilution, covering a TTX concentration range of 6.3–1010 ng/mL (<a href="#app1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>, <a href="#app1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Figure S4</a>). The solution matrix was kept at −20 °C. The detection of the toxin in the <em>D. hystrix</em> solutions was tested by using HPLC-HRMS/MS-SRM, HPLC-HRFTMS, and HPLC-HRFTMS<sup>2</sup> by an in-house method. The corroboration of the HPLC-MS/MS method was based on the estimated limit of quantification (LOQ) and transition ion ratio (TIR) tolerance, as well as on the retention time tolerance. The corroboration of the HRMS method was focused on the reproducibility of retention time and on the detection or absence of <sup>13</sup>C<sub>1</sub>/<sup>12</sup>C<sub>n−1</sub> ions ratio in both the standard and several blank solutions. The HRMS calculations were undertaken using five significant figures (Sig. figs.), indicated in subscript format, although only four were used.</p></section><section id="sec4dot5-toxins-15-00409"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.5. Analytic Methodologies Used in Tetrodotoxin Detection</h3>
<section id="sec4dot5dot1-toxins-15-00409"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">4.5.1. HPLC-MS/MS-SRM</h4>
<p>Measurements were performed utilizing an HPLC-MS/MS API 3200 System (Applied Biosystems) supplied with a triple quadrupole MS/MS. The retention time, <em>m</em>/<em>z,</em> and presence of parent-product ion transitions were taken into account for a positive identification [<a href="#B51-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B51-toxins-15-00409">51</a>,<a href="#B52-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B52-toxins-15-00409">52</a>]. The following analytical conditions were employed: Mobile phase: CH<sub>3</sub>CO<sub>2</sub>NH<sub>4</sub> 16 mM/CH<sub>3</sub>CN (3:7), pH 5.5, 40.0 min isocratic elution in a TSKgel Amide-80 HILIC-column (150 × 2.0 mm i.d.; 5 µm, Tosho, Tokyo Japan), flow 0.2 mL/min, column oven temperature: 25 °C. Sample volume injection: 5 µsL. Two ion transitions: 320 → 302 and 320 → 162, corresponding to [M + H-H<sub>2</sub>O]<sup>+</sup> and [C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>8</sub>N<sub>3</sub>O]<sup>+</sup> ions of TTX fragmentation, respectively, were used. Ionization was achieved via electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive mode with selected reaction monitoring (SRM).</p>
<p>The occurrence of the toxin was determined by comparing the retention time variation (Δ<em>t<sub>R</sub></em>) with the transition ion ratio (TIR) of the standard variables [<a href="#B53-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B53-toxins-15-00409">53</a>,<a href="#B54-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B54-toxins-15-00409">54</a>]. They were computed using the following equation:</p>

<p>Preparation of the calibration curve: A concentrated TTX solution was prepared gravimetrically and utilized on a daily basis, ensuring freshness. Nine calibration standards were prepared from that solution by serial dilution at 6.3, 13, 25, 50, 100, 200, 600, 800, and 1010 ng/mL and used to plot the calibration curve (<em>R</em><sup>2</sup><sub>(320→302)</sub> = 0.996; <em>R</em><sup>2</sup><sub>(320→162)</sub> = 0.994; LOQ: 320 ng/mL; LOD: 76 ng/mL). The Δ<em>t<sub>R</sub></em> tolerance used was 0.1 min [<a href="#B55-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B55-toxins-15-00409">55</a>], and the TIR tolerance was obtained as 1.3379 ± 0.2006 (TIR ± 15%). The calibration curve and the parameters derived from the calibration process are presented in the <a href="#app1-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link">Supplementary Material (SM)</a>.</p></section><section id="sec4dot5dot2-toxins-15-00409"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">4.5.2. HPLC-HRFTMS</h4>
<p>Acquisition parameters: They were set to increase analyte response and diminish isobaric ion interference as much as possible. HPLC-HRFTMS analyses were accomplished in an LTQ-Orbitrap Discovery mass spectrometer coupled with a Thermo Scientific Accela ESI-HPLC system. The chromatographic separation was achieved using a TSKgel Amide-80 HILIC-column (150 × 2.0 mm i.d., 5 µm, Tosho, Tokyo, Japan) at 25 °C and a 40.0 min isocratic elution with a mobile phase of CH<sub>3</sub>CO<sub>2</sub>NH<sub>4</sub> 16 mM/CH<sub>3</sub>CN (3:7) pH 5.5. The sample was dissolved in CH<sub>3</sub>COOH (1%). Injection volume: 25 µL. Elution flow: 0.2 mL/min. Scan Event: FTMS. Mode: Full scan: 65–450. Resolution: 30,000. ESI-(+) ion source parameters were set as follows: Capillary temp: 350 °C. Capillary voltage: 26.00, and Source voltage: 4.50 kV.</p>
<p>Data analysis: The accurate mass measured (<em>m</em>) was calculated by averaging the masses detected (<em>m<sub>i</sub></em>) at three different randomly selected peak positions in the extracted ion chromatogram (<a href="#toxins-15-00409-f002" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>). The mass measurement error (or accuracy) was determined by calculating the difference in ppm between the theoretical exact mass (<em>m<sub>a</sub></em>) and <em>m</em> (∆<em>m</em>/<em>z</em>) [<a href="#B56-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B56-toxins-15-00409">56</a>,<a href="#B57-toxins-15-00409" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B57-toxins-15-00409">57</a>] and expressed as <sup>12</sup>C<sub>n</sub> (<sup>13</sup>C<sub>1</sub><sup>12</sup>C<sub>n−1</sub>) ppm.

</h4>
<p>Sample preparation: Samples were concentrated by manually submitting them through a Waters Sep-Pak Plus C<sub>18</sub> Short Cartridge (Part No. WAT020515) to mitigate any possible matrix interferences (<a href="#toxins-15-00409-f007" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>). Analysis conditions: The equipment and separation conditions were the same as in the HPLC-HRMS section, although the scan type was in Selected Reaction Monitoring Mode (SRM). The MS conditions were: parent ion mass (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em>) 320.00, Normalized Collision Energy (eV): 35.0, Acquisition Time (ms): 30.00, and mass range detected (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em>): 320.10→301.50–302.50 and 320.10→161.50–162.50, corresponding to [M + H-H<sub>2</sub>O]<sup>+</sup> and [C<sub>8</sub>H<sub>8</sub>N<sub>3</sub>O]<sup>+</sup> ions, respectively. Resolution: 30,000 and CID: 35%. Data analysis: Molecular formulae and Ring/Double Bond Equivalents (DBE) were computed in Xcalibur V 3.0 by using the exact mass detected. Fragments selected for the analysis met two criteria: they were abundant during fragmentation and did not originate from the same part of the molecule under study.</p></section></section><section id="sec4dot6-toxins-15-00409"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.6.
