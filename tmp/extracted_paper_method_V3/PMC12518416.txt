</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Purpose</h3>
<p id="Par1">To integrate the results from multiple electrophysiological tests, which has the potential to significantly improve outcome predictions in patients with Bell's palsy.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p id="Par2">This retrospective study analyzed 193 patients who were diagnosed with Bell's palsy at our Department of Physical Medicine &amp; Rehabilitation, from January 2020 to December 2022. All patients were followed for at least 6 months, with a mean follow-up duration of 6.8 months (range: 6–9 months). Clinical data, including House-Brackmann (H-B) grade and electrophysiological data from five tests, were analyzed using multiple logistic regression analysis and decision tree analysis to predict outcome at 6 months. The five electrophysiological tests were: electroneurography degeneration index (ENoG DI), compound muscle action potential (CMAP) latency, blink reflex (BR), nerve excitability test (NET), and needle electromyography (nEMG).</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p id="Par3">The decision tree model identified five key predictors of recovery: ENoG DI in the orbicularis oculi, initial H-B grade, interference pattern in orbicularis oculi, NET difference, and CMAP latency in the frontalis. Patients with an ENoG DI &lt; 71.72% and initial H-B grade ≤ 3 had a high probability of complete recovery. For higher ENoG DI values, a NET difference ≥ 4.50 mA and CMAP latency &gt; 3.80 ms predicted incomplete recovery. This analysis led to an overall accuracy of 86.01%.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p id="Par4">This study demonstrated that the combined use of initial H-B grade with the results from multiple electrophysiological results provided reliable outcome predictions in patients with Bell's palsy.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h3>
<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00405-025-09419-4.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Bell’s palsy, Electroneurography, Blink reflex, Nerve excitability test, Needle electromyography, Prognosis</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par5">Bell's palsy is an idiopathic, acute, and peripheral facial neuropathy characterized by unilateral facial weakness or paralysis. It is a relatively common disease, with an annual incidence of 13 to 34 cases per 100,000 people, depending on the population [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>]. The prognosis of these patients is generally favorable, and 80 to 85% of them fully recover following glucocorticoid treatment [<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>]. However, some patients do not fully recover and may experience post-paralysis sequelae. Along with physical challenges like difficulty opening the eyes or making facial expressions, they may develop psychosocial issues that significantly impact quality of life [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>]. Therefore, accurate prediction of the prognosis of patients with Bell's palsy is critical for improving treatment-related decisions and patient counseling.</p>
<p id="Par6">Clinical examinations, clinician-based grading scales, and electrophysiological tests can be utilized to predict recovery and guide the management of these patients [<a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>]. The five major electrophysiological tests are needle electromyography (nEMG), electroneurography (ENoG), compound muscle action potential (CMAP), blink reflex (BR), and the nerve excitability test (NET). Findings from each of the five frequently utilized electrophysiological tests for patients with Bell’s palsy can indicate good or poor prognosis (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>). Additionally, although many previous studies have examined the prognostic value of these tests, these studies have primarily focused on the predictive power individual tests.
</p>
<section class="tw xbox font-sm" id="Tab1"><h3 class="obj_head">Table 1.</h3>
<div class="caption p"><p>Electrophysiological test results that are associated with good prognosis and poor prognosis</p></div>
<div class="tbl-box p" tabindex="0"><table class="content" frame="hsides" rules="groups">

<div class="tw-foot p"><div class="fn" id="_fn_p13"><p><em>BR</em> Blink reflex; <em>CMAP</em> Compound muscle action potential; <em>ENoG DI</em> Electroneurography electroneurography degeneration index; <em>NET</em> Nerve excitability test; <em>nEMG</em> Needle electromyography</p></div></div></section><p id="Par7">Each of the electrophysiological tests employed in this study—ENoG DI, CMAP, BR, NET and nEMG—has known limitations when used in isolation to assess facial nerve damage or predict outcomes. ENoG is a valuable electrophysiological tool for quantitatively assessing the severity of facial nerve injury. However, it has several limitations. Within the first three days after symptom onset, Wallerian degeneration may not be sufficiently progressed, which can result in ENoG values that do not accurately reflect the extent of axonal damage. Even when using a 75% amplitude reduction as the cutoff point, previous studies have reported a sensitivity of 60%, specificity of 79%, and overall accuracy of 73% in predicting defective healing, indicating limited reliability for prognostic purposes [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>]. The latency of the CMAP reflects nerve conduction velocity and may be sensitive to demyelinating lesions; however, its diagnostic utility is limited in axonal injuries. Moreover, in the early stages of nerve damage, changes in CMAP amplitude or latency may not yet be apparent. BR is a simple and useful test that allows for the localization of lesions along the trigeminal and facial nerve pathways, and it is particularly sensitive to brainstem involvement. However, BR abnormalities may also be present in various central nervous system disorders and other pathological conditions, limiting its specificity as a diagnostic indicator in peripheral nerve lesions such as Bell’s palsy [<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>]. The NET is a simple and noninvasive procedure; however, unlike ENoG, it does not allow for the quantitative assessment of the proportion of degenerated nerve fibers. This limitation reduces its utility in accurately predicting clinical outcomes [<a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>]. Needle electromyography (nEMG) is useful for evaluating neuromuscular function, but it is invasive, highly operator-dependent, and limited by its timing; pathological findings typically do not emerge until 10 to 14 days after nerve injury. These individual limitations underscore the rationale for our approach in this study, which integrates multiple electrophysiological tests to develop a more accurate prognostic model. By utilizing the complementary strengths of each modality, we aim to provide a more comprehensive and precise evaluation of facial nerve function. These limitations highlight the insufficiency of relying on single tests for accurate prognostication. Although some studies combine variables such as nEMG, BR, and ENoG, no research has comprehensively analyzed all electrophysiological parameters in Bell's palsy [<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>]. Our study highlights the potential benefit of integrating multiple tests for a comprehensive assessment. It aims to determine whether integrating results from the five electrophysiological tests can enhance the prediction of Bell's palsy outcomes and identify the optimal combination of test results.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data and study population</h3>
<p id="Par8">This retrospective study examined patients who were diagnosed with Bell's palsy at the Department of Physical Medicine &amp; Rehabilitation of our institution, and received medical rehabilitation from January 2020 to December 2022. Patients were included if they were diagnosed with idiopathic, acute facial palsy. Initially, 296 patients were assessed and 103 patients were excluded based on the presence of central nervous system disorders, bilateral facial palsy, history of previous facial palsy, pregnancy, absence of electrophysiological test results, previous receipt of facial botulinum toxin injections, or insufficient follow-up. A total of 193 patients were included in the final analysis. All patients'facial palsy severity was assessed using the House-Brackmann (H-B) grade at the initial visit and six months after symptom onset. The initial H-B grade distribution was as follows: grade II, 13 patients (6.7%); grade III, 113 patients (58.6%); grade IV, 42 patients (21.8%); grade V, 18 patients (9.3%); and grade VI, 7 patients (3.6%). All patients received oral corticosteroids (standard prednisone dosage: 80 mg/d for the first 4 days followed by tapering to 60 mg/d on days 5 and 6, 40 mg/d on days 7 and 8, and 20 mg/d on days 9 and 10) and physical rehabilitation therapy, including facial massage and expression exercises, with instructions for home practice. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Hospital (2024–10-007), with consent waived due to its retrospective nature.</p></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Clinical and electrophysiological assessment</h3>
<p id="Par9">Patient age, sex, and side of facial paralysis were recorded. The severity of facial paralysis was assessed using the H-B grade at the initial visit and at the 6-month follow-up. Outcomes were recorded at 6 months after symptom onset, and patients with complete recovery (H-B grade 1) and incomplete recovery (H-B grade 2–6) were compared according to sex, age at initial visit, and initial H-B grade. Electrophysiological tests, including a nerve conduction study (NCS), BR, NET, and nEMG, were conducted 14 days after the onset of symptoms. The NCS provides values for ENoG and CMAP latency. The nEMG provides values for abnormal spontaneous activity (ASA), motor unit action potential (MUAP) interference pattern grade and amplitude. All electrophysiological tests were conducted using the Nicolet EDX Machine (Natus, USA) in a controlled environment with specific settings and protocols to ensure consistency and accuracy. These tests were performed by two experienced operators to assure reliability and consistency.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Electroneurography electroneurography degeneration index (ENoG DI)</h3>
<p id="Par10">NCS tests were conducted on the bilateral frontalis, orbicularis oculi, nasalis, and orbicularis oris muscles. Recording electrodes were placed on facial muscles, with reference electrodes on the contralateral side and a ground electrode on the shoulder. Preauricular stimulation used a bipolar stimulator (cathode near the anterior tragus, anode between the mandible and mastoid) with an initial current of 30 mA (pulse duration: 0.1 ms) increased to maximal response. Sensitivity was 1 mV/division, sweep speed 20 ms/division, and filter range 20 to 10 kHz [<a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>, <a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>]. This procedure allows measurement of CMAP amplitude (peak to peak in mV) for ENoG and onset latency. The ENoG value indicates the proportion of intact nerve fibers. The ENoG degeneration index (DI), which represents the degree of facial nerve degeneration [<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>, <a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>], was calculated as:</p>

<p id="Par11">BR measurements followed standardized 2-channel guidelines. Electrodes were placed on the orbicularis oculi muscles (active and opposite), side of the nose (reference), and chin (ground). Supraorbital nerve stimulation started at 0 mA and increased to supramaximal levels (pulse duration: 0.2 ms). Sensitivity was 100 µV/division, sweep speed 100 ms/division, and filter range 20 to 10 k Hz [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>]. This test identified two components: the R1 response (evaluated for delays on the stimulated side) and the R2 response (latencies on both the stimulated and opposite sides) [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>].</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Nerve excitability test (NET)</h3>
<p id="Par12">NET recorded the minimal current intensity required to elicit visible facial muscle contractions using rectangular impulses (duration: 0.1 ms, frequency: 1 Hz) applied near the anterior tragus [<a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>]. The minimal current intensities (mA) that caused visible contractions were recorded. The difference between the two sides is an indication of the degree of nerve damage and is related to prognosis.</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Needle electromyography (nEMG)</h3>
<p id="Par13">The nEMG examination used a bipolar needle electrode to assess electrical activity at rest and during voluntary contractions in the frontalis, orbicularis oculi, nasalis, and orbicularis oris muscles. Sensitivity ranged from 20 to 500 µV/division, with a sweep speed of 100 ms/division and a filter range of 100 to 1 k Hz. At rest, the nEMG was used to assess ASA, including fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves (PSWs). Patients were asked to perform maximal voluntary contractions to evaluate the MUAP amplitude and interference pattern [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>, <a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>, <a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>].</p>
<p id="Par14">By convention, the interference pattern is categorized into five grades based on the density of electrical activity during muscle contraction: full interference pattern, indicating dense and complex electrical activity typical in healthy muscles (Grade 5); reduced interference pattern, indicating decreased activation of motor units (Grade 4); discrete activity, indicating that only a few motor units are active and their electrical potentials do not overlap (Grade 3); single voluntary MUAP, indicating that only one motor unit fires during contraction (Grade 2); and no voluntary MUAP, indicating no detection of motor unit activity (Grade 1) [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>].</p></section><section id="Sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="Par15">The relationship of complete and incomplete recovery (based on H-B grade at 6 months) with other variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test (categorical data) or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (continuous data). Multiple logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate relationships of different variables (age, sex, paralysis side) with initial H-B grade after adjustment for confounding. To determine the cut-off value for distinguishing complete and incomplete recovery of the follow-up H-B grade, receiver operating characteristics (ROC) analysis was used to calculate area under the curve (AUC). Decision tree analysis using a classification and regression tree (CART) was also conducted. To reduce bias caused by differences between complete and incomplete recovery, upsampling was applied during the CART analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC) and R version 4.2.0. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered significant.</p></section></section><section id="Sec10"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<p id="Par16">We analyzed the clinical and electrophysiological data of 193 patients with Bell's palsy to identify factors associated with outcome at 6 months. We first divided the patient cohort into a complete recovery group (H-B grade 1, <em>n</em> = 141) and incomplete recovery group (H-B grade 2–6, <em>n</em> = 52) based on measurements at 6 months after symptom onset (Table <a href="#Tab2" class="usa-link">2</a>). These two groups had no significant differences in age, sex, and side of facial paralysis. However, patients whose initial H-B grade was 3 or less were more likely to achieve complete recovery.

</div></div></section><p id="Par20">To further validate our findings, we conducted a subgroup analysis focusing on 67 patients with initial H-B grades exceeding 3 (Supplementary Tables <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">1</a> and <a href="#MOESM1" class="usa-link">2</a>). This additional analysis confirmed the prognostic significance of our key electrophysiological indicators, demonstrating consistent results across different severity levels of facial palsy. We then performed decision tree analysis to identify the combination of variables (electrophysiological test results and other factors) that best predicted patient outcome (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>). The decision tree model identified five key predictors of recovery: ENoG DI in the orbicularis oculi, initial H-B grade, interference pattern in orbicularis oculi, NET difference, and CMAP latency in the frontalis. The root node was the orbicularis oculi ENoG DI with a threshold of 71.72%. Patients with an ENoG DI &lt; 71.72% and initial H-B grade ≤ 3 had a high probability of complete recovery. For higher ENoG DI values, a NET difference ≥ 4.50 mA and CMAP latency &gt; 3.80 ms predicted incomplete recovery. This analysis led to an overall accuracy of 86.01%. We then entered the OR values into an ROC analysis for calculation of sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and AUC (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>). The AUC value of 0.86 suggests that the model is highly reliable for distinguishing between patients likely to experience complete or incomplete recovery. Thus, this result indicates that the integration of multiple electrophysiological and clinical variables in our model led to robust predictions of 6-month outcome in patients with Bell’s palsy.</p>

<p id="Par21">Many studies have used electrophysiological tests to predict the prognosis of patients with facial paralysis, and the five most common tests are ENoG DI, CMAP latency, BR, NET, and nEMG (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>). However, there are no clear guidelines on which tests are most useful in clinical practice. We identified prognostic factors related to 6-month outcome based on electrophysiological test results of patients with Bell’s palsy during the acute phase, and investigated which combination of results rests best predicted outcome using decision tree analysis with a CART model. Decision tree models use an intuitive nonlinear approach to identify predictive variables and their interactions, and provide an easy-to-understand visual representation of these relationships [<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>]. Our results showed that an initial ENoG DI value less than 71.72 in the orbicularis oculi muscle, combined with an initial H-B grade of 3 or lower, was associated with complete recovery at 6 months (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>). Even for patients with an initial H-B grade of 4 or higher, prognosis depended on the orbicularis oculi interference pattern based on EMG. Furthermore, for an initial ENoG DI value of 71.72 or more in the orbicularis oculi, a NET side-to-side difference less than 4.5 and a CMAP latency less than 3.8 in the frontalis muscle were associated with complete recovery. These findings suggest that, while the ENoG DI value is an important predictor, clinicians should consider a combination of variables, such as H-B grade and interference patterns, to make a more comprehensive assessment of prognosis. By incorporating multiple electrodiagnostic measures into the evaluation, clinicians can make more accurate predictions about patient outcomes, even when one single variable, such as the ENoG DI value, may suggest a less favorable prognosis. This approach can provide a more reliable prediction of recovery, guiding treatment decisions in the acute phase of Bell’s palsy.</p>
<p id="Par22">Previous studies have demonstrated that ENoG DI &lt; 90% degeneration rate shows a favorable outcome [<a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>, <a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. However, this study presents an integrated analysis of various electrophysiological variables in conjunction with the initial facial palsy severity. These results suggest that electrophysiological indicators of upper facial muscles, such as the orbicularis oculi and frontalis, may be more sensitive in predicting facial nerve palsy outcomes. These muscles perform delicate and complex motor functions, with motor units characterized by smaller and more intricate structures. This specificity implies differential regeneration mechanisms following nerve damage and presents a new perspective on understanding facial nerve palsy recovery processes. However, these hypotheses remain at an exploratory level and require further in-depth anatomical and electrophysiological research to be refined and validated.</p>
<p id="Par23">ENoG testing involves delivering a supramaximal electrical stimulus at the stylomastoid foramen to activate the facial nerve on the same side. Previous observations indicated that an ENoG DI value of 90% or more was associated with incomplete recovery [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>, <a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>]. Our decision tree analysis confirms that an ENoG DI value of 71.72% or higher predicts incomplete recovery, especially when combined with other measures like NET and CMAP latency.</p>
<p id="Par24">The BR test has diagnostic value in distinguishing between central and peripheral issues, and in identifying the locations of lesions in conditions [<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>]. A 2024 study that analyzed factors also evaluated as prognostic of poor recovery included ENoG DI and BR test [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>]. In this 2024 study, the ipsilateral R1 latency of the BR test was significantly longer in the group with poor prognosis (14.85 ± 1.33 ms). However, in agreement with our results (Table <a href="#Tab3" class="usa-link">3</a>), there were no significant differences in ipsilateral R2 latency and contralateral R2 latency between the good prognosis and poor prognosis groups [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>].</p>
<p id="Par25">Previous studies of NET showed that for patients with facial palsy, a side-to-side NET difference below 3.5 mA suggested a good prognosis, and that a difference of 3.5 mA or more or the absence of facial muscle movement with electrical stimulation of 20 mA predicted poor prognosis and incomplete recovery (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>) [<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>]. We also compared the NET results for the paralyzed and non-paralyzed sides in our complete and incomplete recovery groups. The side-to-side difference was 2.04 ± 2.8 in the complete recovery group and 7.33 ± 5.32 in the incomplete recovery group (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001, Table <a href="#Tab3" class="usa-link">3</a>), consistent with the use of a cut-off value of 3.5 mA in the previous study [<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>].</p>
<p id="Par26">The nEMG is the reliable test for detecting muscle denervation and is a widely accepted standard for diagnostics of facial nerves, but only a few studies have examined its prognostic value [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>]. The nEMG records denervation activity during rest with complete relaxation of the facial muscles as fibrillation potentials or PSWs, and the results indicate ongoing denervation [<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>]. For patients with Bell's palsy, the indicators of poor prognosis related to spontaneous activity include the presence of pathological spontaneous activity, such as fibrillation potentials and PSWs (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>). Although these two potentials generally have the same clinical significance, our analysis of the two waveforms showed that PSWs were better predictors of complete recovery. Although the presence of PSWs and fibrillation potentials can be important indicators of denervation following nerve injury, these two waveforms do not always appear together [<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>]. Additionally, although the presence of fibrillation potential in our study had a positive association with incomplete recovery, this association was not statistically significant (OR, 1.345; 95%CI, 0.57–3.14).</p>
<p id="Par27">Our results also emphasize the importance of MUAP interference patterns during voluntary contraction. A lesion in a facial nerve can decrease the number of normal MUAPs and recruitment during voluntary activity [<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>]. The decrease of MUAPs following acute denervation is associated with a greatly increased ENoG DI, particularly when there is more than 90% damage(ENoG DI ≥ 90%), and there is evidence of a very high specificity for the absence of MUAPs in such cases [<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>]. There is also evidence that a lower grade of volitional nEMG activity indicates poor prognosis [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>]. Several studies showed that the presence of MUAPs, whether reduced or with a full interference pattern, suggested favorable functional recovery consistent with our findings [<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>, <a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>]. Specifically, for low-grade interference (1–3, no voluntary MUAPs, single voluntary MUAPs, and discrete activity) the OR value for incomplete recovery ranged from 2.37 (95%CI, 0.97–5.79) for the frontalis to 5.61 (95%CI, 1.68–18.75) for the nasalis (Table <a href="#Tab3" class="usa-link">3</a>).</p>
<p id="Par28">Our study of electrodiagnostic testing for predicting the outcome of patients with Bell’s palsy had a relatively large sample size, but it also had several limitations. First, the retrospective design and unblinded H-B grading may have introduced observer bias, as evaluators’ prior knowledge could influence assessments. Although the H-B scale is widely used in clinical settings, it has known limitations in terms of inter-rater and intra-rater reliability compared to other grading systems, such as the Sunnybrook Facial Grading System [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>]. Nevertheless, we used the H-B scale in this study because of its clinical familiarity and accessibility, its compatibility with prior literature, and its practicality in categorizing patients for integration with electrophysiological data. Future studies should consider incorporating more diverse and detailed facial function assessment tools to further examine their relationship with electrophysiological findings. Second, conducting the study at a single institution may reduce the external validity of the results, limiting their applicability to other, more heterogeneous populations. Third, because our decision tree algorithm was developed using our sample of patients, a limitation is that the selected variables may change as the sample size increases and as different populations are examined. Finally, our analysis was limited to specific electrodiagnostic tests, and did not include clinical variables such as blood pressure, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia that could also affect the results. Therefore, future studies should include a broader range of clinical variables to validate the model presented here.</p></section><section id="Sec12"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="Par29">This study used decision tree analysis and identified five electrophysiological variables that reliably predicted 6-month outcome in patients with early Bell's palsy. These key factors were the initial ENoG DI value in the orbicularis oculi muscle, the initial H-B grade, the interference pattern in the orbicularis oculi muscle on EMG, the NET difference, and the CMAP latency in the frontalis muscle. The integration of these electrophysiological measures into clinical practice offers significant advantages, allowing for more precise prognostic assessments and treatment plans. By incorporating these combined findings into standard diagnostic protocols, clinicians can refine decision-making in the acute phase of Bell’s palsy. This approach can transform current management practices, shifting focus from single-variable assessments to a more comprehensive evaluation strategy that improves patient care and outcomes.</p></section><section id="Sec120"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h2>
