Abstract

High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) is a prominent analytical tool that characterizes chlorinated disinfection byproducts (Cl-DBPs) in an unbiased manner. Due to the diversity of chemicals, complex background signals, and the inherent analytical fluctuations of HRMS, conventional isotopic pattern (37Cl/35Cl), mass defect, and direct molecular formula (MF) prediction are insufficient for accurate recognition of the diverse Cl-DBPs in real environmental samples. This work proposes a novel strategy to recognize Cl-containing chemicals based on machine learning. Our hierarchical machine learning framework has two random forest-based models: the first layer is a binary classifier to recognize Cl-containing chemicals, and the second layer is a multiclass classifier to annotate the number of Cl present. This model was trained using ∼1.4 million distinctive MFs from PubChem. Evaluated on over 14,000 unique MFs from NIST20, this machine learning model achieved 93.3% accuracy in recognizing Cl-containing MFs (Cl-MFs) and 92.9% accuracy in annotating the number of Cl for Cl-MFs. Furthermore, the trained model was integrated into ChloroDBPFinder, a standalone R package for the streamlined processing of LC-HRMS data and annotating both known and unknown Cl-containing compounds. Tested on existing Cl-DBP data sets related to aspartame chlorination in tap water, our ChloroDBPFinder efficiently extracted 159 Cl-containing DBP features and tentatively annotated the structures of 10 Cl-DBPs via molecular networking. In another application of a chlorinated humic substance, ChloroDBPFinder extracted 79 high-quality Cl-DBPs and tentatively annotated six compounds. In summary, our proposed machine learning strategy and the developed ChloroDBPFinder provide an advanced solution to identifying Cl-containing compounds in nontargeted analysis of water samples. It is freely available on GitHub (https://github.com/HuanLab/ChloroDBPFinder).

Introduction

The disinfection of drinking water using chemical agents, such as chlorine and chloramine, is a common practice employed in the drinking water treatment process to eradicate pathogenic bacteria, viruses, and protozoa. A significant concern arising from the disinfection process is the occurrence of chlorination reactions with natural organic matter, forming a diverse array of disinfection byproducts (DBPs). , Epidemiological studies suggest a potential association between chronic exposure to DBPs and adverse health effects, including bladder cancer, developmental effects, and miscarriages. − More than 700 DBPs have been currently identified; the majority contain at least one chlorine atom, termed chlorinated DBPs (Cl-DBPs). − Despite this knowledge, the specific Cl-DBPs responsible for the toxicity remain unclear. Research suggests that unidentified Cl-DBPs might contribute significantly to the toxicity observed, underscoring the urgent need for innovative technologies to identify and analyze them within water samples. ,

Various analytical tools have been developed for characterizing Cl-DBPs. , Among them, high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) provides accurate mass and isotope information and has demonstrated its crucial role in Cl-DBP analysis. In particular, liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) has highlighted the possibility of achieving nontargeted detection of Cl-DBPs. With excellent sensitivity and throughput, LC-HRMS has been widely applied to discover novel Cl-DBPs, investigation of chlorination pathways, and transformation studies of Cl-DBPs. − For instance, LC-HRMS has been used to investigate the formation of novel Cl-DBPs from specific precursors, including aspartame, resveratrol, halohydroxybenzonitriles, and indoles, during laboratory reactions as well as in authentic water samples.

Besides the potential of HRMS analysis, a critical challenge lies in effectively recognizing the Cl-DBPs from the raw LC-HRMS data. Currently, there are two primary strategies for the nontargeted discovery of Cl-DBPs. The first strategy leverages the distinct MS patterns of the Cl element, including (1) the negative mass defect, which represents the mass difference between the exact mass and nominal mass for Cl-containing compounds, , and (2) the unique isotope intensity pattern of 35Cl/37Cl (Table S1). − However, given the diverse DBP chemical structures, relying solely on mass defect or a single isotope ratio cutoff is inaccurate and not sufficient for capturing all Cl-DBPs.

The second common strategy for the nontargeted discovery of Cl-DBPs relies on molecular formula (MF) assignment, followed by isotope pattern checking. In this approach, the seven golden rules is generally used to deduce plausible MFs from the measured accurate mass and isotope pattern. This approach can be achieved using software created by academic researchers and MS vendors. ,, For example, halogen extraction code (Table S1) assigns MFs using the MFAssignR package followed by automatic Cl isotope pattern checking. However, due to signal fluctuation and limited instrumental resolution, the accuracy of the assigned MFs can sometimes be problematic, leading to erroneous MFs and misinterpretations. Although ultra-HRMS technologies like Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance (FTICR) MS and high-end Orbitrap MS systems can significantly enhance the accuracy of MF assignments, ,, their widespread integration into routine laboratory analysis is impeded by their high cost and demanding maintenance requirements.

To address the existing challenges associated with identifying Cl-containing compounds, we leveraged the power of machine learning to directly discern Cl-DBPs from their experimental isotope patterns. In contrast to the existing methods, machine learning introduces a data-driven decision-making approach. This approach capitalizes on the holistic information encoded within isotopic patterns, significantly reducing bias and human-induced errors. As exemplified by the work of Böcker and his team, machine learning models can be trained to detect the presence of rare or uncommon elements. By drawing inspiration from this achievement, we have adapted a machine learning strategy to facilitate the recognition of Cl-DBPs. Additionally, we integrated this hierarchical model to create a streamlined bioinformatic platform for automated (1) recognition of Cl-containing features, (2) alignment, (3) missing value imputation, and (4) compound annotation. Notably, molecular networking analyses in both spectral similarity and reaction connectivity are implemented to expedite the structure confirmation of the unknown Cl-DBPs.

Methods and Materials

LC-HRMS Analysis and Data Processing

Suwannee River humic acids were chlorinated to simulate the water chlorination treatment. Nontargeted LC-HRMS analysis was performed using an Agilent 1290 series LC system coupled to the Sciex X500R QTOF mass spectrometer (Concord, Ontario, Canada). Both the method blank and chlorinated humic acid were injected in triplicate at seven different volumes (2, 4, 6, 10, 13, 16, and 20 μL). These serial injection volumes were used to evaluate the confidence of the extracted chemical features. The mass spectrometer was operated in the electrospray positive ionization mode, with MS1 and MS/MS spectra collected in the information-dependent acquisition mode. Further details about sample preparation and data collection are provided in Text S1. All LC-HRMS data sets (Sciex wiff2) were first converted into the mzML format using MSConvert (version 3.0.22035), as detailed in Text S2. A desktop computer (Intel i9-10900 CPU@2.8 GHz with 10 cores and 32 GB memory; Windows 10; 64-bit operating system) was used to process the data. The plots of molecular networking were drawn using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1). The charge distribution of the natural bond orbital for chemicals was calculated using WebMO with access to the Gaussian program, as detailed in Text S3.

Simulating Experimental MS Spectra

To simulate the experimental MS spectra from a given MF, we first calculated its theoretical monoisotopic mass and the theoretical isotope pattern using the isopattern­() function in the enviPat R package. Considering mass resolution limitations, we grouped adjacent isotopic peaks with the same nominal mass (Text S4). Next, to simulate the experimental MS spectra, we applied a random mass error [error ∼ U(0, 5), ppm] to the masses of each peak and random intensity fluctuations [fluctuation ∼ N(0, δ2), δ = 11.8% for M + 1, δ = 15.8% for M + 2, +3, +4] to the intensity of each peak (Text S5). The fluctuation values were chosen by referring to 9262 experimental MS spectra from 585 chemical features generated using a Sciex X500R QTOF (Text S6). These simulated MS spectra were then used for various purposes, including the evaluation of MF assignment accuracy and the validation of machine learning models.

Evaluating the Performance of MF Assignment from the Accurate Mass Measurement

Unique MFs were extracted from the NIST20 database (https://www.nist.gov/programs-projects/tandem-mass-spectral-library). These MFs were then filtered using two criteria: (1) restricting the elemental composition to be within seven elements, including C, H, N, O, P, S, and Cl and (2) ensuring that the element ratios were within a reasonable range as derived from seven golden rules (Text S7). After filtering, 10,211 unique MFs were retained, including 8817 non-Cl and 1394 Cl-containing MFs (Cl-MFs). Next, the abovementioned workflow was applied to generate the simulated experimental MS spectra for MF assignment and accuracy evaluation. To assign MFs, the calcMF­() function in the MassTools R package was applied. In another test involving the consideration of the isotope pattern, MF candidates from the initial MF prediction were ranked based on their isotope similarity to the query MFs. Details of the MF assignment are provided in Text S8.

Machine Learning Model Training and Evaluation

To prepare the machine learning training data, the MFs of Cl and non-Cl compounds were extracted from the PubChem database. Several criteria were applied to select MFs. For example, we restrict the elemental composition to be within seven elements, including C, H, N, O, P, S, and Cl. Additionally, given that polychlorides with more than five Cl are less common in environmental water, the maximum Cl allowed was set to five. Details can be found in Text S9. Next, the experimental m/z and isotope patterns of these extracted MFs were simulated for model training. To improve the model robustness, we added a mass error (error∼ U (0, 5), ppm) and an intensity fluctuation (fluctuation ∼ N (0, δ2), δ = 8%) to each peak.

The training and testing of random forest-based machine learning models were performed in R (ver. 4.3.0) using the randomForest R package. To train the binary classifier that predicts whether Cl exists in the HRMS data, we selected all 386,420 Cl-MFs and randomly sampled an equal number of non-Cl MFs without replacement from the total pool of 968,442 non-Cl MFs. Six machine learning features were engineered from the masses and intensities of their M, M + 1, and M + 2 peaks (Text S10). To train the multiclass classifier that predicts the number of Cl in the Cl-MFs, we used 185 K mono-Cl, 118 K di-Cl, and 84 K multi-Cl (n = 3, 4 or 5). 11 machine learning features were engineered from the masses and intensities of their M, M + 1, M + 2, M + 3, and M + 4 peaks (Text S11). Before fitting the models, the entire data set was split into 80% training and 20% testing data. Details of model training and tuning are provided in Text S12. Furthermore, the model was first validated using the simulated experimental MS spectra from all unique MFs in the NIST20 database, as detailed in Text S13.

Results and Discussion

Hidden Complexity of Recognizing Cl-DBPs in HRMS Analysis

The first challenge of recognizing Cl-DBPs in HRMS analysis is the structural diversity and multitude of possible Cl-DBPs in the environment. Although natural Cl isotopes (35Cl/37Cl) exhibit a distinct intensity pattern, this pattern becomes obscured by M and M + 2 isotopic peaks in HRMS analysis, especially when dealing with a large number of MFs. To demonstrate this, we investigated ∼1.4 million unique MFs sourced from PubChem, comprising 386,420 Cl-MFs and 968,442 non-Cl MFs. We graphed the theoretical intensity ratios of their M + 2 isotopic peaks against monoisotopic M peaks. As shown in Figure A, 15% (145,276 out of 968,442) of the non-Cl MFs overlap with Cl-MFs in the intensity ratio range of 0.32 to 0.99. Consequently, distinguishing Cl- and non-Cl-containing compounds solely based on a fixed intensity ratio cutoff, such as 0.30, is unfeasible.

Additionally, we investigated the efficacy of using a mass defect to distinguish Cl-containing features from non-Cl chemical features. Using a typical LC-HRMS data set derived from a water sample, we plotted the mass defect for non-Cl and Cl-containing chemical features across different m/z values. As shown in Figure C, Cl-containing features partially overlap with non-Cl-containing features in the mass defect plot. This overlap can be attributed to other elements, such as C and O, compensating for the negative mass defect caused by Cl. A similar observation was reported previously in the HaloSelect study. In that study, the authors did a more comprehensive study on all compounds in PubChem and concluded that the overlap is more significant between nonhalogenated compounds and Cl + Br < 3 compounds. Thus, relying solely on a mass defect to recognize Cl-DBPs is difficult in nontargeted analysis, underscoring the need for a more effective and automated approach.

Furthermore, the inherent analytical variation in HRMS analysis poses another significant challenge in accurately recognizing Cl-containing compounds. The conventional workflow of recognizing Cl-DBPs starts from MF assignment using the measured monoisotopic m/z values. However, MF assignment can be incorrect due to errors in the m/z measurement, resulting in misleading Cl recognition. To demonstrate this, we selected 10,211 unique MFs from the NIST database and introduced 0–5 ppm random mass errors to mimic real experimental conditions. Our analysis revealed that the percentage of top-1 correctness was only 25.6 and 13.0% for non-Cl and Cl-MFs (Figure , black curves), respectively. Notably, among the wrong predictions for non-Cl MFs, 42.1% (2758 out of 6557) were mistakenly assigned as Cl-MFs; these mistakes would lead to false positive recognition of Cl-DBPs in downstream data interpretation. Conversely, among the false predictions for Cl-MFs, 60.7% (736 out of 1213) were mistakenly labeled as non-Cl, resulting in a loss of potential Cl-DBPs.

In practical scenarios, additional information such as isotope patterns can enhance the accuracy of the MF assignment. However, experimental isotope distributions also fluctuate. For instance, we checked the experimental isotope pattern of 9262 MS1 spectra from 585 high-quality chemical features in LC-HRMS analysis (Text S6). Specifically, we observed increased median relative standard deviations of the intensity ratios for different isotope peaks: 11.8, 15.8, 20.1, and 28.6% for M + 1, M + 2, M + 3, and M + 4, respectively (Figure B). After applying these experimental fluctuations to the theoretical isotope patterns, the accuracy can be improved to only 63.4 and 69.9% (Figure , blue curves), respectively. In short, direct MF assignment from the experimental MS spectra is neither efficient nor accurate for discovering Cl-DBPs due to the analytical variation.

Machine Learning-Guided Recognition of Cl-Containing Features

To address the issues of existing solutions, we introduce a novel machine learning strategy to directly predict whether Cl exists in the experimental HRMS data. In particular, the pattern of Cl-containing MS spectra was learned using ∼1.4 million unique MFs, including 968,442 non-Cl and 386,420 Cl-MFs (Figure A,B). Notably, our machine learning pipeline operates in a hierarchical framework: the first layer, a binary classifier, recognizes whether Cl exists in the HRMS data using M, M + 1, and M + 2 peaks (Figure C); if Cl exists, the second layer is a multiclass classifier that predicts the number of Cl atoms utilizing M, M + 1, M + 2, M + 3, and M + 4 peaks (Figure D). This hierarchical design is crucial because non-Cl compounds typically lack M + 3 and M + 4 isotopic peaks or exhibit low abundances in M + 3 and M + 4. However, in real experimental environmental samples, coeluting compounds may incidentally share the same m/z values as the M + 3 and M + 4 peaks of non-Cl compounds. By exclusively relying on M, M + 1, and M + 2 to predict the existence of Cl, this design mitigates the risk of mistakenly incorporating coeluting compounds as M + 3 and M + 4 peaks into the isotope pattern of non-Cl compounds during the analysis of the experimental HRMS data. A more detailed description of the model training result can be found in Text S14.

The trained models were externally tested on the simulated experimental MS spectra of 14,399 unique MFs of chemicals in the NIST20 database, containing 2454 Cl-containing and 11,945 non-Cl MFs. The binary classifier demonstrated an overall accuracy of 93.3% in determining the presence of Cl, correctly identifying 13,431 out of 14,399 MFs for their respective classes (Table S2A). Specifically, it accurately identified 99.1% (2433 out of 2454) of Cl-MFs and 92.1% (10,998 out of 11,945) of non-Cl MFs. It should be noted that 7.9% of the non-Cl MFs was falsely identified as Cl-MFs due to the presence of uncommon elements such as Br. For the multiclass classifier, the overall accuracy was 92.9%, correctly classifying 2279 out of 2454 Cl-MFs to their corresponding classes (Table S2B). In particular, the correct percentages for mono-, di-, and multi-Cl were 92.5% (1630 out of 1763), 93.5% (517 out of 553), and 95.7% (132 out of 138), respectively. Details of the validation results are provided in Text S15. Notably, addressing the false annotations contributed by Br will be a future research focus to enable a more comprehensive exploration of halogenated DBPs. In real applications, the ability to recognize Cl-containing compounds also depends on the quality of the experimental data. In the scenarios where the M + 3 and M + 4 peaks are absent due to low-intensity monoisotopic peaks, the second layer of the hierarchical model will not work as it relies on the information from M + 3 and M + 4 to make a prediction. In those cases, the first layer, which distinguishes between Cl-containing and non-Cl compounds, remains effective.

ChloroDBPFinder

We further extended the developed hierarchical machine learning framework to create ChloroDBPFinder, an R package to recognize and prioritize Cl-containing compounds in nontargeted LC-HRMS analysis. The schematic workflow of ChloroDBPFinder is shown in Figure . It contains four essential modules, as detailed in Text S16, including (1) extraction of Cl-containing chemical features, (2) alignment, (3) missing value imputation, and (4) compound annotation through spectral library search and molecular networking analysis. The initial step involves peak-picking for chemical features. This can be accomplished either in ChloroDBPFinder using the embedded XCMS R package or other data processing programs. The extracted chemical features are subjected to the isotope pattern evaluation. Compared to other programs, ChloroDBPFinder incorporates three unique features to further enhance its Cl-DBP recognition accuracy. First, in the investigation of MS isotope patterns, our algorithm bypasses the traditional peak-picking algorithms to mitigate the risk of failing to pick up the isotope chromatographic peaks by automated peak-picking algorithms, , particularly for low abundant M + 1, M + 2, M + 3, and M + 4 isotopes. Instead, isotopic peaks are recognized directly from the raw MS1 scans, distinguishing ChloroDBPFinder from many existing Cl-extraction software approaches. Furthermore, to minimize the impact of signal fluctuation on isotope patterns, the MS spectra for each chemical feature were averaged across the whole chromatographic peak (step 1 in Figure ). Second, a machine learning algorithm was used to determine the presence of Cl, as opposed to relying on a hard cutoff of the IntM+2/IntM ratio. Third, a suite of bioinformatic algorithms was incorporated to filter out false positive chemical features, including salt adducts, natural isotopes, and in-source fragments. , These meticulous design choices contribute to ChloroDBPFinder’s robustness in accurate Cl-DBPs recognition, as demonstrated in the performance comparison with two other published programs (Text S17).

In addition to recognizing Cl-containing features, ChloroDBPFinder offers comprehensive solutions to facilitate compound annotation (step 4 in Figure ). It incorporates a spectral library search function, enabling automated calculation of spectral similarity using the dot product between acquired experimental tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) data and a reference library of MS/MS spectra. Furthermore, ChloroDBPFinder integrates molecular networking analysis to deduce potential structures of unknown compounds based on known chemicals. , Notably, ChloroDBPFinder employs two distinct approaches in molecular networking: MS/MS spectral similarity networking using GNPS-based MS/MS similarity and reaction networking. , This dual methodology is grounded in two fundamental principles: first, MS/MS similarity serves as a reliable indicator of structural resemblance among chemicals, and second, chemicals within a given system often undergo various reactions and transformations involving the addition or removal of specific chemical moieties. Consequently, ChloroDBPFinder constructs both networking and integrated networking. The integrated networking emphasizes connections where both criteria, high MS/MS spectral similarity and plausible chemical transformation, are met. Additionally, ChloroDBPFinder enables users to upload a table of identified reference chemicals, which can be either non-Cl- or Cl-containing compounds. These reference chemicals are then used in molecular networking analysis, providing valuable insights into structural similarities and reaction transformations. Given the same data structure of QTOF, FTICR, and Orbitrap-based LC–MS analyses, the developed ChloroDBPFinder can also be applied to process and recognize Cl-containing compounds in the nontargeted data generated by using FTICR and Orbitrap MS systems.

Proof-of-Principle Applications

ChloroDBPFinder was first tested using an existing Cl-DBP data set from a previous LC-HRMS study of the addition of aspartame (APM), a widely used artificial sweetener, into authentic tap water. Eight chlorinated derivatives were unintentionally formed from APM. The same data set was reprocessed using ChloroDBPFinder. Among 1294 chemical features, our hierarchical machine learning model recognized 326 Cl-containing features (Figure A). After the elimination of false positives, including 97 salt adducts, 53 natural isotopes, and 17 in-source fragment ions, 159 high-quality Cl-DBP features remained. Through manual checking, all high-quality Cl-DBP features were found to possess good chromatographic peak shapes and diversity in chlorides (Figure B). They also display a wide range of m/z values (154.0418–949.1429 Da), retention times (15.77–40.80 min), and intensities (3.24 × 103–3.01 × 106), highlighting the variety within Cl-DBPs. In comparison to the manual method, ChloroDBPFinder not only accurately identified all eight Cl-containing features from the previous study, annotating them with the correct number of Cl atoms, but also discovered 151 additional Cl-containing DBP features. Notably, the manual method required a day for processing, but ChloroDBPFinder achieved the task in just 12 min, as detailed in Text S18. In another application, we tested ChloroDBPFinder on another Cl-DBPs data set that was generated by mixing ascorbic acid with chlorinated APM in tap water. As illustrated in Figure S1, only 40 Cl-DBPs were extracted by ChloroDBPFinder in the sample with ascorbic acid. This significantly decreased number is consistent with the fact that ascorbic acid is an efficient reducing reagent to quench chlorinated chemicals and reduce the number of Cl-DBPs.

Besides the recognition of Cl-containing DBP features, we also demonstrated molecular networking analysis using ChloroDBPFinder on the structure propagation of newly discovered Cl-DBPs, as detailed in Text S19. Along with the eight reported Cl-DBPs, APM and two degradation products were included in the molecular networking analysis to serve as references (Table S3). In the reaction networking, we tentatively assigned MFs for 22 extracted Cl-containing DBP features (Text S20). In the integrated networking, 83 pairwise connections were established (Figure C). 22 of these pairwise connections are direct links between reference compounds and unknown Cl-DBPs. Based on the reference-unknown links, MFs and structures of 10 newly discovered Cl-DBPs were tentatively annotated (Table S4). One example shown is between reference 7 (m/z 284.0690, RT 28.77 min, [C13H14ClNO4 + H] +) and unknown feature 191 (m/z 351.9914, RT 31.77 min) to illustrate the structure propagation in Figure D. The mass shift of +67.9224 Da in feature 191 indicates the substitution reaction of two hydrogens by two Cl atoms in reference 7. Combined with the MS/MS spectra (Figure E,F) and the charge distribution of the natural bond orbital of reference 7, we proposed that the two Cl atoms are located either on the benzyl group or the α carbon site connected to the benzyl group. Details are provided in Text S21.

ChloroDBPFinder was also applied to analyze a set of chlorinated humic acid samples analyzed in triplicate at seven different injection volumes. Using ChloroDBPFinder, we identified 97 Cl-containing features. After removal of 18 features whose average intensity was less than twice that of the method blank analysis, 79 Cl-containing features (81.4%) were retained. We further correlate the series of injection volumes with the MS signal intensities of the 79 Cl-containing features to assess their quantitative quality. As illustrated in Figure A, all 79 features demonstrated a strong correlation (mean correlation coefficient: 0.98). An exemplary feature illustrating this trend is presented in Figure B. These findings strengthen our confidence that the Cl-containing features detected by ChloroDBPFinder are, with a high likelihood, true chemical entities. In addition to evaluating the quantity and quality of the detected Cl-containing features, we utilized the spectral library search function in ChloroDBPFinder to annotate the Cl-containing features above. When cross-referenced with the NIST20 spectra database, six features received tentative annotations (with an MS/MS match number ≥4 and a dot product score ≥0.7): chlorzoxazone, 7-chloroquinolin-4-ol, 5-chloroquinolin-8-ol, 2-chloro-3-quinolinecarboxylic acid, quinoclamine, and 5,7-dichloro-8-quinolinol (or their structural isomers of similar MS/MS patterns). Upon manual review, we observed that the experimental MS1 and MS/MS spectra for these six annotations aligned with their theoretical counterparts (Figures C and S2).

Conclusions

This work develops a hierarchical machine learning framework to precisely recognize Cl-containing compounds from HRMS data. Trained with ∼1.4 million unique MFs, this machine learning strategy achieved high accuracy in recognizing Cl-containing features from HRMS data. Furthermore, this machine learning strategy was incorporated into a standalone R package, ChloroDBPFinder, enabling (1) the automatic processing of the large-scale nontargeted LC-HRMS data, (2) recognition of Cl-containing chemical features, and (3) annotation of both known and unknown Cl-DBPs via direct database spectral matching and molecular networking strategies. Tested in proof-of-principle applications, ChloroDBPFinder achieved more efficient data processing and discovered additional highly likely Cl-containing DBP features. It is also worth noting that ChloroDBPFinder can be widely applied to recognize Cl-containing compounds and metabolites in the HRMS analysis of other environmental and biological samples, representing important progress in the discovery of known and unknown Cl-containing compounds.
