</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Although recent studies have revealed an involvement of hippocampal interneurons in learning the association among time-separated events, its underlying cellular mechanisms remained not fully clarified. Here, we combined multichannel recording and optogenetics to elucidate how the hippocampal parvalbumin-expressing interneurons (PV-INs) support associative learning. To address this issue, we trained the mice (both sexes) to learn hippocampus-dependent trace eyeblink conditioning (tEBC) in which they associated a light flash conditioned stimulus (CS) with a corneal air puff unconditioned stimuli (US) separated by a 250 ms time interval. We found that the hippocampal PV-INs exhibited learning-associated sustained activity at the early stage of tEBC acquisition. Moreover, the PV-IN sustained activity was positively correlated with the occurrence of conditioned eyeblink responses at the early learning stage. Suppression of the PV-IN sustained activity impaired the acquisition of tEBC, whereas the PV-IN activity suppression had no effect on the acquisition of delay eyeblink conditioning, a hippocampus-independent learning task. Learning-associated augmentation in the excitatory pyramidal cell-to-PVIN drive may contribute to the formation of PV-IN sustained activity. Suppression of the PV-IN sustained activity disrupted hippocampal gamma but not theta band oscillation during the CS-US interval period. Gamma frequency (40 Hz) activation of the PV-INs during the CS-US interval period facilitated the acquisition of tEBC. Our current findings highlight the involvement of hippocampal PV-INs in tEBC acquisition and reveal insights into the PV-IN activity kinetics which are of key importance for the hippocampal involvement in associative learning.</p>
<p><strong>SIGNIFICANCE STATEMENT</strong> The cellular mechanisms underlying associative learning have not been fully clarified. Previous studies focused on the involvement of hippocampal pyramidal cells in associative learning, whereas the activity and function of hippocampal interneurons were largely neglected. We herein demonstrated the hippocampal PV-INs exhibited learning-associated sustained activity, which was required for the acquisition of tEBC. Furthermore, we showed evidence that the PV-IN sustained activity might have arisen from the learning-associated augmentation in excitatory pyramidal cell-to-PVIN drive and contributed to learning-associated augmentation in gamma band oscillation during tEBC acquisition. Our findings provide more mechanistic understanding of the cellular mechanisms underlying the hippocampal involvement in associative learning.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> associative learning, hippocampus, interneuron, mouse, multichannel recording, trace conditioning</p></section></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Learning to associate two events that occur separately in time is a critical function of brain networks (<a href="#B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B43">Pilkiw and Takehara-Nishiuchi, 2018</a>). Among the networks, the hippocampus is believed to be a key structure as lesion of the dorsal hippocampus produces profound deficits in associative learning (<a href="#B50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B50">Solomon et al., 1986</a>; <a href="#B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B39">Moyer et al., 1990</a>; <a href="#B63" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B63">Weiss et al., 1999</a>; <a href="#B58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B58">Tseng et al., 2004</a>).</p>
<p>By performing multichannel recordings in the dorsal hippocampus, researchers have found that a sizable proportion of hippocampal pyramidal (PYR) cells display sustained activity spanning the time gap between time-separated stimuli (<a href="#B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34">McEchron and Disterhoft, 1997</a>; <a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">Hattori et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29">Klee et al., 2021</a>). In addition, calcium imaging studies have revealed that a number of hippocampal PYRs can exhibit sequential firing activity in the time gap between time-separated stimuli (<a href="#B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B37">Modi et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38">Mount et al., 2021</a>). The sustained and/or sequential firing of hippocampal PYRs enables the hippocampus to bridge the time gap between time-separated stimuli, which is hypothesized to support associative learning (<a href="#B60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B60">Wallenstein et al., 1998</a>; <a href="#B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27">Kitamura et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B26">Kitamura, 2017</a>). However, a recent study has shown that disinhibition of the hippocampal PYRs lead to great sustained activity but impairment in associative learning (<a href="#B70" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B70">Zhang et al., 2021</a>). Likewise, overactivity of hippocampal PYRs has been demonstrated to produce adverse effects on associative learning (<a href="#B30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30">Koh et al., 2016</a>). These findings indicate that additional mechanisms beyond the hippocampal PYR activity may be involved in associative learning.</p>
<p>Trace eyeblink conditioning (tEBC) is a hippocampus-dependent associative leaning task (<a href="#B62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B62">Weiss and Disterhoft, 2011</a>), which requires time-separated presentations of a conditioned stimulus (CS) and a reinforcing unconditioned stimulus (US). Recently it has been demonstrated that a number of hippocampal interneurons show increased firing responses to the CS presentations, and optogenetic suppression of the increased firing of hippocampal interneurons impairs the acquisition of tEBC (<a href="#B70" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B70">Zhang et al., 2021</a>). Moreover, there is an increase in the intrinsic excitability of hippocampal interneurons after tEBC training (<a href="#B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B36">McKay et al., 2013</a>). Along this line, genetic elimination of γCamKII subunit in the hippocampal interneurons reduced the ability to learn associative fear responses in mice (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">He et al., 2021</a>). These findings indicate a critical involvement of hippocampal interneurons in associative learning. Considering the diversity of hippocampal interneurons, however, the question remains about the role of distinct subtype of hippocampal interneurons in associative learning.</p>
<p>The parvalbumin (PV)-expressing cells consist of the largest proportion of interneurons in the dorsal hippocampus (<a href="#B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42">Pelkey et al., 2017</a>). It has been demonstrated that the hippocampal PV interneurons (PV-INs) receive strong excitatory inputs from the PYRs (<a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">Gulyás et al., 1993</a>; <a href="#B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12">English et al., 2017</a>); and vice versa, the hippocampal PV-INs innervate the soma and proximal dendrites and strongly control the outputs of neighboring PYRs (<a href="#B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23">Hu et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2">Amilhon et al., 2015</a>). Consequently, the PV-INs are in a good position to interact with the PYRs to influence hippocampal oscillatory states (e.g., theta and gamma band oscillation; <a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">Antonoudiou et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#B51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B51">Strüber et al., 2022</a>), which are highly correlated with associative learning (<a href="#B48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B48">Shearkhani and Takehara-Nishiuchi, 2013</a>; <a href="#B40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B40">Nokia and Wikgren, 2014</a>). Accordingly, the dysfunction of PYR-to-PVIN interaction has been associated with impairment in learning associative fear responses (<a href="#B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15">Fuchs et al., 2007</a>; <a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">He et al., 2021</a>). The above evidence indicates that the PV-IN is a candidate subtype of hippocampal interneuron critically involved in associative learning. Yet it remains unclear how the hippocampal PV-INs respond to time-separated stimuli and how they interact with the neighboring PYRs to support associative learning.</p>
<p>To address these issues, we measured and manipulated the firing activity of hippocampal PV-INs in mice during a tEBC task. Our results establish a causal relationship between the learning-associated hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity with the acquisition of tEBC. Moreover, our results shed new light on how the hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity supports the acquisition of tEBC.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Subjects</h3>
<p>All experiments were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Army Medical University and followed the National Institutes of Health <em>Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals</em>. Mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory and maintained in the C57BL/6J background (<em>n</em> = 70, 3–5 months old, both sexes). The following genotypes of mice were used: PV-Cre (B6.129p2-<em>Pvalb<sup>tm1(cre)Arbr/</sup></em>J, stock #017320) and Ai40D (stock #021188). Before the experiment and between the recording sessions, mice were individually housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle with lights on at 8:00 A.M. All experiments were conducted during the light phase of the cycle.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Surgical procedures for multichannel recording and optical fiber implant</h3>
<p>The mice were positioned in a brain stereotaxic apparatus under isoflurane anesthesia. A pair of stainless steel wires (bare diameter, 76 µm; insulated diameter, 140 µm; catalog #791000, A-M. Systems) were subcutaneously passed through the left upper orbicularis oculi muscle (OOM). A craniotomy was performed in the skull above the hippocampus. With the help of a stereotaxic arm, a diode-tetrode assembly was implanted into the area above the left dorsal hippocampus at the following coordinates (AP, −1.9 mm, ML, −1.6 mm, DV, −0.8 mm relative to the bregma). Fabrication of the diode-tetrode assembly has been previously described in detail (<a href="#B67" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B67">Zhang et al., 2019</a>). Briefly, a 200 µm optic fiber (catalog #FT200EMT, Thorlabs) was combined with the tetrode array. The laser power was measured with an optical power meter (catalog #PM100D, Thorlabs) before the surgery. Two stainless steel screws were secured to the skull above the cerebellum to serve as ground and reference, respectively. Screws and headstage were fixed in place using C&amp;B Metabond luting cement (Parkell) and dental cement. Low-viscosity silicone (Kwik-Cast, World Precision Instruments) was applied to cover the craniotomy. The mice recovered in their home cage for 1 week before further experimental manipulation. During the recovery, the diode-tetrode assembly was moved down (∼35 µm/d) until it reached the pyramidal cell layer characterized by large-amplitude sharp wave ripples (SWRs).</p>
<p>To optogenetically manipulate the activity of hippocampal PV-INs during tEBC training, two optical fibers (catalog #FT200EMT, Thorlabs) were implanted into bilateral dorsal hippocampus (AP, −1.9 mm, ML, ±1.6 mm, DV, −1.0 mm) of virus-injected PV-Cre mice. The fiber implantation was performed 3–4 weeks after viral injection. After the surgery, the mice were individually housed and were allowed to recover for 1 week.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Stereotaxic virus injection for behavioral test</h3>
<p>We injected adeno-associated virus (AAV)2-CAG-FLEX-ArchT-GFP for neuronal inhibition, AAV2-Ef1a-DIO- ChR2(h134R)-mCherry for neuronal activation, and AAV2-Ef1a-DIO-EGFP or AAV2-Ef1a-DIO-mCherry for control. The virus was obtained from OBiO and was injected into bilateral dorsal hippocampus of PV-Cre mice according to the mouse brain atlas (<a href="#B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13">Franklin and Paxinos, 2008</a>). The virus was injected by a Nanoject III injector (Drummond Scientific). The coordinates and volumes for virus injection in bilateral dorsal hippocampus were as follows: sites 1 and 2, bregma, AP −1.9 mm, ML ± 1.25 mm, DV −1.10 mm, volume, 140 nl per site; sites 3 and 4, bregma, AP −1.9 mm, ML ± 1.75 mm, DV −1.25 mm, volume, 140 nl per site.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Eyeblink conditioning experiments</h3>
<p>Following post-surgery recovery, the mice were habituated to the experimental chamber and preamplifier plug-in manipulation for 2 d. During the habituation, the mice could freely move in a light- and sound-attenuated chamber.</p>
<p>Following habituation, the mice (<em>n</em> = 86) received tEBC training for 5 consecutive days. The CS was a 150 ms blue light, which was delivered by an LED mounted on the headstage. The US was a 100 ms cornea air puff, which was delivered via a blunted 27 gauge needle placed ∼5 mm away from the left eye. A CS was followed by a US with a 250 ms trace interval. The daily training consisted of 100 CS-US paired presentation trials with an average intertrial interval of 23 s (randomly varied between 18 and 28 s). Pseudo-conditioned mice (<em>n</em> = 8) received 100 CSs and 100 USs per training day (mean intertrial interval = 11.5 s), but the two stimuli were explicitly unpaired (referred to as unpaired training group).</p>
<p>To evaluate the involvement of hippocampal PV-IN activity in the delay paradigm of eyeblink conditioning (dEBC), 14 mice received CS-US paired presentation training trials in which a 500 ms CS preceded and coterminated with a 100 ms US. Daily dEBC training consisted of 100 CS-US paired presentation trials with an average intertrial interval of 23 s.</p></section><section id="sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Optogenetic manipulation</h3>
<p>To optogenetically identify hippocampal PV-INs <em>in vivo</em>, a green laser diode (catalog #PL520B, Osram) was activated by a pulse current (120–150 mA) from a laser diode controller (catalog #LDC-205C, Thorlabs). Optogenetic stimulation was applied through the diode-tetrode assembly implanted in PV-Cre mice. One hundred pulses of green light flashes (520 n<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> in wavelength, 300 ms in duration, 10–15 mW/mm<sup>2</sup> measured at fiber tip) were delivered when the mice were undergoing pretraining sleep. The interstimulation interval varied from 10 to 20 s with a mean value of 15 s.</p>
<p>To optogenetically inhibit hippocampal PV-IN activity during the CS-US interval period, green laser flashes (520 n<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> in wavelength, 400 ms in duration, 15 mW/mm<sup>2</sup>) were delivered at CS onset in each conditioning training trial. To inhibit hippocampal PV-IN activity during the CS presentation period, green laser flashes (520 n<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> in wavelength, 150 ms in duration, 10 mW/mm<sup>2</sup>) were delivered at CS onset in each training trial. To inhibit hippocampal PV-IN activity during the trace interval period, green laser flashes (520 n<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> in wavelength, 250 ms in duration, 15 mW/mm<sup>2</sup>) were delivered at CS offset in each training trial.</p>
<p>To optogenetically activate hippocampal PV-INs with 40 Hz frequency during the CS-US interval period in PV<sup>ChR2</sup> mice, blue laser flashes (450 n<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> in wavelength, 10 ms laser on and 15 ms laser off, 400 ms in duration, 8–10 mW/mm<sup>2</sup> duration, emitted by blue laser diodes (catalog #PL450B, Osram) were delivered at CS onset in each conditioning training trial. PV<sup>mCherry</sup> control mice also received 40 Hz blue laser stimulation at CS onset in each conditioning training trial.</p>
<section id="sec8"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">In vivo multichannel recording</h4>
<p>As we reported previously (<a href="#B44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B44">Qin et al., 2018</a>; <a href="#B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7">Chen et al., 2021</a>), the multiple unit signals were amplified, digitized at 2, 000 Hz using a multiplexed preamplifier (catalog #C3335, Intan Technologies) connected to an electrophysiological signal acquisition board (catalog #RHD2000, Intan Technologies) and stored for off-line analysis. The wideband signal was resampled to 1250 Hz and used as the local field potential (LFP). The differentiated EMG signals were amplified 1000 times and 150–1000 Hz bandpass filtered. Both the CS and the EMG signals were fed into the digital input ports of an acquisition board (catalog #RHD2000, Intan Technologies). Data were visualized by NeuroScope software (<a href="http://neurosuite.sourceforge.net" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://neurosuite.sourceforge.net</a>; <a href="#B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21">Hazan et al., 2006</a>).</p></section></section><section id="sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data analysis</h3>
<section id="sec10"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Eyeblinks</h4>
<p>The EMG activity of the orbicularis oculi muscle was rectified and integrated with a 1 ms time constant. For each trial, EMG activity was quantified for the baseline period (1–350 ms before CS onset) across 100 daily trials. The baseline EMG activities were averaged, and the SD was calculated. The average value plus four times the SD was defined as the threshold. Each trial with the maximum baseline EMG activity exceeding the threshold was defined as an invalid trial. The eyeblink conditioned response (CR; 51–400 ms after CS onset) and eyeblink unconditioned response (UR; 1–350 ms after US onset) were exclusively measured in the valid trials. A significant eyeblink response was defined as exceeding the average baseline by 4 SDs of the baseline activity for a minimum of 25 ms duration. Any significant eyeblink response during the periods mentioned above was counted as a CR or a UR, respectively (<a href="#B68" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B68">Zhang et al., 2022</a>).</p></section><section id="sec11"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Spike detection and sorting</h4>
<p>The spikes were extracted from bandpass-filtered (600–6000 Hz) signals, and their waveforms were projected onto a common basis obtained by principal component analysis of the filtered data (<a href="#B69" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B69">Zhang et al., 2020</a>). Single-unit spikes were isolated off-line using both semiautomatic clustering with KlustaKwik (<a href="http://klustakwik.sourceforge.net/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://klustakwik.sourceforge.net/</a>, <a href="#B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19">Harris et al., 2000</a>) and manual clustering with Klusters (<a href="http://neurosuite.sourceforge.net/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://neurosuite.sourceforge.net/</a>, <a href="#B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21">Hazan et al., 2006</a>). A cluster was considered a putative single unit if it was well isolated (confirmed by the existence of a 2 ms refractory period devoid of spikes in the autocorrelogram), had a signal-to-noise ratio, and its spike waveforms remained stable throughout the course of the recording (<a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">Hattori et al., 2015</a>).</p></section><section id="sec12"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Unit classification</h4>
<p>Previous studies have demonstrated that the hippocampal PYRs can be identified versus INs based on a cutoff average firing rate, ranging from 5 to 10 Hz (<a href="#B35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B35">McEchron and Disterhoft, 1997; McEchron et al., 2001</a>; <a href="#B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9">Csicsvari et al., 1999</a>; <a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">Hattori et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#B65" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B65">Witton et al., 2016</a>). In this study, units with a mean overall firing rate &lt;6 Hz and a valley-to-peak spike width &gt;0.35 ms were classified as putative hippocampal PYRs, whereas units with an overall firing rate ≥6 Hz and a valley-to-peak spike width ≤0.35 ms were classified as putative hippocampal INs (<a href="#B33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B33">Li et al., 2022</a>). Using these criteria, we sorted 1284 putative PYRs and 198 putative INs in the dorsal hippocampus from 16 mice. The proportions of putative PYRs and INs are consistent with previous reports about the cellular composition in the hippocampus (<a href="#B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42">Pelkey et al., 2017</a>).</p></section><section id="sec13"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">In vivo PV-IN identification</h4>
<p>For each unit, the spikes observed during each laser stimulation trial were assigned to a series of 50 ms time bins 1 s before (baseline period) and 1 s after the green laser onset. To identify the PV-INs, the firing rates of all putative INs were normalized according to their baseline firing rates as follows:

<p>In this study we focused our analysis on the units recording from the same tetrode. The local fast excitatory drive from PYRs onto PV-INs was identified by a short-latency peak in a cross-correlogram (CCG) between the PYR and the PV-IN spiking responses (<a href="#B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4">Barthó et al., 2004</a>). A peak in a CCG was defined as significant when at least one of the bins exceeded the 99th percentile of the mean. The mean (control number of spikes) was calculated between –50 and –10 ms to control for a potential low-frequency fluctuation of firing rate. Normalized CCG was obtained by dividing the observed CCG by the expected number of spikes per bin. The expected number of spikes per bin could be estimated by the PV-IN average firing rate. The CCG data were averaged across all PYR/PV-IN pairs recorded in the same tetrodes. Significant peaks (1 ms bin width) within 3 ms of the center bin were used to assess the fast PYR-to-PVIN drive strength (<a href="#B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12">English et al., 2017</a>).</p></section><section id="sec16"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Quantification of CS-evoked changes in oscillatory amplitude</h4>
<p>To examine CS-evoked changes in oscillatory amplitude, the Hilbert transform was used to calculate the instantaneous amplitude of specific frequency bands. Theta (5–12 Hz) and gamma (35–85 Hz) bands were examined. The quantification procedures have been introduced by <a href="#B57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B57">Tanninen et al. (2017)</a>, First, in each trial, raw LFP signals during a 2 s window around CS onset were filtered with a bandpass filter (third-order Chebyshev type 1, forward and backward for zero-phase distortion). Second, the instantaneous amplitude was calculated as the absolute value of the Hilbert transform of the filtered signal. Third, the amplitude was divided by the averaged amplitude during a 400 ms window before the CS onset. Fourth, the normalized amplitude was averaged during the time 1–400 ms from CS onset. Similar procedures were used to quantify the oscillatory amplitude in either baseline or US periods. The time window for baseline period analysis was 1–400 ms before the CS onset, whereas the time window for US period analysis was 1–400 ms after the US onset.</p></section></section><section id="sec17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Histologic verification</h3>
<p>To visualize the recording sites in PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice (<em>n</em> = 16), electrolytic lesions (30 µA for 10 s, DC current) were made at the end of all behavioral and recording experiments. Two days later, the mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, i.p.) and perfused with ice-cold saline and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; prepared in 0.1 <span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> phosphate buffer, pH 7.4). The brain was removed and postfixed in 4% PFA for 24 h. Thereafter, the brain was dehydrated in a 30% sucrose solution for 48 h. Coronal sections (40 µm thick) were cut on a freezing microtome (model CM1900, Leica) and collected in PBS (0.01 <span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span>, pH 7.4) for later staining. After three washes (5 min each), the sections containing the dorsal hippocampus were mounted in Fluoromount medium with DAPI fluorescence (catalog #F6057, Sigma-Aldrich). The tip placements of tetrodes were checked by a fluorescence microscope (model BX53, Olympus).</p>
<p>Ninety-two virus-injected PV-Cre mice were transcardially perfused immediately following optogenetic experiments to confirm viral expression and optical fiber implantation position. Coronal slices (40 µm in thickness) were obtained using the same procedure mentioned above. The slices of PV-Cre mice were prepared for 1 h of permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS followed by 1 h of block with 5% BSA. Slices were incubated with primary antibody (parvalbumin, 1:400; catalog # 11427, Abam) overnight at 4°C and secondary antibody (AlexaFluor 568, 1:800; catalog #A10042, Invitrogen) were incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were taken by a confocal laser-scanning microscope (IXplore SpinSR, Olympus).</p></section><section id="sec18"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistics</h3>
<p>Data are expressed as the mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted. The statistical significance for behavioral analysis was determined by one-way or two-way ANOVAs with repeated measures as needed. Difference in firing activity between two groups (paired vs unpaired) was determined by independent <em>t</em> test (two tailed). Paired <em>t</em> tests were used to determine the difference in firing activity between two states (CR vs no CR) and the effect of optogenetic manipulation on the CR performance. A value of <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05 was significant for all tests. Significance levels of data were denoted as *<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05, **<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01 and ***<em>p</em> &lt; 0.001; <em>p</em> &gt; 0.05 was insignificant and was denoted as n.s.</p></section></section><section id="sec19"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="sec20"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Optogenetic identification of hippocampal PV-INs <em>in vivo</em>
</h3>
<p>To achieve specific identification and manipulation of PV-INs <em>in vivo</em>, we selectively expressed the light-activated inhibitory proton pump (ArchT) in the PV-INs by crossing <em>PV-</em>Cre mice with mice expressing cre-dependent ArchT (Ai40D). We first performed immunohistological experiments to assess the efficiency and specificity of ArchT expression in the hippocampus of heterozygote PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><em>A</em>). The results showed that 82.4 ± 2.3% of PV-INs expressed ArchT in the dorsal hippocampus of PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice, along with high-level expression specificity (90.7 ± 1.6%, averaged from 8 PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice). As shown by the example recording traces from a PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mouse in <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a><em>B</em>, the delivery of green laser pulses (10–15 mW/mm<sup>2</sup> light intensity, 300 ms duration, 10–30 s intervals) produced significant suppression in firing activity of a hippocampal PV-IN. We referred to those units showing significantly suppressive responses (&gt;50% in firing suppression) that were time locked to green laser stimulation as the optogenetically tagged PV-INs (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.01, permutation test, laser on vs laser off baseline). With this opto-tagging method, we identified 106 dorsal hippocampal PV-INs (72.7 ± 1.5% in firing suppression averaged from 106 units in 16 PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice; <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><em>C</em>) which exhibited high firing rate (13.6 ± 0.8 Hz) and narrow valley-to-peak spike width (0.27 ± 0.01 ms averaged from 106 units; <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><em>D</em>). The optogenetically identified PV-INs increased their firing during the hippocampal SWRs (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><em>E</em>,<em>F</em>) and preferred to emit their spikes at the ascending phase of SWRs (205.0 ± 9.9° with 0° at the peak, <em>n</em> = 106; <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><em>G</em>). In addition, the optogenetically identified PV-INs emitted more spikes at the descending phase of theta oscillations (166.3 ± 16.5° with 0° at the peak, <em>n</em> = 106; <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><em>H</em>,<em>J</em>). The firing characteristics of optogenetically identified PV-INs are consistent with the previous findings reported for hippocampal PV-INs (<a href="#B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28">Klausberger et al., 2003</a>; <a href="#B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32">Lapray et al., 2012</a>). We thus established a reliable approach to identify hippocampal PV-INs and manipulate their firing activity <em>in vivo</em>.</p>

<p>Having optogenetically identified the hippocampal PV-INs, we next sought to investigate whether and how these interneurons change their firing activities during learning an associative sensorimotor behavior. For this purpose, we trained the freely moving mice to learn a tEBC task in which a light CS was paired with an air puff US (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a><em>A</em>). To avoid the aversive USs, the mice responded to the light CSs and lowered their upper eyelids just before the presentation of the air puff USs (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a><em>B</em>,<em>C</em>). We found that the mice receiving CS-US paired presentations showed fewer CRs on days 1–2 (CR incidence, 39.7 ± 7.0%) and more CRs on days 4–5 (CR incidence, 61.5 ± 9.3%; within-group effect, <em>F</em><sub>(4,28)</sub> = 3.327, <em>p</em> = 0.024, <em>n</em> = 8, one-way ANOVA with repeated measures; <a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a><em>B</em>). Across five training days, the paired training mice exhibited a significantly higher CR level than the unpaired training mice (CR incidence, between-group effect, <em>F</em><sub>(1,14)</sub> = 34.064, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.001; training days times group interaction, <em>F</em><sub>(4,56)</sub> = 1.920, <em>p</em> = 0.120; CR peak amplitude, between-group effect, <em>F</em><sub>(1,14)</sub> = 5.512, <em>p</em> = 0.034; training days times group interaction, <em>F</em><sub>(4,56)</sub> = 1.230, <em>p</em> = 0.309; <em>n</em> = 8 for both paired and unpaired training mice, two-way ANOVAs with repeated measures; <a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a><em>B–D</em>). These results suggested that the paired training mice learned the CS-US association across five training days. Based on the CR level in paired training mice, we further separated behavioral training into two stages, the early learning stage (ELS; days 1–2) with relatively few CRs and the late-learning stage (LLS; days 4–5) with an asymptotic high CR level (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a><em>B</em>,<em>C</em>).</p>

<p>Considering that the hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity was associated with the CR occurrence, we hypothesized that it might contribute to the acquisition of tEBC. We tested this hypothesis by selectively suppressing the hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity during the acquisition of tEBC. For this purpose, we injected viral vectors encoding ArchT (AAV2-FLEX-ArchT-GFP, PV<sup>ArchT</sup>, <em>n</em> = 9) or an opsin negative control vector (AAV2-Ef1a-DIO-GFP, PV<sup>GFP</sup>, <em>n</em> = 7) into bilateral dorsal hippocampus of <em>PV</em>-Cre mice (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a><em>A</em>,<em>B</em>). It was showed that optogenetic suppression of hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity produced severe impairments in the CR acquisition (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a><em>C–E</em>). Two-way mixed ANOVA (suppression times training day) across training days revealed no significant effects in the interaction between suppression and training days (CR incidence, <em>F</em><sub>(4,56)</sub> = 0.282, <em>p</em> = 0.888; CR peak amplitude, <em>F</em><sub>(4,56)</sub> = 0.020, <em>p</em> = 0.999; <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a><em>D</em>,<em>E</em>), but significant suppression effects (CR incidence, <em>F</em><sub>(1,14)</sub> = 12.493, <em>p</em> = 0.004; CR peak amplitude, <em>F</em><sub>(1,14)</sub> = 4.579, <em>p</em> = 0.049; <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a><em>D</em>,<em>E</em>). The behavioral results could not be explained by the disability of PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice because they could acquire the CRs across 5 additional days (days 6–10) when there were no green laser stimulations. As illustrated in <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a><em>C</em>, a representative PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mouse exhibited obvious CR improvement across training days 6–10. Statistical analysis revealed that the PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice emitted comparable CRs on days 6–10 to those of PV<sup>GFP</sup> mice on days 1–5 (CR incidence, <em>F</em><sub>(1,14)</sub> = 0.088, <em>p</em> = 0.771; CR peak amplitude, <em>F</em><sub>(1,14)</sub> = 0.061, <em>p</em> = 0.999, two-way ANOVA with repeated measures). These results indicate a requirement of hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity for the acquisition of tEBC.</p>

However, the specific role of hippocampal PV-IN activity during either the CS presentation or the trace interval period remains unclear. Notably, a 150 ms suppression of PV-IN activity during the CS presentation period caused a significant impairment in the CR acquisition (suppression effects, CR incidence, <em>F</em><sub>(1,10)</sub> = 8.670, <em>p</em> = 0.015; CR peak amplitude, <em>F</em><sub>(1,10)</sub> = 4.433, <em>p</em> = 0.062; suppression times training day interaction, CR incidence, <em>F</em><sub>(1,10)</sub> = 0.848, <em>p</em> = 0.379; CR peak amplitude, <em>F</em><sub>(1,10)</sub> = 0.418, <em>p</em> = 0.532; <em>n</em> = 6 for both PV<sup>ArchT</sup> and PV<sup>GFP</sup> mice, two-way ANOVA with repeated measures; <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a><em>F</em>). These results suggest that the hippocampal PV-IN activity during either the CS presentation or the trace interval period are both involved in the CR acquisition.</p>
<p>The PV-IN sustained activity diminished at the LLS, raising a possibility that the diminished PV-IN sustained activity may be an important mechanism that contributes to accelerate learning at this stage. Alternatively, the role of PV-IN sustained activity in learning becomes minimal at the LLS. To test these two possibilities, we suppressed the PV-IN sustained activity when the CRs were well learned at the LLS (i.e., on days 5 and 6; <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a><em>H</em>). It was shown that optogenetic suppression of PV-IN sustained activity produced no significant effects on the CR performance in PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice (<em>n</em> = 5, CR incidence, <em>t</em><sub>(4)</sub> = −1.826, <em>p</em> = 0.142; CR peak amplitude, <em>t</em><sub>(4)</sub> = −0.698, <em>p</em> = 0.523, paired <em>t</em> tests; <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a><em>H</em>). In addition, there were no significant differences in either the CR incidence or the CR magnitude between the PV<sup>ArchT</sup> and PV<sup>GFP</sup> mice at the LLS (CR incidence, <em>t</em><sub>(9)</sub> = −1.118, <em>p</em> = 0.265; CR peak amplitude, <em>t</em><sub>(9)</sub> = −0.231, <em>p</em> = 0.823, <em>n</em> = 5 for PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice and <em>n</em> = 6 for PV<sup>GFP</sup> mice, independent <em>t</em> tests; <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a><em>H</em>). These results indicate that the requirement of hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity for tEBC is minimal at the LLS.</p>
<p>To further confirm the specific requirement of PV-IN sustained activity in tEBC acquisition, we also evaluated the role of PV-IN activity in the acquisition of delay eyeblink conditioning (dEBC), a forebrain-independent task in rodents (<a href="#B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31">Kotani et al., 2002</a>). To address this issue, we trained the mice to acquire dEBC in which a 500 ms CS preceded and coterminated with a 100 ms US and optogenetically suppressed the hippocampal PV-IN activity with 400 ms green laser stimulation (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a><em>A</em>). We found that both the PV<sup>ArchT</sup> and the PV<sup>GFP</sup> mice exhibited significant improvement in CR level across five training days (61.7 ± 8.7%, <em>n</em> = 7 for PV<sup>ArchT</sup> and 57.5 ± 6.4%, <em>n</em> = 7 for PV<sup>GFP</sup> mice; <a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a><em>B</em>). A two-way mixed ANOVA revealed no significant suppression effect (CR incidence, <em>F</em><sub>(1,12)</sub> = 0.944, <em>p</em> = 0. 350; CR peak amplitude, <em>F</em><sub>(1,12)</sub> = 0.183, <em>p</em> = 0.677; <a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a><em>B</em>), indicating that the hippocampal PV-IN activity is not required for the acquisition of dEBC.</p>

<p>After establishing the causal relationship between the hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity and the tEBC acquisition, we next explored how the PV-IN sustained activity was formed at the ELS. Considering the sustained PYR firing activity (<a href="#B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34">McEchron and Disterhoft, 1997</a>; <a href="#B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B37">Modi et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">Hattori et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29">Klee et al., 2021</a>; <a href="#B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38">Mount et al., 2021</a>) and strong excitatory-to-inhibitory synapses in the dorsal hippocampus (<a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">Gulyás et al., 1993</a>; <a href="#B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12">English et al., 2017</a>), we asked whether the PV-IN sustained activity was, at least in part, because of the excitatory drive from hippocampal PYRs.</p>
<p>To test this, we first analyzed the firing pattern of hippocampal PYRs during the acquisition of tEBC. Based on their firing characteristics, we sorted 738 putative hippocampal PYRs (average firing rate, 1.65 ± 0.05 Hz; valley-to-peak spike width, 0.725 ± 0.004 ms; <a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a><em>A</em>,<em>B</em>) from eight paired training mice and 546 putative hippocampal PYRs (average firing rate, 1.55 ± 0.05 Hz; valley-to-peak spike width, 0.736 ± 0.003 ms; <a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a><em>A</em>,<em>B</em>) from eight unpaired training mice. At the ELS (days 1–2), it was found that 40% (102/255) of putative hippocampal PYRs showed firing responses during the CS-US interval period in paired training mice, whereas 37.3% (82/220) of putative hippocampal PYRs showed the CS-evoked firing in unpaired training mice (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a><em>C</em>). In particular, the CS-evoked firing activity of hippocampal PYRs in paired training mice was greater than that of the unpaired training mice (<em>Z</em>-scored firing rate during CS-US interval period, 4.35 ± 0.26, <em>n</em> = 102 units for paired vs 3.23 ± 0.11, <em>n</em> = 82 units for unpaired training mice, <em>t</em><sub>(181)</sub> = 3.692, <em>p</em> = 0.0003, independent <em>t</em> test; <a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a><em>D</em>). At the LLS (days 4–5), however, the CS-evoked firing was comparable between the paired and unpaired training mice (<em>Z</em>-scored firing rate during CS-US interval period, 4.28 ± 0.58, <em>n</em> = 114 units for paired vs 3.53 ± 0.18, <em>n</em> = 68 units for unpaired training mice, <em>t</em><sub>(180)</sub> = 1.168, <em>p</em> = 0.2245, independent <em>t</em> test; <a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a><em>E</em>,<em>F</em>). These results suggest that the hippocampal PYRs also exhibit learning-associated firing activities in the CS-US interval at the ELS.</p>

As illustrated in <a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Figure 8</a>, <em>A</em> and <em>B</em>, the hippocampal PV-INs in paired training mice tended to have greater reliability to emit spikes immediately after the PYR discharge in the CS-US interval period. Consistent with this, we observed the presence of a sharp peak at short latency (&lt;3 ms after the discharge of the reference PYR) in the PYR-to-PVIN cross-correlation histogram (<a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a><em>C</em>), indicating a fast excitatory input from the PYRs onto PV-INs during tEBC training. We then quantified the strength of fast excitatory PYR-to-PVIN drive by measuring the spike transmission probability at the putative monosynaptic peak bins (i.e., ≤3 ms) at the ELS and the LLS, respectively. At the ELS (days 1–2), it was revealed that the paired training mice exhibited greater PYR-to-PVIN drive strength than the unpaired training mice (0.074 ± 0.005, 65 PYR-to-PVIN pairs from eight paired training mice vs 0.058 ± 0.004, 44 PYR-to-PVIN pairs from eight unpaired training mice, <em>t</em><sub>(14)</sub> = 2.634, <em>p</em> = 0.020, independent <em>t</em> test; <a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a><em>C</em>,<em>D</em>). Moreover, the strength of fast excitatory PYR-to-PVIN drive at the ELS was positively correlated with the CR incidence in the paired training mice on day 5 (<em>r</em><sup>2</sup> = 0.630, <em>p</em> = 0.032, <em>n</em> = 8, Pearson's correlation test; <a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a><em>E</em>). In contrast, at the LLS (days 4–5), the paired training mice showed PYR-to-PVIN drive strength comparable to that of the unpaired training mice (0.066 ± 0.005, 51 PYR-to-PVIN pairs from eight paired training mice vs 0.064 ± 0.007, 53 PYR-to-PVIN pairs from eight unpaired training mice, <em>t</em><sub>(14)</sub> = 0.190, <em>p</em> = 0.852, independent <em>t</em> test; <a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a><em>F</em>,<em>G</em>). In addition, the strength of the fast excitatory PYR-to-PVIN drive seemed to not correlate with CR incidence in the paired training mice on day 5 (<em>r</em><sup>2</sup> = 0.078, <em>p</em> = 0.503, <em>n</em> = 8, Pearson's correlation test; <a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a><em>H</em>). These results suggest that there is learning-associated augmentation in the strength of fast excitatory PYR-to-PVIN drive at the ELS. As both the PYRs and the PV-INs elevated their firing activities during the CS-US interval period, the learning-associated augmentation in fast excitatory PYR-to-PVIN drive effect may, at least in part, contribute to the formation of hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity.</p>

<p>Hippocampal oscillatory activities have been suggested to be strongly influenced by the interaction between the PYRs and PV-INs (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">Antonoudiou et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">He et al., 2021</a>). In particular, several types of hippocampal oscillatory activities (e.g., 3–8 Hz theta band and 30–100 Hz gamma band) have been demonstrated to subserve the acquisition of tEBC (<a href="#B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10">Darling et al., 2011</a>; <a href="#B57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B57">Tanninen et al., 2017</a>). Therefore, we next sought to explore whether and how the PV-IN sustained activity is involved in hippocampal oscillatory activities to support the acquisition of tEBC.</p>
<p>To address this issue, we first tested the effect of suppressing PV-IN sustained activity on the interaction between the hippocampal PYRs and PV-INs (<a href="#F9" class="usa-link">Fig. 9</a><em>A</em>,<em>B</em>). Considering that both PV-IN sustained activity and PYR-PVIN interaction are greater at the ELS, we focused our analysis and manipulation on training days 1–2. Optogenetic suppression of the PV-IN sustained activity only occupied ∼1.67% (100 × 0.4 s/2400 s) of training time on days 1–2 and produced no significant changes in average firing rates of either the PV-INs (14.175 ± 3.506 Hz averaged from five PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice vs 14.814 ± 2.881 Hz averaged from five PV<sup>GFP</sup> mice, <em>t</em><sub>(8)</sub> = −0. 157, <em>p</em> = 0.879, independent <em>t</em> test; <a href="#F9" class="usa-link">Fig. 9</a><em>C</em>) or the PYRs (2.988 ± 0.604 Hz averaged from five PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice vs 2.592 ± 0.138 Hz averaged from five PV<sup>GFP</sup> mice, <em>t</em><sub>(8)</sub> = 0.715, <em>p</em> = 0.405; <a href="#F9" class="usa-link">Fig. 9</a><em>D</em>). Nevertheless, selective suppression of the PV-IN sustained activity reduced the possibility that the PV-INs emitted spikes immediately (≤3 ms latency) after the PYR discharge during tEBC training (0.0581 ± 0. 0034, <em>n</em> = 5 for PV<sup>ArchT</sup> mice vs 0. 0773 ± 0. 0031, <em>n</em> = 5 for PV<sup>GFP</sup> mice, <em>t</em><sub>(8)</sub> = −4.649, <em>p</em> = 0.002, independent <em>t</em> test; <a href="#F9" class="usa-link">Fig. 9</a><em>A</em>,<em>B</em>,<em>E</em>).</p>

<p>Suppression of the PV-IN sustained activity impaired both the augmentation of gamma band oscillation in CS-US interval and the acquisition of tEBC. These findings raise a possibility that the augmentation of gamma band oscillation in CS-US interval may be beneficial for the acquisition of tEBC. Therefore, we optogenetically activated the hippocampal PV-INs during the CS-US interval period at gamma frequency (40 Hz) early in learning to see whether the mice could show an increased learning rate compared with controls. To do this, we selectively expressed excitatory opsin ChR2 in bilateral dorsal hippocampus of PV-Cre mice (<a href="#F11" class="usa-link">Fig. 11</a><em>A</em>,<em>B</em>). As illustrated in <a href="#F11" class="usa-link">Figure 11</a><em>C</em>, 40 Hz (triggered by the CS onset, 10 ms laser on and 15 ms laser off, 400 ms in duration) blue laser stimulation of the hippocampal PV-INs evoked augmented gamma frequency oscillation during the CS-US interval period in a PV<sup>ChR2</sup> mouse instead of in a PV<sup>mCherry</sup> mouse. Intriguingly, augmented gamma frequency (40 Hz) oscillation during the CS-US interval period at the ELS (days 1–2) was predictive of an increased learning rate on subsequent training days (days 3–5, training days effect, CR incidence, <em>F</em><sub>(1,10)</sub> = 39.003, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.001; CR peak amplitude, <em>F</em><sub>(1,10)</sub> = 0.420, <em>p</em> = 0.532; training days times groups interaction, CR incidence, <em>F</em><sub>(2,20)</sub> = 0.064, <em>p</em> = 0. 938; CR peak amplitude, <em>F</em><sub>(2,20)</sub> = 1.164, <em>p</em> = 0.332; two-way ANOVA with repeated measures; <a href="#F11" class="usa-link">Fig. 11</a><em>D</em>,<em>E</em>). Together, our results suggest that the PV-IN sustained activity is involved in the augmentation of gamma oscillation during the CS-US interval period, which is beneficial for the acquisition of tEBC.</p>

<p>Using a combination of multichannel recording and optogenetics, we revealed that the hippocampal PV-INs showed CS-evoked firing activity during tEBC acquisition. In particular, the PV-IN firing activity was sustained from the CS presentation to the trace interval and was correlated with the CR occurrence. Selective suppression of the PV-IN sustained activity disrupted the PYR-PVIN interaction and attenuated gamma band oscillation during tEBC acquisition. Selective suppression of the PV-IN sustained activity caused impairment in tEBC acquisition. These results indicate that the hippocampal interneuron is a crucial cellular component of the associative learning network, and sustained activity of its PV-IN subtype plays a key role in associative learning.</p>
<p>There is growing evidence indicating an involvement of hippocampal interneurons in associative learning (<a href="#B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15">Fuchs et al., 2007</a>; <a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">He et al., 2021</a>; <a href="#B70" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B70">Zhang et al., 2021</a>). However, the mechanisms by which the hippocampal interneurons participate in associative learning remain not fully understood. In particular, considering the diversity of hippocampal interneurons (<a href="#B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14">Freund and Buzsáki, 1996</a>; <a href="#B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B41">Parra et al., 1998</a>; <a href="#B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42">Pelkey et al., 2017</a>), a question remains about the contribution of a specific subtype of hippocampal interneurons to associative learning. We herein observed a learning-associated sustained activity in the hippocampal PV-INs during tEBC acquisition (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>). Importantly, the PV-IN sustained activity was biased between the CR and no-CR trials with greater firing responses in CR state (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>). We thus expected that the PV-IN sustained activity might contribute to the acquisition of tEBC. In support of this expectation, we revealed that selective suppression of the PV-IN sustained activity impaired the tEBC acquisition (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>). Our findings not only add new data that the hippocampal PV-INs are critically involved in tEBC acquisition but also suggest that the sustained activity of hippocampal PV-INs is a candidate cellular mechanism supporting associative learning.</p>
<p>Accumulating evidence suggests that the acquisition of tEBC relies on a distributed forebrain network (<a href="#B66" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B66">Woodruff-Pak and Disterhoft, 2008</a>; <a href="#B62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B62">Weiss and Disterhoft, 2011</a>) and that areas in this forebrain network communicate with each other (<a href="#B55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B55">Takehara-Nishiuchi et al., 2012</a>; <a href="#B48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B48">Shearkhani and Takehara-Nishiuchi, 2013</a>). Among the forebrain areas, the hippocampus is considered a hub because lesion or inactivation of the dorsal hippocampus results in severe impairment in the acquisition of tEBC (<a href="#B50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B50">Solomon et al., 1986</a>; <a href="#B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B39">Moyer et al., 1990</a>; <a href="#B63" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B63">Weiss et al., 1999</a>; <a href="#B58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B58">Tseng et al., 2004</a>; <a href="#B47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B47">Sakamoto et al., 2005</a>). Intriguingly, the hippocampus connects with several forebrain areas such as the prefrontal cortex (<a href="#B25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B25">Jay and Witter, 1991</a>; <a href="#B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5">Cenquizca and Swanson, 2007</a>) and the entorhinal cortex (<a href="#B1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1">Amaral and Witter, 1989</a>; <a href="#B45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B45">Rozov et al., 2020</a>), which have been demonstrated to be critically involved in tEBC (<a href="#B61" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B61">Weible et al., 2000</a>; <a href="#B46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B46">Ryou et al., 2001</a>; <a href="#B49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B49">Siegel et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#B56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B56">Tanninen et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6">Chen et al., 2016</a>). Electrophysiological studies have documented that a number of hippocampal PYRs exhibited sustained excitatory activity in the CS-US interval during tEBC, whereas a larger number of hippocampal PYRs showed sustained inhibitory activity (<a href="#B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34">McEchron and Disterhoft, 1997</a>; <a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">Hattori et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#B70" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B70">Zhang et al., 2021</a>; <a href="#B33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B33">Li et al., 2022</a>). Likewise, both sparse excitatory and strong inhibitory activities were observed in the hippocampal PYRs during appetitive auditory trace conditioning (<a href="#B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29">Klee et al., 2021</a>). The excitation mixed with stronger inhibition has been hypothesized to increase the signal-to-noise ratio of hippocampal PYR encoding for the CS-US association (<a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">Hattori et al., 2015</a>). Here, we showed evidence that the PV-INs received augmented inputs from the excitatory PYRs during tEBC acquisition (<a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a>). Anatomically, the hippocampal PV-INs in turn innervate the soma and proximal dendrites of local PYRs (<a href="#B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23">Hu et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2">Amilhon et al., 2015</a>). Consequently, it is reasonable to propose that the PV-INs may efficiently cooperate with the PYRs to augment the signal-to-noise ratio of hippocampal encoding and thereafter contribute to inter-brain-area communication.</p>
<p>Our results revealed that the requirement of hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity for tEBC underwent dynamic change across associative learning. This view was supported by several facets of evidence in this study. First, the PV-IN sustained activity diminished at the LLS (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>). Second, there was no significant difference in the PV-IN sustained activity between the CR and no-CR states at the LLS (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>). Third, suppression of the PV-IN sustained activity had no obvious effect on the performance of well learned CRs at the LLS (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>). Indeed, a number of studies have demonstrated that the hippocampus plays a time-limited role in the acquisition of tEBC, with greater contribution at the initial learning stage (<a href="#B53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B53">Takehara et al., 2002</a>, <a href="#B54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B54">2003</a>). Therefore, our current findings indicate that the dynamic change of hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity may be a candidate cellular process reflecting the distinct involvement of hippocampus in tEBC acquisition at various learning stages.</p>
<p>Although the role of hippocampal PV-IN sustained activity has been revealed in the acquisition of tEBC, how it forms is still a mystery. Previous studies have suggested that the entorhinal cortex can support the development of hippocampal neuronal activity that bridges time-separated stimuli (<a href="#B52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B52">Suh et al., 2011</a>; <a href="#B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27">Kitamura et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#B44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B44">Qin et al., 2018</a>). Consequently, the entorhinal cortical input to the hippocampus might be one of the sources contributing to the formation of PV-IN sustained activity. However, it should be noted that the PV-INs also receive excitatory inputs from neighboring PYRs within the hippocampus (<a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">Gulyás et al., 1993</a>; <a href="#B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12">English et al., 2017</a>). Here, we found a learning-associated augmentation in the excitatory PYR-to-PVIN drive, which was positively correlated with the CR occurrence (<a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a>). Because the hippocampal PYRs elevated their firing during the CS-US interval period, it is reasonable for us to propose that the augmented excitatory PYR-to-PVIN drive can contribute to the PV-IN sustained activity either. Indeed, modified hippocampal excitatory PYR-to-interneuron drive strength has been observed after spatial learning (<a href="#B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11">Dupret et al., 2013</a>; <a href="#B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17">Gridchyn et al., 2020</a>). Given this evidence, it seems possible that learning-associated modification of the hippocampal pyramidal–interneuron connection is the other mechanism to shape the PV-IN firing kinetics, which remodels the hippocampal network (<a href="#B59" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B59">Udakis et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">He et al., 2021</a>).</p>
<p>Our results revealed that selective suppression of the PV-IN sustained activity reduced learning-associated augmentation of gamma band (35–85 Hz) oscillation during the CS-US interval period (<a href="#F10" class="usa-link">Fig. 10</a>), indicating a critical involvement of PV-IN sustained activity in influencing hippocampal oscillatory state during tEBC acquisition. Previous studies have demonstrated that conditional ablation of the GluR-A subunit or γCamKII in PV-INs could disrupt the PYR-to-PV-IN interaction and reduce the power of hippocampal gamma oscillation (<a href="#B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15">Fuchs et al., 2007</a>; <a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">He et al., 2021</a>). Therefore, our observation of reduced gamma oscillation during the CS-US interval period was most likely because of the disrupted PYR-to-PV-IN interaction after suppression of the PV-IN sustained activity. Nevertheless, we did not observe significant changes in theta band (5–12 Hz) oscillation after the PV-IN suppression. This result was unexpected because theta band oscillation has been suggested to be highly correlated with tEBC (<a href="#B64" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B64">Wikgren et al., 2010</a>; <a href="#B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10">Darling et al., 2011</a>). One explanation for this result is that other subtypes of hippocampal interneurons, in addition to the PV-INs, are also involved in theta band oscillation during tEBC. This possibility is raised as tEBC training induces an increase in intrinsic excitability of somatostatin-positive hippocampal interneurons (<a href="#B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B36">McKay et al., 2013</a>). Consequently, intact theta band oscillation might result from our selective interrogation on the PV-INs. At the circuit level, theta–gamma coupling has been suggested to be an important mechanism subserving the communication among distributed brain areas during associative learning (<a href="#B24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B24">Igarashi et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#B57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B57">Tanninen et al., 2017</a>). Considering the evidence, we speculated that the diminished gamma but intact theta band oscillation might reduce theta–gamma coupling strength, and thus impaired the communication between the hippocampus and extrahippocampal structures during tEBC.</p>
<p>It should be noted that inhibition of PV-INs might disinhibit local PYRs (<a href="#F9" class="usa-link">Fig. 9</a><em>A</em>, middle), which in turn caused an increased drive from the PYRs to PV-INs. Nevertheless, the PV-INs did not respond to the increased drive because they were inhibited by opsin ArchT (<a href="#F9" class="usa-link">Fig. 9</a><em>A</em>, left). Our method did not aim to reduce excitatory drive from the PYRs to PV-INs. Instead, it aimed to disrupt the interaction between the PYRs and PV-INs during tEBC acquisition. Therefore, future experiments should be guaranteed to specifically interrogate excitatory drive from the hippocampal PYRs to PV-INs during tEBC.</p>
<p>Similar to the findings reported by <a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">Hattori et al. (2015)</a>, we observed that the hippocampal PYRs continued to respond to the CS at the LLS (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a>). Although strong PYR inputs existed, however, the PV-INs did not maintain the sustained activity at this stage (<a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a>). One explanation for this result is that synaptic transmission between hippocampal PYRs and PV-INs might endure plastic change across learning (<a href="#B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11">Dupret et al., 2013</a>; <a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">He et al., 2021</a>). Alternatively, the PV-INs receive inputs from both extrahippocampal neurons (i.e., entorhinal cortex) and neighboring PYRs at the ELS, whereas they only receive inputs from neighboring PYRs at the LLS (<a href="#B55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B55">Takehara-Nishiuchi et al., 2012</a>).</p>
<p>In summary, our results outline a casual role for hippocampal PV-INs in associative learning. Moreover, we provide a mechanistic understanding of the kinetics of PV-IN activity that influences hippocampal network oscillatory state to support associative learning. Together, our data suggest that the recruitment of PV-INs into heterogeneous hippocampal network oscillatory patterns subserve the key aspect of associative learning.</p></section><section id="fn-group1" class="fn-group"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Footnotes</h2>
