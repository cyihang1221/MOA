>Methods   Sixteen male water polo athletes of the Chinese national team were recruited. All athletes underwent a one-week complete break following the end of the previous competitive season to mitigate accumulated fatigue and establish a true resting metabolic baseline. Fasting venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected at 7:00 AM on two time points: the first sample (E1) was collected before commencement of the official training week, and the second sample (E2) was collected immediately after the completion of that week of training. The data were analyzed via XCMS, MetaboAnalyst 6.0, SPSS 21.0, and GraphPad Prism. Results   (1) Metabolomic analysis identified 363 metabolites in total, 33 of which were differentially expressed between pre- and post-training time points. After one week of routine training, 11 metabolites were significantly up-regulated ( p  <  0.01), and 22 were significantly down-regulated ( p <  0.01). (2) KEGG pathway analysis identified the top eight metabolic pathways, with MetPA further highlighting lysine degradation ( p <  0.01) and vitamin B6 metabolism ( p <  0.05) as key altered pathways. (3) Three metabolites were identified as potential markers associated with the training week changes in water polo athletes on the basis of significant alterations post-training. N6, N6, N6-trimethyl-L-lysine ( p <  0.01) and 2-aminoadipic acid ( p <  0.01) were significantly decreased, whereas 4-pyridoxic acid ( p <  0.01) was significantly increased. Conclusions   Non-targeted LC‒MS/MS provides a valuable tool for monitoring metabolic adaptations at the molecular level in aquatic athletes. In this exploratory study, we observed associated changes in the serum metabolome following intensive training, pointing to adjustments in amino acid and lipid metabolism. These findings offer preliminary insights for guiding fitness and performance optimization. Introduction   Metabolomics is an emerging field that focuses on the simultaneous qualitative and quantitative analysis of all low-molecular-weight metabolites in a biological system or cell at a specific physiological state and was initially proposed by Nicholson [ 1 ] in 1999 following the use of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) technology to analyse animal urine. Non-targeted metabolomics is a method that profiles diverse metabolites in biological samples without predefined targets, offering an unbiased view of the metabolic landscape. The advantage of mass spectrometry (MS) lies in its ability to detect and monitor metabolic differences, offering insights into physiological changes and processes occurring at various life stages [ 2 ]. The primary objective of metabolomics studies, therefore, is to identify differences between pathological metabolic profiles and normal physiological profiles, facilitating class prediction through metabolite biomarkers [ 3 ]. At present, it is widely applied across multiple fields, including medical diagnostics [ 4 , 5 ], pharmaceutical and drug development [ 6 , 7 ], and agriculture and food science [ 8 , 9 ].   Whole-body exercise responses have been extensively studied to understand the benefits for human health, particularly in relation to metabolism and exercise physiology [ 10 ]. Monitoring metabolite biomarkers in response to training provides insights that improve our understanding of training-related adaptations, reflect physiological changes linked to performance, and may support the development of evidence-based approaches for athletes' monitoring and nutritional programming [ 11–13 ]. In this context, Moreira et al. [ 14 ] pioneered the use of Raman spectral peaks in urine samples from physically active participants (undergoing 30 min of cycling or running) to identify exercise-associated metabolites (creatine (Cr), ketone bodies, nitrogenous compounds, and phosphate), thereby supporting the monitoring and optimisation of training performance.   Water polo is a physically demanding sport that combines swimming, handball, and volleyball. Research has identified water polo as an intermittent sport characterised by intense bursts of activity lasting less than 15 seconds, interspersed with lower-intensity intervals averaging less than 20 seconds [ 15 ], and involving a mix of metabolic energy systems, including the phosphagen system, anaerobic glycolysis, and aerobic oxidation system [ 16 ]. Significant focus has been given to the technical and strategic aspects of water polo; however, research on the physical and physiological demands, particularly the metabolic requirements that underpin elite performance during competition, remains limited.   Over the past few decades, research has relied predominantly on established physiological and biochemical markers to analyse specific metabolites, providing a broader understanding of the body’s responses to training. These markers include blood lactate for assessing energy metabolism, creatine kinase (CK) as an indicator of muscle damage, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) for evaluating protein metabolism [ 17 ]. However, these markers have limitations in their ability to capture subtle metabolic changes at the molecular level, which constrains the discovery of novel biomarkers and our understanding of complex adaptive processes.   In contrast to traditional techniques, the non-targeted, high-sensitivity advantages of LC‒MS not only enable detailed analysis of metabolites at the molecular level but also reflect the interactions among metabolic biomarkers, including metabolomic pathways and signal transduction information. For instance, Zhao et al. [ 18 ] utilised LC-MS metabolomics systematically revealed significant dynamic changes in the urinary metabolic profiles of elite female football players, confirming multidimensional interactions among energy metabolism, neurotransmitter activity, and amino acid metabolic networks. In a complementary multi-omics study of over 600 serum samples, Contrepois et al. [ 19 ] demonstrated that exercise systematically modulates pathways governing energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory repair in patients. Kozłowska et al. [ 20 ] further investigated the effects of beetroot juice supplementation in fencing athletes using urinary metabolomics and identified significant alterations in tyrosine, tryptophan, epinephrine, and norepinephrine metabolism, providing molecular-level insights relevant to training-related responses and performance adaptation. These studies affirm the cross-disciplinary value of molecular-level exercise research and underscore the power of integrated omics technologies. Research of this kind provides valuable insights for understanding training load and physiological adaptation at the metabolic level.   Accordingly, this study employed a hypothesis-free, non-targeted LC‒MS-based metabolomics approach to investigate the serum metabolic profiles of Chinese national youth male water polo athletes after competition following one week of routine training. By integrating metabolic pathway analysis, enrichment and topology assessments, and energy metabolism changes, this research aims to characterise the acute molecular and metabolic response induced by one week of routine training. 2. Materials and methods   2.1. Subjects   Sixteen male youth water polo athletes from the Chinese national team were selected as research participants (mean age: 17.6 ± 0.5 years, mean height: 185.1 ± 3.9 cm, and mean BMI: 23.9 ± 2.9). The sample size ( n  = 16) is reflective of the finite, elite population available. The initial selection pool consisted of 22 athletes from the national youth team. To ensure metabolic and physiological homogeneity within the cohort, we focused specifically on field players ( n  = 18), excluding goalkeepers. This decision is supported by established research in team sports, which demonstrates that athletes in different positions (e.g. goalkeepers vs. field players) exhibit distinct metabolic and physiological profiles due to marked differences in exercise patterns, intensity, and duration during competition and training [ 21 ]. Ultimately, 16 field players completed the full longitudinal study protocol.   According to the official athlete classification system, 15 participants were classified as national-level athletes and 1 as a first-class athlete. All participants had comparable training and competitive exposure, with similar training loads and competition intensity (mean time: 5.63 ± 0.48 years of systematic water polo training). During the study period, the athletes trained 5.68 ± 0.24 hours per day, with one rest day per week.   Blood samples from 16 athletes were successfully collected and analysed as valid data. All participants were confirmed to be in good health, with no reported metabolic diseases or significant health issues during the training camp. None of the athletes were on medication, smoked, consumed excessive alcohol, stayed up late, or used stimulants or functional beverages. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants before their participation in the study. All the participants were fully accustomed to the procedures used in this research and were told that they could withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.   To minimise experimental variability, all participants underwent the same training programme at the Shenzhen Sports School swimming pool during the trial period. To standardise dietary intake and minimise metabolic differences, all meals were uniformly prepared and provided by the water polo training centre. Training programme   The training programme is structured with a combination of land-based and water-based sessions throughout the week to improve strength, technique, and tactical skills. In the morning, athletes engage in approximately 60 minutes of land-based strength training. Water-based training involves 200 m of warm-up, technical swimming (e.g. 4 sets of 50 m), and interval sets (e.g. 8–10 sets of varying distances from 25 m to 75 m). Tactical drills, such as 6-on-5 offensive and defensive plays, are also incorporated. The total training time in the water ranges from 90–120 minutes.   Afternoon sessions focus on refining technical skills, such as passing, shooting, and defensive positioning. These drills typically last 15–20 minutes per set, with 4–6 sets per session. Athletes also work on small-range ball control and position-specific training, which includes 6-on-5 and 3-on-2 drills. The total duration for these exercises is approximately 90 minutes. Sample collection and storage   To establish a standardised metabolic baseline, all athletes underwent a standardised one-week complete break (washout period) following their previous competitive season, during which they completely abstained from all forms of structured land-based and water-based training to mitigate accumulated fatigue. Fasting venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected at 7:00 AM on two time points: the first sample (E1) was collected before commencement of the official training week, and the second sample (E2) was collected immediately after the completion of that week of training. The samples were drawn into 10 mL vacutainer tubes and labelled E1 (pre-training) and E2 (post-training). After collection, 2 mL of the supernatant obtained via centrifugation of the blood sample (4 °C, 3000 rpm for 15 minutes) was placed in a 5 mL EP tube, initially stored in liquid nitrogen, and then transferred to a −80 °C freezer for later analysis. 2.4. Serum sample thawing and preparation   Fasting blood samples were collected in 5 mL vacutainer tubes, after which the samples were centrifuged for 15 min (1500 × g, 4 °C). Each aliquot (50 μL) of the serum sample was stored at –80 °C until UPLC-Q-TOF/MS analysis. Serum samples were thawed on ice. For each sample, 50 µL was aliquoted, and 200 µL of extraction reagent (containing a mixed isotopic internal standard solution) was added. The mixture was placed on a shaker at 1000 rpm and vortexed for 10 minutes. After mixing, the tubes were centrifuged at 15000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 minutes. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a protein precipitation plate. An equal volume of the filtrate was transferred to a new centrifuge tube and subjected to low-temperature freeze-drying.   For detection, the freeze-dried samples were reconstituted with 200 µL of reconstitution solvent. Subsequently, 10 µL of the reconstituted sample was analysed via quantitative mass spectrometry.   Preparation of quality control (QC) samples: QC samples were prepared by mixing equal volumes of sample homogenates. The pretreatment process for the QC samples was identical to that for the experimental samples. QC samples were used to evaluate instrument performance, equilibrate the chromatography‒mass spectrometry system prior to analysis, and monitor the system stability and overall quality throughout the experiment.   Distribution of the QC samples: The QC samples were randomly distributed within the detection sequence to ensure uniform placement across all the analytical points. 2.6. Chromatography‒mass spectrometry analysis   2.6.1. Chromatographic conditions   Metabolite separation was performed via the HILIC approach with an Acquity™ BEH AMIDE column (Waters Corp., USA). The mobile phase consisted of water and acetonitrile, both of which contained 25 mM ammonium formate and were adjusted to pH 9.0. A linear gradient elution was applied, increasing the aqueous phase from 2% to 98% over 10 minutes, followed by a 2-minute column wash and re-equilibration. The flow rate was maintained at 0.4 mL/min, and the column temperature was set to 40 °C. Data acquisition was carried out in both positive and negative electrospray ionisation (ESI) modes. 2.6.2. Mass spectrometry conditions   Metabolite detection was performed via a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operated in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. The instrument was equipped with a heated electrospray ionisation (HESI) source. The ionisation voltage was set to 4.0 kV in positive mode and 3.5 kV in negative mode. The sheath gas and auxiliary gas flow rates were set to 45 and 10 arbitrary units, respectively. The capillary temperature was maintained at 320 °C, while the HESI probe heater temperature was set to 355 °C. The RF level of the S-lens was set at 50%.   Full MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 70,000 (at m/z 200), with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1 × 10⁶ and a maximum injection time of 100 ms. The scan range was set from m/z 70-1050. MS/MS spectra were acquired at a resolution of 17,500 (at m/z 200), with an AGC target of 1 × 10⁵ and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. Fragmentation was performed via higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a normalised collision energy (NCE) of 40%, and ultrapure nitrogen was used as the collision gas. 2.6.3. MS data processing and compound identification   Raw mass spectrometric data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.0 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which was employed for peak picking, peak alignment, compound identification, and quantitative analysis of metabolites. Metabolite identification was conducted in accordance with the confidence levels defined by the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) [ 22 ]. Specifically, Glutamic acid, Succinic acid, Phenylalanine, Fumaric acid, Asparagine, and Uric acid were categorised as Level 1 metabolites(confirmed identification). Their identification was validated by matching both retention time (RT) and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) spectra against authentic commercial standards that were analysed in-house. All remaining metabolites were designated as Level 2 (putative annotation), based on the matching of experimental MS/MS spectra to public spectral libraries including HMDB v5.0 and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), with a library match score threshold set at >0.7. Further spectral validation was additionally performed using the mzVault and mzCloud databases.   To improve the accuracy of metabolite identification, a panel of isotopically labelled internal standards (¹³C- and ¹⁵N-labelled; purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) was spiked into each sample prior to analysis. These internal standards included Alanine, Valine, Leucine, Phenylalanine, Glutamic acid, Tyrosine, Acetyl-L-carnitine, Propionyl-L-carnitine, Succinic acid, Fumaric acid, Glucose, Adenosine, and Uridine. The consistency of RT and fragmentation patterns between endogenous metabolites and their corresponding isotopically labelled internal standards was verified to ensure analytical reliability.   All analyses were performed on a Q Exactive HF-X hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer equipped with a heated electrospray ionisation (HESI) source. The mass accuracy tolerances were set at 20 ppm for full-scan MS (MS¹) and 0.02 Da for MS/MS (MS²), with an RT tolerance of 0.2 min. In positive ion mode, the adduct ions [M + H]⁺, [M + Na]⁺, and [M + NH₄]⁺ were considered for metabolite annotation; in negative ion mode, the adduct ions [M-H]⁻ and [M + FA-H]⁻ were taken into account.   The resulting quantitative data matrix was exported and imported into MetaboAnalyst 6.0 ( https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/ ) for pattern recognition, and the data were preprocessed by autoscaling and subjected to multidimensional statistical analysis, including unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA), supervised partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). Differentially metabolites between the two time points were initially screened using OPLS-DA Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) values. Metabolites with VIP > 1 were further evaluated using univariate paired t-tests. To control for potential false positives arising from multiple comparisons,  p- values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Metabolites meeting both VIP > 1 and FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly differential.   Cohen’s d_z was calculated as the effect size for paired comparisons, with effect sizes interpreted as small (0.2 ≤ |d_z| < 0.5), medium (0.5 ≤ |d_z| < 0.8), and large (|d_z| ≥ 0.8).
