related_tool: HMDB
sample_description: The sample used in the research consisted of colon tissues from SPF-grade female Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (56 days old), housed under controlled conditions (22–24°C, 50–60% relative humidity) at Hubei Minzu University. Rats were randomly assigned to a control group (n = 6) receiving daily gavage of water (0.5 mL/100 g body weight) or an experimental group (n = 6) receiving daily gavage of 70% ethanol extract of Dysosma versipellis (DV) at 33.75 mg/mL dissolved in water (0.5 mL/100 g body weight) for 5 consecutive days after 5-day acclimatization. One hour after the final gavage, rats were euthanized; colon tissues were excised, rinsed with saline, and either fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histopathology or snap-frozen for transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. For transcriptomics, colon tissue samples from three randomly selected rats per group were used; for metabolomics, colon tissues from all six rats per group were analyzed.
complete_workflow_description: Untargeted metabolomics analysis of colon tissue was performed using LC–MS/MS on a Vanquish UHPLC coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation used a Waters ACQUITY HSS T3 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm) at 40°C with mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and 4 µL injection volume. MS parameters included ESI+ ionization at 3.5 kV, capillary temperature at 320°C, probe temperature at 300°C, stepped collision energy (30, 40, 50 eV), and intensity threshold of 1 × 10⁵. Raw data were processed using MetaboAnalystR (v5.0 or later, implied by VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 criteria) for multivariate statistical analysis including PCA and OPLS-DA (with Variable Importance in Projection [VIP] > 1 and p < 0.05 for feature selection); metabolite annotation leveraged HMDB, KEGG, and MSEA databases; correlation analysis required |r| > 0.8 and p < 0.05; and integrative O2PLS modeling was conducted using OmicsPLS (v2.0.2) in R to jointly analyze differentially abundant metabolites and genes, with R² and loading plots used to identify key variables.

related_tool: HMDB
sample_description: The study utilized two main datasets: (1) a discovery dataset comprising 20 mouse lung adenocarcinoma tumor tissue samples, from which 7,353 LC–MS features were detected and subsequently cleaned to yield 294 HMDB-identified metabolites; and (2) a validation dataset comprising 81 mouse tumor tissue samples, yielding 22,000 total features and 582 HMDB-identified metabolites — with 183 shared HMDB IDs between discovery and validation sets. All tissue samples were frozen, pulverized on ice, and subjected to liquid–liquid extraction using ice-cold 80% methanol containing 2 µL of the Metabolomics QC kit (14 stable isotope-labeled internal standards). Protein pellets were resolubilized in 20 mM HEPES/8 M urea for Bradford-based protein quantification, and metabolite extracts were vacuum-dried and reconstituted in 80% methanol normalized to protein concentration. Human plasma samples followed analogous isotope-labeled internal standard–assisted extraction and HILIC-HRMS analysis.
complete_workflow_description: Data processing and machine learning were implemented using open-source and R-based tools. Raw LC–MS data were converted to mzXML using Raw Converter (Scripps) and imported into MZmine 3.53 for feature detection (mass tolerance: 10 ppm; RT tolerance: 0.25 min), alignment, and peak height export. Normalization was performed separately for positive and negative ion mode datasets using iterative rank order normalization (IRON) via custom command-line scripts (findmedian –pearson –iron-exclusions=unidentified_row_ids.txt –iron-spikeins=heavy_row_ids.txt; iron_generic –proteomics –iron-exclusions=unidentified_row_ids.txt –iron-spikeins=heavy_row_ids.txt), excluding heavy-labeled internal standards and unidentified rows. Normalized datasets were merged, log2-transformed (zero abundances treated as missing), and annotated via fuzzy matching to the curated HMDB/KEGG/PubChem table. Machine learning modeling was conducted in R (v4.3.2) using the tidymodels package (v1.1.1). The discovery dataset (294 identified metabolites) was split 75/25 into training/testing sets with stratified sampling. Ten models were evaluated — naïve Bayes, decision tree, trimmed decision tree, SVM, random forest, boosted tree, lasso regression, ridge regression, elastic net, and k-nearest neighbors (KNN) — under 12 preprocessing configurations (including 'basic preprocessing', i.e., no reduction, normalization, scaling, or augmentation). Hyperparameter tuning used default grid search with 10-fold stratified cross-validation, optimizing for accuracy. Final model selection prioritized rank-sum scores across four metrics: accuracy, ROC AUC, PR AUC ('non-lipid'), and PR AUC ('lipid'). The top four models — decision tree, trimmed decision tree, random forest, and KNN — were applied to the validation dataset (582 identified metabolites). Code is publicly available at https://github.com/biodatalab/leitzinger_2025.git.

complete_workflow_description: The study employed the In Silico Chemical Library Engine (ISiCLE) v1.0+ (github.com/pnnl/isicle), a Snakemake-based automated pipeline for high-accuracy quantum chemical property calculation. Input: SMILES strings. Workflow steps: (1) initial 3D conformation generation; (2) geometry optimization in solvent using B3LYP/3-21G with COSMO solvation model; (3) NMR isotropic shielding calculation at B3LYP/cc-pVDZ level using GIAO method for 13C and 1H nuclei. For validation, isotropic shieldings of 5 randomly selected molecules from each set were computed using three DFT methods to assess directional consistency. Synthetic 'surrogate experimental' NMR spectra were generated by adding zero-mean Gaussian noise to calculated shifts: σ ranged from 0.5–50 ppm (13C, step 0.05 ppm) and 0.1–10 ppm (1H, step 0.01 ppm). Identification workflow (Fig. 2): Step I—calculate all shifts via ISiCLE; Step II—generate noisy surrogate data; Step III—match experimental shifts to computed shifts using the Munkres assignment algorithm (minimizing sum of squared differences in an M×N cost matrix where M ≤ N); Step IV—score matches using RMSE (geometric mean of 13C-RMSE and 1H-RMSE when both nuclei used, to prevent dominance by carbon error scale); Step V—rank molecules by ascending RMSE. Analyses covered three scenarios: 1H-only, 13C-only, and combined 13C+1H identification. All Python scripts and workflows are in Additional file 4. Statistical evaluation used MAE, RMSE, CMAE, and Pearson correlation; DP4 was considered but rejected due to computational intensity and unsuitability for impure samples.
