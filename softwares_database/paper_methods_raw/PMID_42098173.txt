
Method
Experimental design
The previous version of this dataset, proposed in14, consisted of
2160 video sequences recorded from five different camera angles
with a resolution of 1280 × 720 pixels. It included 27 healthy
subjects (20 males and 7 females; mean age 35.52 ± 10.58) who
performed 16 different physical rehabilitation exercises for the
lower and upper limbs, and was recorded from June to July 2022.
The dataset was recorded in the Artificial Vision Applications lab at
the University of Córdoba during the development of the RehApp
project. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee
of the Reina Sofía University Hospital (Córdoba, Spain), with
reference number 5071, under the HIP-CI project, version 1,
entitled “ReHApp: Analysis by vision of postoperative evolution in
surgical interventions”. All participants provided written informed
consent for data collection and for the public release of anonymised
derived data. The dataset shared in this work includes only non-
identifiable representations, namely optical flow, silhouettes, and
semantic segmentation masks, which do not allow participants re-
identification. The subjects were recruited from the city of Córdoba,
and the inclusion criteria were age (older than 18 years) and the
ability to read, understand, and sign the corresponding informed
consent to be recorded. In addition, subjects were informed about
future methods for distributing data for scientific purposes. Subjects
were recruited regardless of physical condition to ensure greater
variability.
The aim of the recording of this dataset was to compare the
performance of state-of-the-art pose estimation models available at
the time of the original study when applied to a specific use case:
postoperative physical rehabilitation. This was addressed in the
mentioned study14, where the authors evaluated how these models
behave in the context of rehabilitation exercises, with a focus on
their accuracy and reliability for assisting patients in their recovery
process.
Data acquisition
In this new release, each participant is now associated with
demographic metadata (sex and age), and every repetition of each
exercise has been independently evaluated and scored by two PhD
physiotherapist from the University Hospital of Jaén (Spain) and
from the Federal University of São Carlos (Brasil), providing expert-
based quality labels that can be used for automatic assessment of
rehabilitation performance.
The scoring process followed a structured 5-point ordinal scale
based on three objective biomechanical pillars: Range of Motion
(ROM), Postural Control, and Execution Velocity. The therapist
evaluated whether the subject executed the full anatomical
trajectory (e.g., reaching the vertical plane in shoulder flexion),
maintained a stable spinal alignment without compensatory
movements, and adhered to an optimal angular velocity (ω ≈ 45°/
s) indicative of proper motor control. The complete grading criteria
are detailed in Table 1.

To assess the reliability of the clinical annotations, two PhD
physiotherapists independently scored the complete dataset
following the rubric defined in Table 1. Both raters performed their
annotations independently and were blinded to subject
demographics and to each other’s scores. Inter-rater agreement was
evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)16 and
Cohen’s weighted kappa17 The ICC(C,1) was
0.41(95%CI: 0.38 − 0.45), and Cohen’s quadratic weighted kappa
was 0.41, both indicating moderate agreement according to
standard interpretation criteria16. Exact agreement between raters
was observed in 64.1% of repetitions, and 97.0% of annotations
differed by at most one score point on the 5-point ordinal scale. The
moderate ICC value is consistent with the inherent subjectivity of
clinical movement assessment, where fine-grained distinctions
between adjacent score levels (e.g., 4 and 5) are particularly
challenging even for trained specialists. The high proportion of
annotations within one point of agreement (97.0%) reflects a solid
practical consistency between raters, supporting the reliability of
the dataset for training and evaluating machine learning models in
rehabilitation contexts.
Importantly, instead of generating separate video clips, the start and
end frames of each repetition have been annotated in the metadata
files, thus allowing researchers to isolate and analyze individual
repetitions without altering the original recordings. The repetitions
of the exercises have a minimum length of 15 frames (e.g., circular
pendulum), a maximum length of 675 frames, and an average
length of 151 frames.
In addition, several new data modalities have been derived from the
original RGB recordings, inspired by recent datasets such as
Health&Gait12: Silhouettes, Optical Flow and Semantic
Segmentation. Together with the previously available RGB videos
and 3D ground-truth joint positions, these additions make the
dataset a rich multi-modal resource for studying rehabilitation
exercises.
Data processing
To ensure a reliable performance evaluation, we split each sequence
into multiple repetitions of the same exercise. For that purpose, we
monitored one key joint per exercise to identify its maximum and
minimum ranges of motion within each sequence. A Fourier filter
was applied to remove high-frequency noise, using an adaptive
cutoff set to the dominant frequency of each signal (i.e., the
frequency with the highest power in the spectrum), and peaks and
valleys of the filtered signal were used to determine the start and
end of each repetition. An example is shown in Figure 1. These split
subsequences were then linked to the corresponding physiotherapist
score in the metadata files. This strategy ensures that complete and
isolated repetitions can be accurately retrieved without explicitly
segmenting the video sequences.

From the original RGB sequences, three additional visual modalities
were derived to enrich the dataset and facilitate multi-modal
analyses. Specifically, we computed silhouettes using instance
segmentation (YOLO11x-seg18) to capture the body outline while
discarding background information; optical flow using both
traditional (TV-L119) and learning-based (GMFlow20) methods to
represent dense motion fields between consecutive frames; and
semantic segmentation through DensePose-based models, assigning
a body-part label to each pixel of the subject and thus enabling fine-
grained motion and posture analysis. All the modalities can be
visualized in the Figure 2.

All processing steps were designed to ensure temporal alignment
across modalities, so that each frame in the RGB sequences has its
corresponding silhouette, optical flow, and semantic segmentation
representation. The resulting data are stored in standard image
formats to guarantee usability and easy integration with deep
learning frameworks, with a complete resolution of 1280 × 720
pixels. Due to storage constraints, the sequences have been
compressed into MP4 format (preserving the original quality), but
the corresponding source code is provided in the GitHub repository
for decomposing them into separate frames and replicating the
conditions of the experiments described in this article.
Silhouettes
Body silhouettes were extracted using an instance segmentation
model (YOLO11x-seg pre-trained on COCO dataset21). This
approach produces binary masks that capture the subject’s body
outline while removing background information.
