</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) and peripheral arterial disease (PAD) are common complications of diabetes mellitus, while diabetic peripheral neuropathy and peripheral arterial disease contribute to the pathogenesis of diabetic foot ulcers, and the pathogenic mechanisms between these three diseases still need further investigation. The keywords ‘diabetic foot ulcer’, ‘diabetic peripheral neuropathy’ and ‘atherosclerosis’ were used to search for related gene sets in the GEO database. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened and analysed for GO, KEGG and enrichR functional enrichment. Potential three disease biomarkers were identified by SVM‐SVM‐RFE and LASSO regression analysis. The results were also validated using external datasets and discriminability was measured by area under the ROC curve (AUC). Finally, biomarkers and co‐upregulated genes were analysed through the GSEA and Attie Laboratories diabetes databases. A total of 11 shared genes (KRT16, CD24, SAMD9L, SRGAP2, FGL2, GPR34, DDIT4, NFE2L3, FBLN5, ANXA3 and CPA3), two biomarkers (SAMD9L and FGL2) and one co‐upregulated gene (CD24) were screened. GO and KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs, enrichr enrichment analysis of shared differential genes and GSEA analysis of biomarkers showed that these significant genes were mainly focused on vasoregulatory, inflammatory‐oxidative stress and immunomodulatory pathways. In this study, we used bioinformatics to investigate the intrinsic relationship and potential mechanisms of three common lower extremity complications of diabetes and identified two pivotal genes using the LASSO model and the SVM‐RFE algorithm, which will further help clinicians to understand the relationship between diabetic complications, improve the diagnosis and treatment of diabetic foot problems and help doctors to identify the potential risk factors of diabetic foot.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> bioinformatics, biomarker, diabetic foot ulcer, diabetic peripheral neuropathy, peripheral artery disease</p></section></section><section id="iwj14748-sec-0003"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p>Diabetic foot ulcers are a serious problem for people with diabetes and can lead to significant health issues. Several factors contribute to their development, including peripheral neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease and local tissue infection. All of the above diseases are complications of diabetes, and the abnormal regulation of glucose in the body is a key factor in these pathologies. Damage to vascular endothelial cells, caused by elevated blood lipids and glucose levels, initiates atherosclerosis.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0001">
<sup>1</sup>
</a> This condition can lead to the narrowing or sclerosis of peripheral arteries, compromising vascular function and reducing blood flow. Consequently, distal limb ischaemia and hypoxia occur, delaying wound healing and increasing the risk of ulcer formation or even amputation. The combination of metabolic dysfunction and neurovascular impairment often results in peripheral neuropathy in diabetic individuals. This condition can lead to impaired blood perfusion and neural dysfunction in the distal limbs, causing pain or reduced sensation.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0002">
<sup>2</sup>
</a> Crucially, the occurrence of foot ulcers in individuals with diabetes is not exclusively attributed to perfusion deficits induced by arterial disease. Microcirculatory dysfunction, notably neuropathy, is a distinctive feature in such individuals. These alterations significantly impact the severity and outcomes of foot ulcers, alongside contributing factors like infection, abnormal biomechanical loading and common comorbidities such as nephropathy. Regrettably, determining the specific influence of microvascular dysfunction caused by atherosclerosis and diabetic peripheral neuropathy on the advancement of diabetic foot ulcers is challenging.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0003">
<sup>3</sup>
</a>
</p>
<p>The development of transcriptomics and bioinformatics provides an opportunity to gain novel insights into the influence of DFU, as well as DPN and PAD on DFU, through the identification of shared transcriptional features. This study aims to identify the gene pathways linked to the three conditions—DFU, DPN and PAD—and to pinpoint common genes and potential biomarkers for these conditions. This will further help clinicians understand the relationship between diabetes complications, enhance the diagnosis and treatment of diabetic foot issues, help doctors identify potential risk factors for diabetic foot and ultimately improve the management of diabetic foot to reduce disability and amputation rates, thereby alleviating the societal burden. The research flowchart of this research is shown in Figure <a href="#iwj14748-fig-0001" class="usa-link">1</a>.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/iwj14748-tbl-0001/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="iwj14748-sec-0007"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Screening of genes with differential expression</h3>
<p>Differential gene analysis for DFU, DPN and PAD was conducted using microarrays that profiled mRNA expression. The probe IDs corresponding to each gene were transformed into gene symbols. In the case of a gene symbol with multiple probe IDs, the average of these probe IDs is taken as the final expression value of the gene. We began by checking the quality of the data and normalizing it through log transformation to eliminate batch effects. For the identification of differently expressed genes (DEGs) among the DFU, DPN, PAD and control groups, we employed the ‘limma’ package in R software (version 4.3.0). A significance threshold was established at a <em>p</em>‐value &lt;0.05. The generation of a volcano map depicting DEGs was conducted using ‘ggVolcano’ package, and the visualization of DEGs was facilitated through the ‘Heatmap’ package. Moreover, the ‘FactoMineR’ and ‘factoextra’ package was utilized to evaluate gene patterns through principal component analysis (PCA).</p></section><section id="iwj14748-sec-0008"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis</h3>
<p>The ‘clusterProfiler’ R package was utilized for performing gene ontology (GO) analyses, including biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF) and cellular components (CC). Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analyses were conducted based on the DEGs. Visualization of the enrichment analysis outcomes was accomplished using the R packages ‘GO plot’ and ‘enrichplot’. Moreover, the ‘enrichR’ package was utilized to conduct GO‐Biological Process‐2018, GO‐Cellular Component‐2018, GO‐Molecular Function‐2018 and KEGG‐2016 pathway analyses on genes shared among the three diseases.</p></section><section id="iwj14748-sec-0009"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Screening of diagnostic biomarkers</h3>
<p>Using machine learning to analyse existing data and develop rules or models that predict unknown variables. In this study, we employed two algorithms: the LASSO dimension reduction method and SVM‐RFE. The LASSO algorithm is applied for feature selection in high‐dimensional gene expression data and selects the optimal regularization parameter through cross‐validation to filter the feature genes through the ‘glmnet’ package. The SVM‐RFE algorithm dynamically selects the optimal gene subset from gene expression data, evaluates model performance through cross‐validation and determines the target genes through the ‘e1071’, ‘kernlab’ and ‘caret’ package.</p></section><section id="iwj14748-sec-0010"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Validation and diagnostic assessment of biomarkers</h3>
<p>The predictive performance of biomarkers was evaluated by assessing their sensitivity and specificity using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, along with the calculation of the area under the ROC curve (AUC). External validation was performed using <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE68183" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE68183</a> for DFU, <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE185011" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE185011</a> for DPN and <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE28829" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE28829</a> for PAD. The diagnostic gene expression patterns in the validation sets were depicted using boxplots, and the AUC was calculated to assess their diagnostic accuracy.</p></section><section id="iwj14748-sec-0011"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.7. GSEA analysis of biomarkers and upregulated shared gene</h3>
<p>GSEA analyses were conducted on the co‐upregulated genes and biomarkers of DFU, DPN and PAD using the ‘clusterProfiler’ package. <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05 denotes statistical significance. The top three and bottom three major differential signalling pathways resulting from the enrichment analysis were visually presented.</p></section><section id="iwj14748-sec-0012"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.8. Determination of biomarkers with tissues from diabetic mice</h3>
<p>The potential biomarkers for DFU, DPN and PAD were identified and analysed using the Attie Lab Diabetes database (<a href="http://diabetes.wisc.edu" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://diabetes.wisc.edu</a>). The Attie Laboratory Diabetes Database serves as a repository for gene expression data, compiling profiles in six tissues from 4‐week‐old and 10‐week‐old lean and obese BTBR mice. This study specifically focuses on the gene expression of target genes in the gastrocnemius and flounder muscles.</p></section></section><section id="iwj14748-sec-0013"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. RESULTS</h2>

Conversely, the LASSO algorithm identified a subset of four genes: CD24, FGL2, KRT16 and SAMD9L (Figure <a href="#iwj14748-fig-0005" class="usa-link">5A–C</a>).</p>

<p>To enhance our comprehension of the connection between DFU, DPN and PAD, we intersected outcomes of the machine learning outcomes in the DFU, DPN and PAD groups, identifying two shared diagnostic genes, SAMD9L and FGL2 (Figure <a href="#iwj14748-fig-0005" class="usa-link">5J</a>). <p>DFU, DPN and PAD share certain pathogenic factors, and increasing evidence suggests that DPN and PAD exert influences on the occurrence and progression of DFU. Yet, the exact molecular mechanisms governing this association remain unclear.</p>
<p>The narrowing or atherosclerosis of peripheral arteries hampers vascular function and decelerates blood flow, resulting in ischaemia and hypoxia in the distal extremities. Peripheral arterial disease is a significant contributor to foot ulcers in about 50% of cases.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0009">
<sup>9</sup>
</a> Inadequate peripheral blood supply not only hinders wound healing but also intensifies infections. Atherosclerosis, a prevalent macrovascular complication of diabetes mellitus, induces endothelial dysfunction and inflammation, processes that are heightened in diabetic individuals.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0003">
<sup>3</sup>
</a>
</p>
<p>Diabetic neuropathy adversely impacts the sensory, motor and autonomic nervous systems, resulting in the loss of protective sensation, atrophy of the small foot muscles and diminished function of sweat and sebaceous glands—culminating in the formation of foot ulcers.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0010">
<sup>10</sup>
</a>
</p>
<p>Peripheral autonomic neuropathy induces vasomotor paresis, causing arteriovenous shunts within the subcutaneous vascular network. The historical characterization of DPN as a microvascular complication stems from the microvasculature damage to nerves.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0011">
<sup>11</sup>
</a>
</p>
<p>The hyperglycaemic state serves as a common pathogenic factor for all three conditions. Studies have revealed that the activation of adenosine triphosphate deficiency, polyol pathways, oxidative stress, protein kinase C‐activated metabolic pathways and pro‐inflammatory processes leads to microcirculatory atherosclerosis, heightened inflammation and abnormal endothelial growth, exacerbating ischaemic conditions.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0009">
<sup>9</sup>
</a>
</p>
<p>In this investigation, a bioinformatics approach was employed to uncover common DEGs among patients with DFU, DPN and PAD and identified one upregulated gene shared among the three conditions. Additionally, two diagnostic markers, SAMD9L and FGL2, were pinpointed through the outcomes of two machine learning algorithms.</p>
<p>SAMD9L, a recently discovered gene, remains relatively unexplored in the context of autoimmune diseases. However, its significance has been underscored in various physiological processes and the pathological advancement of tumours and viral infections.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0012">
<sup>12</sup>
</a> According to GTEx, SAMD9L is overexpressed in normal tissues in whole blood.</p>
<p>FGL2, also known as fibroleukin, functions as a prothrombinase and belongs to the fibrin superfamily. Two different versions of the FGL2 protein have been discovered: a membrane‐associated form known as mFGL2 and a soluble form referred to as sFGL2. mFGL2 associates with the phospholipids of cellular membranes and is expressed as a type II transmembrane protein, whereas sFGL2 is capable of being secreted into the bloodstream. The variation in tertiary structure between mFGL2 and sFGL2 indicates that these two forms of FGL2 might have different functional roles.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0013">
<sup>13</sup>
</a> The expression of the FGL2 gene varies among cell types, with the highest levels found in monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages and specific cells such as Kupffer and Langerhans cells. Enterocytes, keratinocytes, alveolar cells and glia cells have a minimal level of FGL2 expression.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0014">
<sup>14</sup>
</a> FGL2 is expressed downregulated in skin tissues of diabetic foot and on atherosclerotic plaques, but upregulated in peripheral blood in diabetic peripheral neuropathy, possibly considering two different expression patterns of FGL2. Increased expression of auto DNA and the antimicrobial peptide cramp/LL37 in atherosclerotic lesions may stimulate plasma cell‐like dendritic cell‐driven autoimmune activation pathway and production of anti‐double‐stranded DNA antibodies, severely exacerbating atherosclerotic lesion formation.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0008">
<sup>8</sup>
</a> FGL2 has immunosuppressive properties, inhibiting T‐cell proliferation and suppressing maturation of bone marrow‐derived dendritic cells.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0015">
<sup>15</sup>
</a> FGL2 is associated with multiple types of microcirculatory disorders. Ping Zhang et al. discovered a strong correlation between FGL2 expression and the size of cerebral infarcts. They also found that although the expression levels of other inflammatory factors were significantly higher, they had a weaker correlation with the size of cerebral infarctions.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0016">
<sup>16</sup>
</a> Guanhua Su et al. proposed that FGL2 might trigger microthrombosis in the kidneys, potentially causing glomerular hypertension and renal ischaemia. FGL2 is associated with processes like apoptosis, angiogenesis and inflammatory responses, all of which play a role in diabetes.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0017" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0017">
<sup>17</sup>
</a>
</p>
<p>CD24 is a heavily glycosylated protein. Its presence is observed in diverse cell types, encompassing myelocytes, keratinocytes, macrophages, renal tubular epithelial cells, muscle fibres, activated T cells, neurons and ganglion cells.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0018">
<sup>18</sup>
</a> The widespread occurrence of metabolic disorders poses a substantial risk to human health, with chronic inflammation recognized as a major contributing factor. However, the host factors responsible for controlling chronic inflammation remain largely unidentified.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0019">
<sup>19</sup>
</a> To explore the influence of CD24 in metabolic disorders, Wang et al. performed a comparative analysis involving CD24 and CD24 littermate mice. The research specifically examined the onset of metabolic disorders, including obesity, dyslipidaemia and insulin resistance, under the conditions of a high‐fat diet and ageing. The findings highlighted the pivotal role of CD24 in mitigating metabolic disorders.<a href="#iwj14748-bib-0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="iwj14748-bib-0020">
<sup>20</sup>
</a>
</p>
<p>We need to recognize the limitations of this microarray data analysis study, namely, the lack of experimental validation. Further investigations, both at the basic and clinical levels, are essential to delve into the alterations of these hub genes and signalling pathways. This is particularly crucial for genes not previously linked to these three diseases, aiming to achieve a more profound comprehension of the interplay among the three conditions.</p></section><section id="iwj14748-sec-0021"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">5. CONCLUSION</h2>
<p>In conclusion, this study examined the DEGs in DFU, DPN and PAD, aiming to uncover potential shared molecular mechanisms contributing to the development of these conditions using bioinformatics. The convergence of the LASSO model and SVM‐RFE algorithm identified two hub genes. To elucidate the relationship between DFU, DPN and PAD, as well as the specific common molecular mechanisms, further systematic clinical investigations involving patients and fundamental research utilizing animal models are warranted based on the outcomes of this data analysis.</p></section><section id="iwj14748-sec-0104"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CONFLICT OF INTEREST STATEMENT</h2>
