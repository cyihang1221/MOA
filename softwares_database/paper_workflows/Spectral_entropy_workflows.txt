related_tool: Spectral entropy
sample_description: A total of 121 small-molecule exposome compounds (100–200 Da) were selected from the Blood Exposome Database based on three criteria: (i) availability of reference [M+H]+ CID MS/MS spectra in the NIST 2023 database (positive ionization mode), (ii) frequent occurrence in peer-reviewed metabolomics and exposomics literature, and (iii) molecular weight between 100 and 200 Da. Chemical identifiers (InChiKey, SMILES) and 3D structure coordinates were retrieved from PubChem. Compounds were categorized as cyclic (C; containing ≥1 ring) or acyclic (A; no ring) to inform QCxMS parameter selection. For each compound, protonated forms ([M+H]+) were generated using CREST v3.0.2 with GFN2-xTB semiempirical quantum method, retaining all topologically unique protomers within a 30 kcal/mol energy window relative to the global minimum; protomers were labeled sequentially (protomer_0 = lowest energy, ΔE = 0; protomer_1, protomer_2, etc., increasing in energy). A total of 18 compounds yielded only one protomer, 27 yielded two, and 76 yielded three or more (up to ten for sesamol). All protomers were used as independent inputs for QCxMS simulations.
complete_workflow_description: The workflow employed QCxMS v5.2.1 to generate in silico CID-MS/MS spectra using GFN2-xTB quantum method with D4 dispersion corrections. Structural preparation involved: (1) conversion of PubChem 3D coordinates to XYZ format using Open Babel v not specified; (2) geometry optimization with xTB v6.7.0’s ANCopt optimizer; (3) generation of [M+H]+ protomers via CREST v3.0.2 with GFN2-xTB and 30 kcal/mol energy window. A hierarchical 'protomer-parameter grid' strategy was implemented: each protomer underwent 11 distinct QCxMS parameter sets, stratified by compound class. For cyclic compounds: n2-maxcol4–60, n2-setcol4–60, n2-maxcol4–80, n2-setcol4–80, n2-maxcol6–60, n2-setcol6–60, n2-maxcol6–80, n2-setcol6–80, n2–60, n2-esi-maxcol4–60, n2-esi-setcol4–60. For acyclic compounds: n2-maxcol4–40, n2-setcol4–40, n2-maxcol4–25, n2-setcol4–25, n2-maxcol6–25, n2-setcol6–25, n2-maxcol6–40, n2-setcol6–40, n2–40, n2-esi-maxcol4–40, n2-esi-setcol4–40. Collision types included fullauto (default chamber length, pressure, 300 K), maxcoll (precursor-gas collisions only, X=4 or 6), and setcoll (precursor- and fragment-gas collisions, X=4 or 6); elab values were 25/40 eV (acyclic) or 60/80 eV (cyclic); esi keyword enabled thermal preactivation scaling internal energy by ion size. Each QCxMS run followed a three-stage protocol: (1) NVT MD equilibration at 500 K (0.5 fs timestep); (2) NVE conformer sampling; (3) collisional MD production: 25 trajectories/atom for fullauto, 80 trajectories for maxcoll/setcoll (0.5 fs, leapfrog integrator). Simulated spectra were compiled into JCAMP-DX format using PlotMS v6.2.0, then converted to NIST MSP format via in-house Python scripts. All computations were performed on cloud-based Intel Xeon Gold 6312U CPUs (24 cores, 48 threads) and a local Intel Xeon E5–2687W v2 server (16 cores, 32 threads).

related_tool: Spectral entropy
sample_description: The study utilized multiple sample types: (1) an in-house standard database (CQMD) comprising 4535 compounds including endogenous metabolites, gut microbial metabolites, medicine and food homology compounds, and common clinical drugs; (2) inter-instrument validation samples consisting of 150 metabolites spiked in positive ionization mode (CP) and 134 metabolites in negative ionization mode (CN), prepared by spiking into pooled plasma from six healthy volunteers at a final concentration of 2 μM; (3) real plasma samples from a metabolomics cohort of patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and healthy controls; and (4) quality control (QC) samples generated by pooling 5 μL of each sample supernatant. All plasma samples adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethics approval from the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. 2023–044), with a waiver of informed consent for residual clinical specimens.
complete_workflow_description: The method employed two primary computational workflows—longitudinal profiling (LFP)-based and cross-sectional profiling-based identification—both implemented using custom Python code available on GitHub (https://github.com/academicCQMD/LongitudinalProf-MSMS-Method, files 001–007). For longitudinal profiling: (1) LFPs were constructed as vectors of RIIs for each identical m/z fragment across 13 individual NCEs (1–100%); (2) Jaccard similarity scores were calculated per LFP between query and reference spectra using Eq. 1; (3) LFPs were ranked by total RII across NCEs (Yi), and the index I (first rank where Si < 0.5) and ratio I/N were computed; (4) identification criteria were optimized via ROC analysis of I/N ratios across LFP count bins (3–7, 8–12, etc.), yielding mode-specific cutoffs (e.g., CP mode cutoffs ranged to achieve AUC 0.874–0.991); (5) candidates were filtered and ranked by 'criterion factor' (ratio of LFPs with Si ≥ 0.5 within top-k ranks to required k), with tie-breaking based on count, ranking position, and sum of Si. For cross-sectional profiling: (1) full MS/MS spectra at each NCE and stepped NCE were vectorized by RIIs; (2) similarity was computed using dot product, entropy, or Jaccard algorithms across 14 spectral acquisitions; (3) candidates were filtered by threshold-based scoring (Eqs. 4–5), selecting the highest-scoring candidate above threshold. Data processing included noise removal, invalid fragment filtering (RII < 2% set to 0), mass tolerance of 0.01 m/z for fragment alignment, and use of MS-DIAL (default parameters) for deconvolution, peak picking, and alignment of real plasma QC samples. All spectral matching used 10 ppm mass tolerance for candidate selection from CQMD.

complete_workflow_description: The study employed multiscale entropy (MSE) and spectral power density (SPD) analyses on pre-processed resting-state EEG data to assess intrinsic neural dynamics before and after cognitive training. MSE was calculated per 2500-ms epoch, then averaged across epochs and electrodes for each participant separately in pre- and post-training conditions. Statistical analyses used partial least squares (PLS), a multivariate data-driven technique, with 500 permutations for significance testing of latent variables (LVs) and 500 bootstrap samples to assess electrode weight stability; results were visualized using the Scientific colour map 'vik' [147].

related_tool: Spectral entropy
sample_description: The study used MS/MS spectra sampled from multiple public metabolomics repositories: MassBank.us, GNPS (Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking), MassIVE/GNPS, MetabolomicsWorkbench.org, MetaboLights, and the West Coast Metabolomics Center. As of March 2023, over 939 million spectra were available — specifically 237,185,147 negative electrospray ionization (ESI) and 701,996,947 positive ESI MS/MS spectra. For benchmarking, between 100 and 1,000,000 spectra were randomly sampled using numpy.random.choice (NumPy v.1.23.5). All spectra were centroided with a mass tolerance of Δm/z < 50 mDa, denoised by removing fragment ions with intensity <1% of the most abundant fragment ion, and normalized so that the sum of all peak intensities equaled 0.5. Spectra were preprocessed to remove non-fragmented precursor ions and any ions with m/z > (precursor m/z − 1.6), consistent with NIST MS Search v.3.0 settings.
complete_workflow_description: The process procedure centers on the 'Flash entropy' algorithm for fast, memory-efficient MS/MS spectral library searching. The workflow includes: (1) Preprocessing: removal of precursor ions (m/z > precursor m/z − 1.6), denoising (<1% max intensity), centroiding (Δm/z < 50 mDa), and normalization to sum intensity = 0.5; (2) Library indexing: spectral entries are converted into tuples (spectrum_id, fragment_mz, intensity), sorted by fragment m/z, and indexed for O(log n) lookup; for hybrid search, two parallel indexes are built—one sorted by fragment m/z (fragment table) and one by neutral loss mass (neutral loss table), with cross-referenced continuous ion numbers to prevent double-counting; (3) Query execution: for each query spectrum, all fragment ions are matched against the indexed library within ±20 mDa (open/neutral loss/hybrid) or ±10 mDa (identity); (4) Similarity accumulation: for each matched ion pair (i,j), the contribution f(I_A,i + I_B,j) − f(I_A,i) − f(I_B,j) is added to the total similarity score, where f(x) = x log₂x; (5) Search modes: identity search restricts library candidates by precursor m/z ±10 mDa before computing equation (5); open search ignores precursor constraints entirely; neutral loss search transforms all library fragment m/z to neutral loss masses (precursor m/z − fragment m/z) prior to matching; hybrid search performs sequential matching—first against fragment table, then against neutral loss table, skipping already-matched ions; (6) Memory optimization: memory usage scales as O(s + p), where s = number of library spectra and p = max matched peaks per query; (7) Time complexity: O(n × m × Δ), where n = query peaks, m = library peaks, and Δ = mass tolerance window; (8) Benchmarking: executed on AMD Ryzen 9 3900X (12-core), 64 GB RAM, NVMe SSD; wall-clock time measured via /usr/bin/time -v; memory limits enforced via systemd-run --scope -p MemoryMax=4G/16G; comparisons made against reference implementations: YuanyueLi/SpectralEntropy (v.1.0.0) for native entropy similarity, BLINK (v. Feb 2023) for dot-product scoring, and MatchMS (v.0.18.0) CosineGreedy for cosine similarity.

complete_workflow_description: The LibGen pipeline implements an end-to-end automated spectral library generation workflow: (1) Raw MS/MS data acquisition across three dissociation techniques (HCD, UVPD, EAD) on high-resolution instruments (IQ-X Exactive and SCIEX 7600 ZenoTOF); (2) Mass error correction; (3) Denoising via subformula assignment — i.e., identifying fragment ions that correspond to chemically plausible neutral losses from the parent molecular formula; (4) Quality control using two metrics — 'explained intensity' (fraction of total spectral intensity attributable to fragments explainable by subformula losses) and 'spectral entropy' (quantifying information richness of the spectrum); (5) Filtering out low-quality spectra where >10% of total intensity is assigned to unexplained (noise) ions; (6) Compilation of curated, high-confidence reference spectra into three distinct spectral libraries (HCD, UVPD, EAD), each containing spectra from 2241 structurally diverse natural products. complete_workflow_description: The paper describes a complete end-to-end computational workflow for peptide identification and rescoring in diverse LC-MS/MS contexts. Raw data (mzML or MGF) are first searched using MSFragger (v3.4 or v3.7 depending on application) within FragPipe (v17.2 or v19.2), generating pepXML and pin files. MSBooster then processes the pin files along with corresponding spectral files to compute deep learning–derived features—including unweighted spectral entropy, delta RT loess, and IM probability uniform prior—by querying pre-trained models only on MSFragger-reported peptides (not the full theoretical proteome). For DIA data, MSBooster implements peak removal logic borrowed from MSFragger-DIA to avoid double-counting fragment ions across co-eluting peptides. After feature augmentation, the enhanced pin files are passed to Percolator (with deterministic seeding enabled via random seed control) for semi-supervised machine learning–based PSM scoring and FDR estimation.
