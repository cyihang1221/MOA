</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ABS1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="P3">Colorectal cancer (CRC) incidence in younger adults has been steadily rising, warranting an in-depth investigation into the distinctions between early-onset CRC (EOCRC, &lt; 50 years) and late-onset CRC (LOCRC, ≥ 50 years). Despite extensive study of clinical, pathological, and molecular traits, differentiating EOCRC from LOCRC and identifying potential biomarkers remain elusive. We analyzed plasma samples from healthy individuals, EOCRC, and LOCRC patients using liquid-chromatography mass spectrometry (LC/MS)-based metabolomics and lipidomics. Distinct polar metabolite and lipid profiles with significant metabolites altered in CRC group (e.g., choline and DG 40:4) were identified. Notably, EOCRC exhibited distinct polar metabolomic and differential lipidomic profiles compared to LOCRC, with polar metabolites like aminoadipate and uridine contributing significantly originating from pathways such as lysine biosynthesis and nucleotide metabolism. Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using TCGA gene expression data identified pathways significantly enriched in either EOCRC or LOCRC. Integrating gene expression and metabolomics data revealed numerous associations differentiating EOCRC and LOCRC. Our multi-omics integration underscores critical molecular distinctions, offering insights into EOCRC development mechanisms and potential plasma biomarkers for diagnosis.</p>

<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/71de/11181242/ef423a4779d0/nihms-1993735-f0001.jpg" loading="lazy" height="411" width="600" alt="graphic file with name nihms-1993735-f0001.jpg"></p></section><section id="S1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="P5">Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cause of cancer deaths in men and women in the United States.[<a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">1</a>, <a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">2</a>] Presently, clinical diagnosis of colorectal cancer (CRC) encompasses methods such as virtual computed tomography colonoscopy, endorectal ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging. Ongoing research endeavors persist in the advancement of innovative diagnostic technologies for CRC.[<a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a>, <a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a>] Although its incidence rate has been constantly decreasing since 1999 with the implementation of CRC screening for adults aged 50 and older, there is an increased incidence rate among adults younger than 50 years old, which has triggered the alarm among health care community.[<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>] According to the National Cancer Institute’s SEER registry, the rate of CRC will increase more dramatically in age group between 20 to 49 by 2030. <sup>2</sup> However, with the increasing CRC occurrence in younger adults, its underlying mechanism remains unclear. Therefore, research efforts have been made to study the different clinical presentations, pathological subtypes and molecular features between early-onset CRC (EOCRC, at age &lt; 50 years) and late-onset CRC (LOCRC, at age ≥ 50 years). Approximately 20% of early-onset (EO) CRC cases can be attributed to germline mutations that cause familial cancer syndromes, whereas about 80% EOCRCs are sporadic.[<a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a>–<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a>] Studies reported high prevalence of P53 gene loss[<a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">9</a>]<sup>i</sup> and hypomethylation of long interspersed nuclear elements-1 (LINE-1) in EOCRC.[<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a>] Multi-omics analysis also revealed that in EOCRC patients, genes related to antioxidant activity, the Glutathione S-Transferase Mu 1/2/3 (GSTM), were significantly downregulated and integrated tissue transcriptomics. Plasma metabolomics analysis showed that there was significant glutathione metabolism imbalance in EOCRC population.[<a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a>]</p>
<p id="P6">Despite these recent progresses, there is still much to be learned about the etiologies causing the progressively increasing incidence of EOCRC, and the potential molecular changes in circulation that can reflect the pathological changes of the tumors. In order to develop our understanding about the molecular characteristics of EOCRC compared to LOCRC, we conducted an analysis by characterizing LC/MS-based plasma metabolic and lipidomic profiles from EOCRC, LOCRC patients, and compared them with the healthy control groups (CRC-free). In addition, to determine metabolome-gene association in EOCRC population, the Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA) [<a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">12</a>] was interrogated and the results were integrated with the metabolomics/lipidomics analyses.</p></section><section id="S2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Chemicals</h3>
<p id="P7">HPLC-MS-grade acetonitrile, isopropanol, ammonium acetate, formic acid, and acetic acid were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA, USA). <p id="P8">Plasma samples of EOCRC and LOCRC patients utilized in this study were obtained from the Ohio State University Biospecimen Services Total Cancer Care Program. Healthy control samples were collected from Dec 2021 to Apr 2022 at Ohio State University Clinical Laboratory and were aliquoted and stored at −80°C, with the institutional review board approval at The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center (IRB#2022C0138). Basic demographic information is reported in <a href="#T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>. Demographic information with more details was listed in <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/T1/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="S5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Plasma Sample Preparation</h3>
<p id="P9">Polar metabolites and lipids were extracted and analyzed with LC/MS-based metabolomics platform according to our established protocols[<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">13</a>, <a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a>]. For polar metabolites extraction, 50 µL plasma samples were mixed with methanol and <sup>13</sup>C<sup>15</sup>N-labeled internal standard. After vortex, samples were stored in −20℃ freezer for 20 minutes to precipitate protein. 150 µL supernatant was loaded onto sample tray in Vanquish UHPLC System (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA). For lipids extraction, 20 µL plasma samples were mixed with iso-propanol and isotope-labeled lipid standards. After vigorous mixing, samples were sonicated on ice water bath for 200 minutes and incubated at 4℃ for 30 minutes. After centrifuging, 150 µL supernatant was collected for mass spectrometry analysis.</p></section><section id="S6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC/MS) Analysis</h3>
<p id="P10">Both metabolomics and lipidomics analyses were performed on a Vanquish UHPLC system (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) in combination with a hybrid Quadrupole Orbitrap Q Exactive<sup>™</sup> mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Both positive and negative modes were utilized for broader coverage. Different columns were used for different types of compound separation: Xbridge BEH Amide (2.5µm, 2.1×150mm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) for polar metabolite analysis and Acquity UPLC CSH C18 column (1.7µm, 2.1×100mm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) for lipid analysis. Detailed LC/MS information were reported in <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>. In addition, pooled quality control (QC) samples for each type of metabolites were ran every ten biological samples to constantly evaluate instrument stability.</p></section><section id="S7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. TCGA Data Acquisition and GSEA Pathway Analysis</h3>
<p id="P11">The normalized colon adenocarcinoma mRNAseq data and clinical data was downloaded from the Broad Institute (<a href="https://gdac.broadinstitute.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gdac.broadinstitute.org</a>). The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA), openly accessible for global research use, gathers diverse genomic, epigenomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data to facilitate advancements in cancer diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. By using R studio (4.2.2), gene expression and clinical data were merged, and patients were dichotomized into EOCRC and LOCRC groups. Pathway enrichment analysis for the grouped data were analyzed using KEGG and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) software.[<a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">15</a>, <a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">16</a>] Nominal p-value and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-value of each regulated pathway was calculated by GSEA software algorithm.</p></section><section id="S8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Statistical Analysis</h3>
<p id="P12">Polar metabolites analysis was processed by Compound Discoverer 3.1 (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) cross-referencing our in-house database. Raw files from lipid analysis were processed by MSDIAL software (<a href="http://prime.psc.riken.jp/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://prime.psc.riken.jp</a>) cross-referencing online databases provided by MSDIAL. Exported data sheets were proceeded for filtering steps including compound annotation and coefficient of variation (CV) of QC samples &lt; 25%. Remaining polar metabolites and lipids with their peak area information were uploaded to R studio, log10-transformed, and scaled for principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), variable importance of projection (VIP) score analysis and volcano plot generation.</p></section></section><section id="S9"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. TCGA-based Gene-Metabolite Interaction</h3>
<p id="P18">As metabolite and lipid level changes are generally considered as downstream events of genomics and transcriptomic changes, we seek to further leverage existing information from TCGA to generate gene-metabolite interaction networks thorough molecular level investigation of the differences between EOCRC and LOCRC. Transcriptomics and patients’ clinical data (47.2% female, 66.97±13.04 years old, 46.5% white) were downloaded from TCGA website and GSEA analysis identified a total of 21 KEGG pathways in which 1 pathway (neuroactive ligand receptor interaction, FDR&lt;0.01) was enriched in the EOCRC group while 20 pathways (e.g., antigen processing and presentation, regulation of autophagy, type I diabetes mellitus, FDR&lt;0.01) were enriched in the LOCRC group (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Table S3</a>). In these 21 pathways, significantly regulated genes were summarized and uploaded to MetaboAnalyst together with polar metabolites with VIP score larger than 1 (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Table S4</a>) for gene-metabolite interaction network. <a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a> showed associations among different metabolites and different genes revealed in our omics-level analyses. Four metabolites, L-lysine, cytosine, choline, and L-glutamic acid were included in the interaction map. Sixty-two genes were also involved in this interaction (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Table S4</a>). These genes were engaged in pathways like neuroactive ligand receptor interaction, cytosolic DNA sensing pathway, toll like receptor signaling pathway, starch metabolism, and sucrose metabolism. These interactions showed possible biological pathways and/or physiological functions that were dysregulated and impacted in EOCRC patients.</p>

<p id="P19">In this study, we utilized LC/MS-based metabolomics analysis to differentiate large-scale polar metabolome and lipidome from plasma samples in the CRC-free/healthy, EOCRC, and LOCRC populations. Metabolomics techniques could sensitively detect unique metabolites in EOCRC population. The distinct metabolic profiles between CRC-free control group and general CRC group indicating that metabolic pathways have been dysregulated in CRC patients which is consistent with previous studies and has been recognized as a feature of CRC prognosis.[<a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">17</a>–<a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a>] Among the top five metabolites displaying significant regulation, urea was the sole metabolite to exhibit an increase in the colorectal cancer (CRC) groups. The present review summarizes previous extensive investigations on the association between the urea cycle and cancer.[<a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a>] Several studies have proposed elevated levels of urea cycle metabolites, including ornithine and urea, in cancer patients.[<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a>–<a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">24</a>] Notably, ornithine can be catabolized by ornithine decarboxylase, leading to the generation of polyamines that play a crucial role in the proliferation of CRC cells. In addition, dysregulation of lipid metabolism in cancer cells is gradually recognized as one of the characteristics of CRC, associated with a poorer prognosis and shorter disease-free survival.[<a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">25</a>, <a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a>] Increased sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid profiles in CRC cell lines and tumor cells were reported[<a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a>, <a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a>] which is also consistent with our finding of increasing level of SM 34:2;2O and LPC 16:0. As a result, metabolomics analysis advanced our understanding about differential polar metabolome and lipidome between CRC-free control and CRC population.</p>
<p id="P20">Diving deeper into the comparison of EOCRC and LOCRC’s metabolic profiles, our analyses indicated largely distinct metabolome with unique molecular characteristics. As previously mentioned, a multiomics analysis done to study the etiologies behind increasing incidence of EOCRC revealed thirty-five metabolites, mostly lipids, yielded significant interaction between EOCRC and LOCRC populations.[<a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a>] Among these thirty-five metabolites, twenty-nine were from phosphatidylcholine (PC) class. In our study, we also reported that there were increasing trend of PC 34:2, PC 36:3, PC O-38:7 in EOCRC group while PC 34:3 showed decreasing trend among top 15 lipids contributed most to the difference between EOCRC and LOCRC groups. On the other hand, urea, metabolite from urea cycle, has showed higher plasma abundance in LOCRC population, which is also linked in tumor metabolism including synthesis of nitric oxide and polyamines associated with tumor vascular invasion.[<a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">28</a>, <a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29">29</a>] In addition, most amino acids (L-lysine, L-glutamic acid, L-tyrosine) in top 15 most significantly different polar metabolite showed increasing trend in EOCRC group except L-threonine. Amino acid metabolism has been implicated in macrophage function during the development of colorectal cancer, involving the regulation of key enzymes such as GPX4 and GSR[<a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">30</a>] Notably, previous research has reported alterations in the concentrations of tyrosine and lysine in CRC patients compared to healthy controls, across various studies and sample matrices.[<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a>–<a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">33</a>] These findings suggest the potential utility of lysine and tyrosine as prospective biomarkers for distinguishing EOCRC and LOCRC. Furthermore, glutamic acid was associated with IL1B, IL6, and NFĸB1, which are genes encoded for proteins critical in inflammation process. This is consistent with altered adipose tissue inflammation level evidenced by adipose tissue NFκB1, IL6, and IL1B gene expression higher in CRC population compared to healthy control subjects.[<a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34">34</a>] In our study, metabolomics and lipidomics analyses showed consistent changing trend with previous research as well as unique plasma molecules, and potential physiological pathways in EOCRC population compared to LOCRC population. Our overall multi-omics results suggested that there may be critical molecular level differences between EOCRC and LOCRC that not only can help us to understand the underlying mechanism of EOCRC development, but also identify plasma biomarkers for EOCRC diagnosis.</p>
<p id="P21">Last but not least, we fully acknowledge that there were limitations of this study. The study population was relatively small, which may limit the translational value of this study to a broader population. With predominately white and male study participants, we also acknowledge the potentially biased results generated, which could be due to the skewed demographic representation. Differences in plasma metabolite profiles based on age represent an additional limitation given the inherent age variations between the EOCRC and LOCRC cohorts. Also, the metabolomics and genomics analyses between EOCRC and LOCRC groups were not generated from the same batch of clinical samples, which could lead to inherent variations among data points when doing metabolites and genes interaction analysis. Therefore, we will continue our future studies by including more CRC patients with more diverse demographic background and using MS-based metabolomics/ lipidomics analyses to support and validate findings from this study.</p></section><section id="S14"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Data Availability Statement</h2>
