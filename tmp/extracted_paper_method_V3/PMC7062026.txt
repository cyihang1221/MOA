</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>In the perpetual struggle between high-speed predators and their prey, individuals need to react in the blink of an eye to avoid capture. Alarm calls that warn of danger therefore need to do so sufficiently rapidly that listeners can escape in time. Paradoxically, many species produce more elements in their alarm calls when signalling about more immediate danger, thereby increasing the reliability of transmission of critical information but taking longer to convey the urgent message. We found that New Holland honeyeaters, <em>Phylidonyris novaehollandiae</em>, incorporated more elements in alarm calls given to more dangerous predators, but video analysis revealed that listeners responded in 100 ms, after only the first element. Consistent with this rapid response, the acoustic structure of the first element varied according to the danger, and playbacks confirmed that birds need hear only the first element to assess risk. However, birds hid for longer and were more likely to flee, after calls with more elements. The dual mechanisms of varying both element structure and number may provide a widespread solution to signalling rapidly and reliably about immediate danger.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> alarm call, anti-predator, signal design, communication, predation</p></section></section><section id="s1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>Split-seconds are all that separate the quick and the dead. Fast-moving predators can reach speeds of over 50 m s<sup>−1</sup> [<a href="#RSPB20192772C1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C1">1</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C2">2</a>], so potential prey need to react rapidly. A delay in response of even 0.5 s can represent a gain of up to 25 m by an attacking predator. Experimental studies show that vertebrates can initiate escape in less than 0.5 s of a model predator's attack [<a href="#RSPB20192772C3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C3">3</a>–<a href="#RSPB20192772C7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C7">7</a>], and, even in this short time, prey can adjust their escape strategy according to the type of predator, its proximity and location [<a href="#RSPB20192772C8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C8">8</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C9">9</a>]. However, in natural circumstances, individuals do not always detect the approaching predator themselves [<a href="#RSPB20192772C7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C7">7</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C10">10</a>], but instead respond to information on danger encoded in the alarm calls given by others [<a href="#RSPB20192772C11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C11">11</a>]. Surprisingly, despite selection for rapid responses to danger [<a href="#RSPB20192772C12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C12">12</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C13">13</a>], the speed of response to alarm calls has generally been overlooked [<a href="#RSPB20192772C14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C14">14</a>–<a href="#RSPB20192772C16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C16">16</a>], and there has been no study on how alarm calls are designed to allow both rapid and reliable communication.</p>
<p>Alarm calls vary in the information they convey about threats, some of which require more immediate action than others. Species can use acoustically distinct vocalizations to signal about the category of predator and to differentiate between threats that select for different responses in prey [<a href="#RSPB20192772C17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C17">17</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C18">18</a>]. For example, Japanese tits, <em>Parus minor</em>, produce a specific call to warn their nestlings of an approaching snake as opposed to less threatening nest predator species, as snakes are particularly dangerous and the young need to jump out of the cavity to avoid consumption [<a href="#RSPB20192772C19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C19">19</a>]. In some species, predator behaviour determines the immediacy of the danger: flying raptors represent an urgent threat and prompt a different alarm call to when they are perched and pose a reduced risk [<a href="#RSPB20192772C20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C20">20</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C21">21</a>]. Urgency can also be governed by predator proximity, and in species like the great gerbil, <em>Rhombomys opimus</em>, and the Arabian babbler, <em>Turdiodes squamiceps</em>, individuals switch between different call types according to how close the threat is [<a href="#RSPB20192772C22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C22">22</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C23">23</a>]. Alarm calls can even provide multiple messages: meerkats, <em>Suricata suricatta</em>, give different calls to aerial and terrestrial predators and, across alarm types, produce noisier calls when facing more dangerous threats [<a href="#RSPB20192772C24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C24">24</a>], while white-browed scrubwrens, <em>Sericornis frontalis</em>, use more elements in their aerial alarm calls when the predator flies closer [<a href="#RSPB20192772C25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C25">25</a>]. Overall, warning signals can increase receiver survival [<a href="#RSPB20192772C26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C26">26</a>] and must communicate information easily and rapidly, especially in urgent situations.</p>
<p>Alarm calls have proved excellent models for studying the relationship between form and function in signal design. In a classic example, aerial alarm calls are often high-frequency, narrow-band calls, enabling the caller to warn conspecifics to flee without revealing its location to the flying predator [<a href="#RSPB20192772C27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C27">27</a>–<a href="#RSPB20192772C32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C32">32</a>]. By contrast, mobbing calls, which prompt others to approach the caller and harass the threat, tend to be lower pitched with a broader bandwidth––features that increase detection and localizability, and thus facilitate community engagement in driving off perched or ground predators [<a href="#RSPB20192772C27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C27">27</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C28">28</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C33">33</a>]. Repetition of a signal can also increase the likelihood of the message being detected by receivers [<a href="#RSPB20192772C34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C34">34</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C35">35</a>]. Black-capped chickadees, <em>Poecile atricapilla</em>, include more D notes in their mobbing alarm calls when presented with more deadly predators, a strategy that should enhance recruitment of neighbours in the most urgent situations [<a href="#RSPB20192772C36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C36">36</a>]. Redundancy is a common feature in the design of important signals [<a href="#RSPB20192772C37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C37">37</a>], and this strategy of increasing the number of alarm calls or elements to signal greater danger is found in several birds and mammals [<a href="#RSPB20192772C38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C38">38</a>–<a href="#RSPB20192772C40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C40">40</a>]. The structure of alarm signals can thus facilitate their function, ensuring information reaches receivers while reducing unwanted eavesdropping by predators, but there has been no study of how their design facilitates rapid communication in situations of immediate danger.</p>
<p>Alarm calls must be fast as well as reliable, but this leads to a fundamental dilemma in signal design. There are advantages to communicating greater danger by increasing the number of elements: in addition to enhancing signal detection, it makes for a more reliable and error-resistant signalling system than using fewer alarm elements [<a href="#RSPB20192772C25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C25">25</a>]. This is because calls composed of more elements must necessarily begin with fewer elements, so a system in which fewer elements signalled greater danger would result in unnecessary flights to cover [<a href="#RSPB20192772C25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C25">25</a>]. However, when calling in response to fast-moving predators that pose immediate danger, call design must facilitate a speed of information transfer that at least matches that of the predator. Consequently, using more elements to signal more immediate peril is paradoxical. It takes longer to hear more elements, which means receivers will take longer to respond because they need to hear the number of repetitions to determine the degree of danger. The delay will be even greater for more dangerous threats because they prompt longer, multi-element calls [<a href="#RSPB20192772C25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C25">25</a>]. This puzzle of how multi-element calls can facilitate sufficiently rapid responses in receivers facing urgent danger has yet to be solved.</p>
<p>We tested experimentally how New Holland honeyeaters, <em>Phylidonyris novaehollandiae</em>, resolve the dilemma of communicating rapidly but also reliably about immediate danger. Honeyeaters produce loud, multi-element aerial alarm calls in response to flying threats [<a href="#RSPB20192772C41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C41">41</a>] (<a href="#RSPB20192772F1" class="usa-link">figure 1</a>), and these calls prompt a rapid flight to cover in conspecific receivers [<a href="#RSPB20192772C41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C41">41</a>], allowing for a simple quantification of response. Their alarm calls usually have multiple notes and can take up to 5 s to produce and therefore provide an excellent model for examining the paradox of using long calls to signal about the threats that require fast responses.
</p>

<section id="s2a"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">(a). Study site and species</h3>
<p>Data were collected on a colour-banded population of New Holland honeyeaters between June 2014 and July 2017 in the Australian National Botanic Gardens in Canberra, Australia (−35.28° S, 149.11° E). These honeyeaters are small (20 g), pair-breeding passerines that feed on nectar from flowering plants, supplemented with arthropods [<a href="#RSPB20192772C42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C42">42</a>]. The Botanic Gardens comprise 40 ha of both natural and planted vegetation. The birds in the study population were resident in the gardens and accustomed to the presence of people. Further details about the study population are provided in the electronic supplementary material.</p>
<p>New Holland honeyeaters give distinct aerial alarm calls in response to flying threats. Calls are composed of a mean of 7.5 elements of similar structure (interquartile range: 1.5–10; <em>n</em> = 847 calls to 222 prompts). Each element consists of a pure, descending whistle of about 33 ms in length. The elements are loud, with an amplitude of about 70 dB at 6 m and relatively low in pitch with a peak frequency of 3.5 kHz [<a href="#RSPB20192772C41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C41">41</a>].</p></section><section id="s2b"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">(b) Production. of alarm calls</h3>
<section id="s2b1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">(i). Natural observations</h4>
<p>In order to determine whether honeyeaters encode urgency in the number of elements and acoustic structure of their aerial alarm calls, we opportunistically recorded 13 151 alarm calls given during natural encounters with predators and other threats over the course of 1627 h of observation between 2014 and 2017. Recordings were made using Marantz PMD670 and PMD661 MK II solid-state digital recorders with sampling rates of 44.1 kHz and 16 bits, and a Sennheiser ME66 shotgun microphone. The recording equipment was attached to the observer's shoulder and recorded continuously while the birds were followed at a distance of 10–20 m. Where possible, the distance between the microphone and the calling bird, the identity of the calling individual and the probable prompt were recorded for all alarm calls.</p>
<p> The three most commonly observed prompts were red wattlebirds (<em>Anthochaera carunculata</em>), pied currawongs (<em>Strepera graculina</em>) and collared sparrowhawks (<em>Accipiter cirrhocephalus</em>). Wattlebirds are abundant in the Gardens, and about 15 pairs of currawongs [<a href="#RSPB20192772C43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C43">43</a>] and two pairs of <em>Accipiter</em> hawks breed in the Gardens each year (J.R. McLachlan 2014–2017, personal observation). These species represent an increasing intensity of threats, so they were used to look at how alarm call design varies with danger: wattlebirds are aggressive but non-predatory nectivores that can displace honeyeaters during competitive interactions over food, currawongs are primarily nest predators but will opportunistically target small adult birds, and sparrowhawks pose the greatest threat, as they principally feed on small birds around the size of New Holland honeyeaters [<a href="#RSPB20192772C42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C42">42</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C44">44</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C45">45</a>].</p>
<p>As birds need to show rapid responses to attacking hawks, we hypothesized that the first element of honeyeater alarm calls would convey urgency information. We generated spectrograms of the recordings using R<span class="font-variant-small-caps">aven</span> P<span class="font-variant-small-caps">ro</span> 1.4, a Hann window function with a 256 sample size, a temporal grid resolution of 2.9 ms with an overlap of 50% and a frequency grid resolution of 172 Hz. The spectrograms were used to count the number of elements per call and measure their acoustic structure. We used semi-automated measures that are robust to variation in the manual placement of the selection boxes [<a href="#RSPB20192772C46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C46">46</a>]. We recorded the duration (the temporal interval containing 90% of the energy; s), minimum frequency (the frequency above which 95% of the energy occurred; Hz), maximum frequency (the frequency below which 95% of the energy occurred; Hz), peak frequency (the frequency at maximum amplitude; Hz) and frequency range (the frequency interval containing 90% of the energy; Hz) of the first element of alarm calls given during natural encounters with wattlebirds, currawongs and sparrowhawks.</p></section><section id="s2b2"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">(ii). Model presentation methods</h4>
<p>We presented 16 male honeyeaters with life-sized, gliding models of a pied currawong and collared sparrowhawk in July 2017 [<a href="#RSPB20192772C47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C47">47</a>]. Each bird was presented with both the currawong and sparrowhawk models, but only received one model per day. A model was presented when the honeyeater was perched with a clear view, and its calls were recorded with a Marantz PMD661 MK II solid-state digital recorder with a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz at 16 bits and a Sennheiser ME66 shotgun microphone. Models were launched by a thrower standing 10–15 m from the focal bird, glided for 10–20 m and aimed to pass the bird at a distance of around 10 m. However, model trajectories varied unpredictably, which added ‘natural' variation to model prompts. Half the birds received the sparrowhawk model first and half received the currawong first. We counted the number of elements per call and measured their acoustic structure in R<span class="font-variant-small-caps">aven</span> P<span class="font-variant-small-caps">ro</span> 1.4 (see above for details).</p></section></section><section id="s2c"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">(c). Response to alarm calls</h3>
<p>We conducted three playback experiments between October and December 2015 to test what features affected the honeyeaters' responses to alarm calls. (i) We first used natural alarm calls that retained urgency-associated natural variation in element structure and element number. We presented each bird with five treatments: 1-, 3-, 8- and 13-element natural honeyeater alarm calls to represent a range of urgency, and the contact calls of crimson rosellas, <em>Platycercus elegans</em>, as the control. (ii) We then tested if the first element alone conveyed information on danger. Birds were presented with only the first element of 1-, 3-, 8- and 13-element alarm calls, as well as rosella contact calls as the control. (iii) Finally, we tested if the number of elements alone signalled urgency, while holding element structure constant. To do this, we extracted the fourth elements from 8-element alarm calls and used these elements to create 1-, 3-, 8- and 13-element calls comprised of identical elements with a standardized inter-element interval of 80 ms. Rosella contact calls were again used as the control.</p>
<p>All playbacks were prepared in R<span class="font-variant-small-caps">aven</span> P<span class="font-variant-small-caps">ro</span> 1.4. Each playback set consisted of four honeyeater alarm calls and the contact calls of rosellas as a neutral control. All alarm calls within a set came from the same individual and were recorded during natural encounters with threats. Sounds below the frequency of the calls were filtered out, below 2 kHz for the alarm calls and 1.5 kHz for the control calls. Playbacks were broadcast as wave files from a Roland R-09HR via a custom-made amplifier and Peerless tweeter speaker. The speaker was attached to the experimenter's waist, giving a height of about 1 m. Calls were calibrated to be broadcast at 70 dB at 6 m, which is the mean amplitude of honeyeater alarm calls (mean ± s.d.: 70.3 ± 4.1 dB) [<a href="#RSPB20192772C48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C48">48</a>]. Responses to all playbacks were recorded using a Panasonic HC-V770 M camcorder filming at 50 frames s<sup>−1</sup> at 1920 × 1080p, which was supported by a Wizmount CU2pack over the experimenter's shoulder.</p>
<p>Within each experiment, 12 unique playback sets were presented to 24 honeyeaters, 12 male and 12 female. Each set was used twice, once for a male and once for a female, and each bird received all playbacks within a set. We chose the range of 1–13 element calls to represent varying urgency. Playback order was pseudorandomized, such that each treatment occurred in each position in the playback sequence at least once across the 24 treatment orders. Birds were followed for at least 5 min before each playback, to ensure they were behaving normally, and they were presented with the sounds when foraging on flowers. Playbacks were carried out from a distance of 7–10 m, a minimum of 5 min apart (mean ± s.e. = 42 min ± 4), and during which no alarm calls were produced or predators were nearby.</p>
<p>The video recordings of the responses were analysed frame-by-frame using Adobe P<span class="font-variant-small-caps">remier</span> P<span class="font-variant-small-caps">ro</span> and Q<span class="font-variant-small-caps">uick</span>T<span class="font-variant-small-caps">ime</span>. After recording the frame number of the playback onset, the soundtrack was removed to enable blind scoring. The response of the bird was scored as either an immediate flee to nearby cover (1), which is the most urgent response, or no immediate flee to cover (0), which included birds that continued feeding or visually scanned the environment, even if they later flew. We scored only immediate flees to ensure birds had not acquired additional personal information prior to flying to cover [<a href="#RSPB20192772C49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C49">49</a>]. Birds were deemed to have fled when their feet left the perch. We recorded the latency to respond as the time from the onset of the playback to the time when the bird initiated any response (fleeing or scanning), which generally started with a rapid head movement or raising of the wings. The duration of the response was measured as the time from the onset of the response to the time when the birds resumed foraging or departed the feeding area.</p></section><section id="s2d"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">(d). Statistical analysis</h3>
<p>All statistical analyses were carried out in R v. 3.6.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Generalized linear mixed effects models (GLMMs) were constructed with binomial error distributions and logit link functions, using the glmer() function of the lme4 package [<a href="#RSPB20192772C50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C50">50</a>]. We constructed the linear mixed effects models (LMMs) with normal error distributions and identity link functions, using the lmer() function of the lme4 package [<a href="#RSPB20192772C50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C50">50</a>]. We carried out pairwise comparisons using the glht() function of the multcomp package [<a href="#RSPB20192772C51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C51">51</a>]. Effect sizes were calculated using the r.squaredGLMM function of the MuMIn package [<a href="#RSPB20192772C52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C52">52</a>]. In all cases, the full model with all terms of interest was fitted before likelihood ratio tests were used to identify significant fixed effects by removing them individually from the model and assessing the change in deviance. Some response variables were log-transformed to improve the fit, as indicated in the results tables in the electronic supplementary material. All statistical tests were two-tailed.</p>
<section id="s2d1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">(i). Production of alarm calls</h4>
<p>We counted the number of elements given per alarm call during natural encounters with wattlebirds, currawongs and sparrowhawks and during presentations of a model currawong and sparrowhawk. We only included natural encounters for which the recording distance and the identity of the calling honeyeater were known, resulting in a dataset of 102 calls given to 57 prompts by 26 birds.</p>
<p>We used LMMs to look at how the type of threat affected the number of elements in an alarm call during natural encounters and model presentations, with the identity of the calling bird entered as a random effect. For natural observations, where individual threats sometimes prompted multiple calls, threat identity was also included as a random effect. For model presentations, the order in which the model predators were presented was also included as a fixed effect.</p>
<p>We used LMMs to examine the effect of threat type on the acoustic structure of the first element of alarm calls. The acoustic measures were entered as response variables in separate LMMs. To correct for multiple testing, we calculated the ‘false discovery rate' (FDR)-adjusted <em>p</em>-values using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [<a href="#RSPB20192772C53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C53">53</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C54">54</a>]. Both the unadjusted and the FDR-adjusted <em>p</em>-values are presented. The type of threat and the number of elements within the call were entered as fixed effects and the identity of the calling bird was entered as a random effect. For the natural observations, the threat identity was also included as a random effect and the recording distance, which ranged from 3–20 m, was included as a fixed effect. For the model presentations, the order in which the model predators were presented was also included as a fixed effect.</p></section><section id="s2d2"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">(ii). Response to alarm calls</h4>
<p>As birds never fled to the controls in any playback experiment, these were excluded from the analyses to avoid issues associated with complete separation of data. The probability of fleeing to cover was entered as the response variable in a GLMM, which ran for 100 000 iterations to ensure adequate convergence. The duration of response was log-transformed to improve fit before being entered as the response variable into a LMM. The playback type, sex of the bird, and playback order were entered as fixed effects in all models, and the identity of the individual bird and specific playback set were entered as random effects.</p></section></section></section><section id="s3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results</h2>

Number of elements</h4>
<p>Even when the acoustic structure of elements was controlled for using repeated elements, playbacks composed of more elements prompted stronger responses. Birds were more likely to flee to calls with multiple elements (GLMM: <em>χ</em><sup>2</sup><sub>3</sub> = 10.05, <em>p</em> = 0.02; <a href="#RSPB20192772F5" class="usa-link">figure 5</a><em>c</em>; electronic supplementary material, table S6a). They also took longer to resume feeding after calls with more elements (LMM: <em>χ</em><sup>2</sup><sub>3</sub> = 24.52, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.001; <a href="#RSPB20192772F5" class="usa-link">figure 5</a><em>d</em>; electronic supplementary material, table S6b).</p></section></section></section><section id="s4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Discussion</h2>
<p>Our study reveals a solution to the paradox of how to signal both rapidly and reliably about immediate danger. To do this, the honeyeaters use a signalling system in which information is transferred by two complementary mechanisms in the form of element number and structure. They produced more elements per call in response to more dangerous threats, a common strategy among small birds in both aerial and mobbing contexts [<a href="#RSPB20192772C38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C38">38</a>]. Furthermore, the first element of honeyeater alarm calls prompted by sparrowhawks was lower pitched than elements from calls prompted by less dangerous threats. As lower frequencies transmit better over longer distance, this could increase the likelihood of detection by both distant conspecifics and oncoming predators [<a href="#RSPB20192772C31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C31">31</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C32">32</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C55">55</a>]. Given that a range of other species have changes in the acoustic structure of calls according to alarm urgency [<a href="#RSPB20192772C25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C25">25</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C38">38</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C56">56</a>–<a href="#RSPB20192772C62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C62">62</a>], the strategy of providing critical information about urgency at the very start of alarm calls may well be a widespread solution to the problem of alarm signal design.</p>
<p>Honeyeaters respond extremely rapidly to conspecific alarm calls, reacting even more quickly to alarm calls than if they had seen the threat themselves [<a href="#RSPB20192772C49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C49">49</a>]. This demonstrates the value of alarm calls at providing an effective warning to conspecifics, particularly for inattentive individuals that may otherwise fail to detect an approaching threat in time [<a href="#RSPB20192772C26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C26">26</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C63" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C63">63</a>]. While work on the reaction time of birds to acoustic playbacks is rare, the initial reaction time of the honeyeaters (100 ms) is comparable to the startle time of captive starlings, <em>Sturnus vulgaris</em>, in response to a loud sound (80 ms) [<a href="#RSPB20192772C64" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C64">64</a>]. Furthermore, the honeyeaters' latency to take-off of 260 ms is similar to that reported in pigeons, <em>Columba livia</em>, responding to the flight of an alarmed companion (250 ms) and blackbirds, <em>Turdus merula</em>, reacting to an alarming visual stimulus (300 ms) [<a href="#RSPB20192772C65" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C65">65</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C66" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C66">66</a>]. The rapidity of the honeyeaters' reactions and their fleeing responses to the first element playbacks show that they can assess the degree of danger based on only the start of the alarm call. However, their rapid response is fine-tuned to signals about danger, as they responded about five times slower to an equally loud sound that was not an alarm call. Our findings complement work on escape behaviour of captive birds in response to flying model predators [<a href="#RSPB20192772C8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C8">8</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C9">9</a>], which together indicate that birds can make detailed, split-second decisions in response to both visual and acoustic cues of immediate danger.</p>
<p>Given that honeyeaters can determine the degree of urgency after hearing only the first element, why do they produce such long, multi-element alarm calls? Repetition of a signal can provide redundancy to ensure the message gets across, reinforcing the information conveyed by element structure [<a href="#RSPB20192772C34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C34">34</a>]. Using multiple elements may also increase the probability that the signal is received [<a href="#RSPB20192772C35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C35">35</a>]. For example, king penguins, <em>Aptenodytes patagonicus</em>, produce more syllables per call and more calls when communicating with their mates in windy conditions [<a href="#RSPB20192772C67" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C67">67</a>]. Increased signal detection could explain why the honeyeaters were twice as likely to flee to the multi-element calls relative to single element calls, regardless of acoustic structure. In addition to facilitating signal detection, multiple elements also provided graded information on danger. Honeyeaters spent more time in cover when presented with longer calls, for both natural alarms and artificial calls made up of the same element repeated. Similarly, superb fairy-wrens, <em>Malurus cyaneus</em>, spent longer in cover in response to playbacks of alarm calls artificially composed of more elements [<a href="#RSPB20192772C56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C56">56</a>]. For a signal that provides critical information in circumstances requiring split-second decisions and reliable information, it makes sense to have evolved a call that simultaneously provides complementary information and a measure of redundancy [<a href="#RSPB20192772C34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C34">34</a>].</p>
<p>Signal detection theory suggests that a receiver's decision to respond to a signal can be shaped in part by the relative costs of false alarms and missed detections [<a href="#RSPB20192772C35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C35">35</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C68" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C68">68</a>]. For signals where a missed detection carries a high cost, receivers may show ‘adaptive gullibility', lowering the threshold for responding and consequently increasing responsiveness to false alarms [<a href="#RSPB20192772C68" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C68">68</a>]. As a failure to respond to an alarm call could carry the considerable cost of death, individuals should seek to minimize missed detections and are therefore likely to make energetically expensive flights to cover and lose foraging time in response to false alarms [<a href="#RSPB20192772C69" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C69">69</a>]. Multi-element signals may serve to mitigate the costs of this trade-off by confirming the message. Indeed, although honeyeaters remained in cover for longer after hearing only the first element of a 13-element call than of a 1-element call, they resumed feeding about 20 s sooner if this signal was not backed up by additional elements, minimizing the temporal cost of fleeing if the caller, or receiver, made an error.</p>
<p>Successful escape from predators is essential for survival and, to ensure success, the signalling systems that facilitate an escape response need to be rapid, robust and flexible. These key features of anti-predator signal design are likely to be important at all levels of communication, from the neural circuitry within a single individual that controls its escape behaviour to the calls that communicate danger between individuals [<a href="#RSPB20192772C70" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C70">70</a>–<a href="#RSPB20192772C73" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C73">73</a>]. In our study, we demonstrate that the need for speed, reliability and flexibility in anti-predator signalling systems can shape the structure of alarm calls. Graded alarm signals, such as those used by the honeyeaters and other species, enable receivers to flexibly assess the degree of danger and whether to engage in a costly flight to cover [<a href="#RSPB20192772C38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C38">38</a>–<a href="#RSPB20192772C40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C40">40</a>], while the repetition of alarm elements also provides a measure of redundancy and increases the likelihood of a response [<a href="#RSPB20192772C25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C25">25</a>,<a href="#RSPB20192772C56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="RSPB20192772C56">56</a>]. Furthermore, we show that honeyeaters can front-load the critical information at the very start of their calls, providing, to our knowledge, the first clear evidence of how alarm calls can facilitate an extremely rapid escape response. This neat solution to the issue of delivering rapid, reliable and flexible information in the face of danger demonstrates the importance of those features in anti-predator signal design and raises questions about how widespread this phenomenon is in alarm signalling at large.</p></section><section id="sec18"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
