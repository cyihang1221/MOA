<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h3>
<p>Most of the studies on alcohol dependence (AD) emphasize its impact on the nervous system and organ functions of the human body, but its molecular mechanism is largely unknown so far. This study determines serum protein changes in alcohol-dependent patients for the identification of biomarkers and the revelation of molecular mechanisms behind alcohol dependence.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>Serum samples from seven newly diagnosed alcohol-dependent patients and four healthy controls are subjected to researcher-conducted proteomic analyses using data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry. Functional enrichment analysis of gene activity and biological pathways is performed by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) to reveal key processes related to alcohol dependence. The expression of Small Nuclear Ribonucleoprotein Polypeptide B (SNRPB) is evaluated to assess its potential as a biomarker, and survival analysis is performed to investigate its clinical relevance.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>The mass spectrometry detected a total of 1,249 and 1,020 proteins in the serum from alcohol-dependent patients and healthy controls, respectively. Biomarker analysis identified 195 proteins as potential markers with a differentially expressed pattern. Among them, 99 proteins are upregulated and 96 proteins downregulated in alcohol-dependent patients compared with controls. The GO and KEGG pathway analysis revealed that alcohol-dependent patients exhibit enhanced activity in pathways related to adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-dependent chromatin remodeling and immune responses but reduced activity in metabolic pathways. Alcohol-dependent patients exhibited significantly higher levels of the SNRPB protein, suggesting its potential role in immune system regulation and cell growth control. Survival analysis results indicated that higher levels of SNRPB are linked to worse outcomes in liver cancer patients, specifically those with liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), suggesting it could be a useful biomarker for alcohol-related diseases.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p>This study provides valuable insights into the systemic protein changes in alcohol dependence, identifying several potential biomarkers for early diagnosis and therapeutic targeting. The upregulation of SNRPB suggests its role as a potential biomarker in clinical applications.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Alcohol dependence, Proteomics, Serum biomarkers, SNRPB, Mass spectrometry, DIA</p></section></section><section id="sec5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Alcohol dependence (AD) is a significant factor in chronic diseases and public health issues globally, affecting the physical and mental health, as well as the social well-being of millions of individuals (<a href="#ref-10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-10">Cargiulo, 2007</a>; <a href="#ref-32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-32">Nutt et al., 2015</a>). Clinically, alcohol dependence is characterized by recurrent drinking, excessive alcohol consumption, tolerance, and dependence on alcohol, often accompanied by severe psychological and physiological disorders (<a href="#ref-10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-10">Cargiulo, 2007</a>). While extensive research has been conducted on alcohol dependence, revealing its impact on the nervous system and various organs, the molecular mechanisms underlying alcohol dependence remain inadequately understood (<a href="#ref-23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-23">Kalsi et al., 2009</a>). Most studies have focused on the central nervous system’s response to alcohol, with relatively little research addressing the systemic molecular changes induced by alcohol dependence, particularly the alterations in serum proteins (<a href="#ref-15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-15">Gorini, Roberts &amp; Mayfield, 2013</a>). Some studies have identified potential biomarkers associated with alcohol dependence, but most are limited to specific organ systems or animal models, but a comprehensive investigation of molecular changes in peripheral systems, such as human serum, is lacking (<a href="#ref-29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-29">Mayfield &amp; Harris, 2009</a>; <a href="#ref-13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-13">Gika &amp; Wilson, 2014</a>). Therefore, the study on systemic protein changes, particularly in serum, in alcohol-dependent patients could provide novel perspectives for early diagnosis and personalized treatment of alcohol dependence.</p>
<p>In recent years, with the rapid advancement of proteomics technologies, data-independent acquisition (DIA), mass spectrometry and other high-throughput techniques have been widely applied for protein identification and quantification in biological samples (<a href="#ref-20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-20">Hu, Noble &amp; Wolf-Yadlin, 2016</a>). DIA mass spectrometry, by simultaneously acquiring mass spectometry (MS) and MS/MS data, offers comprehensive and unbiased proteomic analysis and has become a powerful tool for studying protein expression patterns in complex biological systems (<a href="#ref-35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-35">Sajic, Liu &amp; Aebersold, 2015</a>; <a href="#ref-24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-24">Kitata, Yang &amp; Chen, 2023</a>). Existing proteomics studies have successfully revealed changes in protein expression in various diseases, particularly in cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and metabolic disorders, demonstrating the potential of proteomics in disease diagnosis and prognosis evaluation. However, proteomics studies related to alcohol dependence remain relatively limited, with most research focused on protein changes in specific organs or animal models, and there is a lack of in-depth analysis of systemic protein alterations (<a href="#ref-28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-28">Matsumoto et al., 2007</a>).</p>
<p>At the same time, a few studies have employed mass spectrometry to explore the impact of alcohol on the proteomes of different human tissues, such as the liver and brain (<a href="#ref-15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-15">Gorini, Roberts &amp; Mayfield, 2013</a>). These studies suggest that alcohol dependence may affect metabolic processes and immune responses through various pathways, altering the expression of multiple biomarkers (<a href="#ref-12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-12">Enculescu et al., 2019</a>). However, systemic protein changes in alcohol dependence, particularly in serum, have not been adequately addressed (<a href="#ref-14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-14">Gorini, Harris &amp; Mayfield, 2014</a>). Serum, as an essential biological fluid in the human body, contains abundant biomolecules and reflects the overall physiological and pathological state of the body. The study of serum proteome in patients with alcohol dependence, particularly focusing on differentially expressed proteins, could help us understand the molecular mechanisms behind alcohol dependence and identify new diagnostic markers (<a href="#ref-25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-25">Lai et al., 2009</a>). Large-scale protein screening using DIA mass spectrometry determines significant differences between patients and healthy controls leading to identificatiuon of proteins for their potential role in pathogenesis (<a href="#ref-15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-15">Gorini, Roberts &amp; Mayfield, 2013</a>). The extensive serum proteomics study demonstrated how alcohol dependency affects protein expression levels and pinpointed vital signalling pathways and biological processes that are connected to them (<a href="#ref-5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-5">Beech et al., 2012</a>; <a href="#ref-14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-14">Gorini, Harris &amp; Mayfield, 2014</a>).</p>
<p>The multifaceted nature of alcohol dependence demands systematic methods for investigating its molecular basis (<a href="#ref-11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-11">Casanova Ferrer et al., 2020</a>). This study presents DIA mass spectrometry as a new method by analysing the complete set of proteins in the blood of both alcohol-dependent patients and healthy individuals. We will use various data analysis techniques, such as Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, kyoto encyclopaedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, and immune infiltration analysis, to study the biological roles of proteins that are expressed differently and find out how they might function in alcohol dependence. The research seeks to discover potential protein biomarkers that will enable better clinical treatments for alcohol dependence and support individual treatment approaches.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study participants and serum collection and processing</h3>
<p>This study screens participants based on the World Health Organization (ICD-10) criteria and the Michigan Alcoholism Screening Test (MAST) (<a href="#ref-36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-36">Selzer, 1971</a>). Group A (alcohol-dependent patients) inclusion criteria: aged 18–65 years; formally diagnosed with alcohol dependence; exhibiting strong alcohol cravings; experiencing withdrawal symptoms after cessation of drinking; demonstrating loss of control over drinking behavior; willing to cooperate with rehabilitation therapy and providing signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria: current participation in other clinical trials; poor compliance or incomplete data; failure to meet rehabilitation treatment standards; Comorbidities such as liver diseases, tumors, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome. After initial screening, liver function indicators (alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), <em>etc.</em>) and the mean corpuscular volume (MCV) are evaluated. Individuals with abnormal results are excluded. Subsequently, carbohydrate-deficient transferrin (CDT) and phosphatidylethanol (PEth) levels were measured. A positive result in these tests is regarded as alcohol dependence. From January to June 2023, 20 cases were collected from the Third Hospital of Quanzhou. Following screening, seven eligible samples were included in Group A.</p>
<p>Group B (healthy volunteers) criteria: aged 18–65 years; no alcohol consumption or drinking habits within the past 30 days; signed informed consent provided. Exclusion criteria were the same as those for Group A. Blood samples were obtained from individuals with normal liver function and within the normal range for mean corpuscular volume (MCV), along with negative PEth and CDT test results, to meet the inclusion requirements. Four healthy adults were included.</p>
<p>Peripheral blood samples were collected from all participants and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses.</p>
<p>The study was conducted in strict accordance with relevant ethical guidelines. Ethical approval for this biomedical research was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Ningde Normal University (Review No.: NDNU-LL-202101, Approval Date: March 4, 2021). Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants.</p></section><section id="sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Mass spectrometry experimental methodology</h3>
<section id="sec9"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Low-abundance protein enrichment and peptide digestion</h4>
<p>For each sample, 100 µL of serum was subjected to low-abundance protein enrichment using magnetic bead-based technology. Specifically, 100 µL of serum was mixed with four µL of pre-washed magnetic beads (washed twice with wash buffer) and incubated for 2 h. Following incubation, the mixture was placed on a magnetic rack to separate the serum from the beads. The serum was then discarded, and the beads were washed five times using a wash buffer, with each wash consisting of a 5-minute inversion and mixing step. All samples were subsequently digested using trypsin in solution (<a href="#ref-9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-9">Blume et al., 2020</a>). After digestion, the peptides from all samples were desalted using a C18 cartridge. The desalted peptides were lyophilized and reconstituted in 20 µL of 0.1% formic acid solution. The peptide concentration in each sample was determined by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm (OD280). Equal amounts of peptides from each sample were pooled to create a composite peptide sample, which was then subjected to reverse-phase (RP) fractionation. The RP-fractionated samples, along with the digested peptides from each individual sample, were spiked with a suitable amount of indexed retention time (iRT) standard peptides before being analyzed by DIA mass spectrometry.</p></section><section id="sec10"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Mass spectrometry method</h4>
<p>The DIA analysis was performed using the Evosep One system, operating at a nanoflow rate for chromatographic separation. After separation through nanoflow high-performance liquid chromatography (nano-HPLC), the samples were analyzed using a timsTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker) under DIA mode. The analysis was conducted in positive ion mode, with both MS and MS/MS spectra recorded within the mass-to-charge (m/z) range of 100 to 1,700. For MS2 analysis, the DIA data acquisition mode was employed, utilizing four TIMS scan windows. Each window had an accumulation time of 100 ms. In Parallel Accumulation Serial Fragmentation (PASEF) mode, the collision energy varied linearly with ion mobility (1/K0), with collision energies ranging from 20 to 59 eV corresponding to ion mobilities of 1/K0 between 0.85 and 1.30 Vs/cm<sup>2</sup>.</p></section><section id="sec11"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Data analysis</h4>
<p>The DIA data were processed using Spectronaut software (version 16). The database used for analysis was the same as the one employed for the library construction. The following parameters were set in the software: the retention time prediction type was configured as dynamic iRT, MS2-level interference correction was enabled, and cross-run normalization was activated. All results were filtered based on a Q value cutoff of 0.01, corresponding to a false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 1%.</p></section></section><section id="sec12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Bioinformatics analysis</h3>
<section id="sec13"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Protein clustering analysis</h4>
<p>The quantitative data of the target protein set were first normalized to a range of (−1, 1). Subsequently, hierarchical clustering was performed on both the sample and protein expression data using the ComplexHeatmap R package (R version 3.4). The distance metric employed was Euclidean, and the linkage method used was average linkage. A heatmap was generated to visualize the clustering results. For further analysis of the target protein set, the data were normalized, and fuzzy c-means (FCM) clustering was performed using the Mfuzz software. This algorithm allowed for the identification of distinct expression modules based on the trends observed in protein expression, thereby generating different clusters.</p></section><section id="sec14"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Subcellular localization analysis</h4>
<p>We used the CELLO method (<a href="http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw</a>) to predict the subcellular localization of proteins. The method uses a type of machine learning called a multi-class support vector machine (SVM), which learns from known protein sequences and their subcellular localization information found in public databases. We used the trained model to determine each protein’s subcellular localization.</p></section><section id="sec15"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Protein domain analysis</h4>
<p>We conducted protein domain analysis using the Pfam database, which contains multiple sequence alignments and hidden Markov models for protein families. This database includes information on protein domains. The InterProScan software package performed functional characterization of the protein sequences during the analysis. The integrated scanning algorithm from InterPro used the Pfam database to identify the functions of protein domains.</p></section><section id="sec16"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">GO functional annotation</h4>
<p>Blast2GO allowed for the functional annotation of the target protein set <em>via</em> its specialized protocol. This process involved four main steps: The analysis included matching sequences using BLAST, then getting GO terms by mapping, followed by a step for GO annotation, and finally adding extra information with InterProScan to enhance the annotations. These steps resulted in the comprehensive functional characterization of proteins through the examination of their corresponding GO terms.</p></section><section id="sec17"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">KEGG pathway annotation</h4>
<p>The target protein set was labeled with KEGG pathway information using the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS). We used this software to link protein sequences to known biological pathways and to categorize their functions using information from the KEGG database. Users uploaded protein sequences to KAAS, which matched them with KEGG pathways by aligning the sequences and assigning them to the right pathways. The chosen method allowed researchers to determine which biological processes and metabolic pathways connected with the target proteins, revealing insights into their cellular functions.</p></section><section id="sec18"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Enrichment analysis</h4>
<p>The study analysed how certain GO categories, KEGG pathways, and protein domains are spread out in the target protein set compared to the full protein dataset. The study used Fisher’s exact test for statistical analysis to determine which functional categories or pathways showed significant enrichment. This study measured how often each GO term, along with KEGG pathways and protein domains, appeared in the target protein set against the reference protein set. The enrichment analysis revealed biological functions and pathways along with domains that showed overrepresentation in target proteins, which helped us understand their biological roles.</p></section><section id="sec19"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Protein-protein interaction network analysis</h4>
<p>Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) among the target proteins were identified using data from the IntAct (<a href="http://www.ebi.ac.uk/intact/main.xhtml" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.ebi.ac.uk/intact/main.xhtml</a>) and STRING (<a href="http://string-db.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://string-db.org/</a>) databases. Users can find data about direct and indirect protein interactions within these databases. We used Cytoscape software (version 3.2.1) to visualize and analyze interaction data, which enabled them to build an interaction network for the target proteins. We looked at the network to show how the proteins are related and to identify important areas or groups that represent key biological functions or categories.</p></section><section id="sec20"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Principal component analysis (PCA)</h4>
<p>The study utilized PCA for data dimensionality reduction while simultaneously discovering patterns. We utilized the R programming language to perform the data analysis. Pareto scaling standardized the dataset before PCA by dividing each variable by its standard deviation’s square root. The scaling method reduces the impact of wide-ranging data variations but maintains essential features that aid pattern recognition. The multidimensional statistical analysis performed after preprocessing helped reveal the principal components that account for most of the data variance, visualize sample relationships, and uncover data structure.</p></section></section><section id="sec21"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Differential database analysis</h3>
<p>To identify outliers and minimize their impact on the accuracy of the results, Z-score analysis was conducted. Values with a Z-score greater than 3.0 or less than −3.0 were considered outliers and subsequently excluded from further analysis. To compare the expression levels of the SNRPB protein between tumor and normal groups within the liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) dataset, the Wilcoxon test was employed. Additionally, differential expression analysis was performed between high and low SNRPB expression groups. The validation of SNRPB expression was conducted across multiple datasets, including E_TABM_36, <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE14520" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE14520</a>, <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE39791" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE39791</a>, and <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE144269" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE144269</a>.</p></section><section id="sec22"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Clinical data validation</h3>
<p>Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using the survival package in R. The optimal cut-off values for SNRPB expression and the high- and low-expression cohorts were determined using the survminer package, ensuring that the minimum proportion of the high- and low-expression groups was not below 0.3. We conducted a log-rank test using the survfit function to assess the statistical significance between high and low expression groups. Patients were stratified into four quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4) based on SNRPB gene expression levels, with Q1 representing the top 25% of expression and Q4 representing the lowest 25%. Chi-square tests were applied to assess significant differences between the composition of these groups. To evaluate the link between SNRPB expression and survival results, a simple Cox survival analysis was done using the inverse variance method for meta-analysis, with the log hazard ratio (HR) as the main measure. We categorized HR values as either less than 1 or greater than 1, signifying tumor-suppressive or tumor-promoting functions, respectively. We conducted statistical analysis and visualization using the Meta package in R (version 4.3.2). Additionally, we conducted a univariate Cox survival analysis using the survival package to evaluate the relationship between the gene of interest and traditional clinical variables. We described the relative risk using the HR and 95% confidence interval (CI), and the forestplotter package facilitated the visualization. Since continuous variables might affect risk in a non-straightforward way, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to analyze these possible non-linear connections. This method was used to examine the link between SNRPB expression and four survival outcomes: overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-free interval (DFI), in order to determine whether the relationship was non-linear.</p></section><section id="sec23"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Enrichment analysis</h3>
<p>The top 30% of samples based on gene expression levels formed the high-expression group, and the bottom 30% of samples became the low-expression group. The Limma package conducted differential analysis to determine the log2 fold change (log2FC) values for each gene. The genes were arranged in order according to the values of their log2 fold changes. The analysis team executed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) with the clusterProfiler package and analyzed both hallmark gene sets and KEGG metabolic gene sets. The analysis generated enrichment scores (ES) for each gene set while conducting significance testing and applying multiple hypothesis corrections. A bubble plot was used for visualization. Visualization analysis was performed on the five most enriched pathways in the high- and low-expression groups. Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the fgsea function from the fgsea package, based on the KEGG gene set. The ES for each gene set was calculated, and significance testing along with multiple hypothesis correction were performed. The CancerSEA database, which organizes 14 different functional states of cancer cells, was used to assess the activity of specific pathways based on the expression of characteristic genes. The Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) package in R, using the z-score parameter, was employed to calculate the activity of 14 functional state gene sets, yielding combined z-scores. The gene set scores were standardized using the scale function and subsequently calculated for Pearson correlations between individual genes and each gene set score. Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package with multiple gene sets, including Gene Ontology—Biological Process (GO-BP), Gene Ontology—Molecular Function (GO-MF), Gene Ontology—Cellular Component (GO-CC), Reactome, and Wikipathways gene sets. The ES for each gene set was calculated, and significance testing along with multiple hypothesis correction were performed. The top five pathways significantly enriched in both the high- and low-expression groups were selected for visualization.</p></section><section id="sec24"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immune infiltration analysis</h3>
<p>Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between SNRPB expression and Tumor Immunophenotype (TIP) scores, as well as the self-correlation of TIP scores. The linkET package was used for visualization of these correlations. Data from the TISIDB database (<a href="http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/download.php" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/download.php</a>) were utilized, which includes information on immunomodulators, chemokines, immune-stimulating genes, immune-suppressing genes, chemokines, and human leukocyte antigens. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon test to compare the immune-related molecules between high and low SNRPB expression groups. The average expression of each gene in both groups was visualized using a heatmap. Patients were divided into four groups (Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4) based on quartiles of SNRPB gene expression. The heatmap visualized the average immune response scores and genomic state scores for each group, with the rows standardized to ensure that each score was scaled to the same range. This allowed for a comparison of the immune reaction scores across the SNRPB expression quartiles. The publicly accessible TIMER2.0 database provided immune infiltration data for all The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) samples to maintain data quality and consistency. Several algorithms helped to evaluate immune cell content and its connection to gene expression patterns. The study presented correlation coefficients from various algorithms through a heatmap that displayed how different cell types related to gene expression. We created scatter plots to demonstrate the relationships between immune cell types and gene expression clearly.</p></section><section id="sec25"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Microbial analysis</h3>
<p>The Cancer Microbiome Atlas (TCMA) database supplied microbial abundance data through comprehensive abundance matrices for multiple sample types. We used Spearman correlation analysis to evaluate the connection between microbial abundance and gene expression levels in patients. Through this analysis, we were able to evaluate possible links between specific microbial presence or abundance levels and particular gene expression measurements. This analysis showed how microbial communities might influence gene expression patterns within the patient group to reveal the microbiome’s impact on health or disease mechanisms.</p></section></section><section id="sec26"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="sec27"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Comprehensive proteomic profiling of serum proteins in alcohol dependence patients and healthy controls</h3>
<p>DIA proteomics found 1,249 proteins present in alcohol dependence patients and 1,020 proteins in healthy controls. Among them, 996 proteins were shared between alcohol dependence patients and healthy controls (<a href="#fig-1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1A</a>). Overall, 277 proteins were identified as potential biomarkers differentiating alcohol dependence patients from healthy individuals. A bar chart analysis revealed 195 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between the two groups, of which 99 were upregulated and 96 downregulated in alcohol dependence patients compared with healthy controls (<a href="#fig-1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1B</a>). GO enrichment analysis showed that the DEPs were involved in various biological processes, including cellular metabolic processes, biological regulation, response to stimuli, and metabolic processes, with molecular functions primarily related to binding and catalytic activity. The cellular components involved were mainly the cell, cell part, organelle, and extracellular region (<a href="#fig-2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2A</a>). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified that upregulated pathways in alcohol dependence were associated with ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, alcohol intoxication, viral protein interactions with cytokines and cytokine receptors, and ribosome function, while downregulated pathways were involved in porphyrin metabolism, glycan degradation, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis, GlycosylPhosphatidylInositol (GPI)—anchor biosynthesis, and biotin metabolism (<a href="#fig-2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2B</a>). Notably, five protein-coding genes related to the alcohol intoxication pathway were significantly upregulated, including H4C16, H2BC12, MACROH2A1, H2AC13, and H2AC21, with H4C16 and H2BC12 among the top 10 most upregulated proteins. Hierarchical clustering of DEPs revealed distinct clustering of alcohol dependence and control samples, with clear separation based on protein expression profiles (<a href="#fig-3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>). Among the proteins identified, SNRPB was notably upregulated in alcohol dependence. The heatmap further confirmed consistent clustering of upregulated and downregulated proteins within each group. Overall, these results highlight significant differences in protein expression between alcohol dependence patients and healthy controls, providing valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms of alcohol dependence and potential biomarkers for further investigation.</p>

<p>The analysis of SNRPB expression in LIHC demonstrated significant upregulation of this protein in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues. In the TCGA LIHC dataset, a volcano plot analysis revealed that SNRPB was significantly overexpressed in the tumor group (<a href="#fig-4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4A</a>). This finding was further validated in several independent datasets, including <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE14520" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE14520</a>, <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE39791" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE39791</a>, and <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE144269" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE144269</a>, where SNRPB expression was consistently higher in tumor samples compared to normal samples (<a href="#fig-4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4B</a>). The diagnostic performance of SNRPB expression was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which showed high accuracy in distinguishing tumor samples from normal tissues (<a href="#fig-5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5A</a>). Additionally, calibration curves further confirmed the reliability of SNRPB expression as a potential biomarker for tumor classification (<a href="#fig-5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5B</a>). Survival analysis revealed that higher SNRPB expression was significantly associated with worse prognosis in LIHC patients. Kaplan–Meier survival curves indicated that patients with high SNRPB expression in the highest quartile (Q4) had significantly poorer overall survival (OS), disease-free interval (DFI), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-specific survival (DSS) compared to patients in the lowest quartile (Q1) (<a href="#fig-5" class="usa-link">Figs. 5C</a>–<a href="#fig-5" class="usa-link">5F</a>). These findings were consistent across multiple datasets, as a meta-analysis of univariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that SNRPB expression was a significant risk factor for poor survival outcomes in LIHC (<a href="#fig-5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5G</a>). Further analysis from multiple datasets, including E_TABM_36, <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE10141" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE10141</a>, <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE116174" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE116174</a>, <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE144269" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE144269</a>, <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE14520" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE14520</a>, <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE54236" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE54236</a>, <a href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE76427" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE76427</a>, ICGC_LIRI, and TCGA, corroborated the prognostic value of SNRPB expression in LIHC. The forest plot from univariate Cox regression analysis confirmed the association of high SNRPB expression with poorer survival outcomes (<a href="#fig-5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5H</a>). In multivariate Cox regression analysis, after adjusting for traditional clinical variables, SNRPB expression remained an independent prognostic factor for poor survival in LIHC patients (<a href="#fig-6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6A</a>). Additionally, non-linear analysis using restricted cubic splines (RCS) demonstrated a linear relationship between SNRPB expression levels and the risk of adverse survival outcomes across all four survival endpoints (OS, DFI, PFI, and DSS) (<a href="#fig-6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6B</a>). In summary, these results demonstrate that SNRPB is upregulated in LIHC and is strongly associated with poor prognosis. High expression levels of SNRPB serve as an independent prognostic factor, suggesting that SNRPB could be a valuable biomarker for survival risk stratification in LIHC patients.</p>

<p>The involvement of SNRPB in the regulation of cell cycle processes and associated pathways was examined through pathway enrichment analyses. Hallmark gene set and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses revealed that the high SNRPB expression group exhibited significant enrichment in cell cycle-related pathways (<a href="#fig-7" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a>). Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted the cell cycle pathway as a predominant feature in the high SNRPB expression group, while metabolic signaling pathways were more enriched in the low SNRPB expression group (<a href="#fig-8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a>). GO, Reactome, and Wiki pathways enrichment analysis further corroborated these findings, indicating that the high SNRPB expression group was significantly associated with cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, and other essential cellular functions (<a href="#fig-9" class="usa-link">Fig. 9</a>). In terms of functional correlations, a Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between SNRPB expression levels and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) scores derived from 14 tumor types. The results demonstrated a significant positive correlation between SNRPB expression and scores for cell cycle, DNA damage, and DNA repair processes, suggesting that SNRPB may play a critical role as a tumor risk factor (<a href="#fig-10" class="usa-link">Fig. 10A</a>). Furthermore, differential expression analysis of key signaling pathways revealed that the high SNRPB expression group had higher scores for Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) and Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) signaling, which are crucial for cell proliferation and survival. In contrast, the low SNRPB expression group showed higher scores for Estrogen, Androgen, Janus Kinase Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (JAK-STAT), and protein 53 (p53) signaling pathways (<a href="#fig-10" class="usa-link">Fig. 10B</a>). These findings highlight the involvement of SNRPB in regulating critical cellular pathways, particularly those associated with cell cycle progression and DNA repair, and suggest its potential role as a biomarker for tumor risk in various malignancies.</p>

<p>To explore the potential immune activation role of SNRPB, we performed immune infiltration analysis. The correlation analysis of immune processes revealed a negative correlation between SNRPB expression and tumor cell regulation by T cells (<a href="#fig-11" class="usa-link">Fig. 11A</a>). Specifically, in the high SNRPB expression group, the expression levels of immune genes were generally upregulated (<a href="#fig-11" class="usa-link">Figs. 11B</a>–<a href="#fig-11" class="usa-link">11C</a>). Further analysis using immune-related scores such as immune antigenicity, DNA damage, and others showed that high SNRPB expression was positively associated with Cancer-Testis Antigen (CTA) score, cell differentiation, and aneuploidy scores (<a href="#fig-11" class="usa-link">Fig. 11D</a>). The immune cell infiltration analysis showed a positive correlation between SNRPB and Th1 cells, Tgd cells, Treg cells, and macrophages, while a negative correlation was observed with endothelial cells and neutrophils (<a href="#fig-12" class="usa-link">Fig. 12</a>). This pattern was reflected in the tumor microenvironment, where high SNRPB expression was associated with increased infiltration of T cells and B cells, while neutrophils and mast cells were less infiltrated (<a href="#fig-13" class="usa-link">Fig. 13A</a>). Further correlation analysis confirmed a positive relationship between SNRPB and Treg, Th2, and Th1 cells, while a negative correlation was observed with neutrophils and endothelial cells (<a href="#fig-13" class="usa-link">Fig. 13B</a>). Moreover, we observed that higher SNRPB expression correlated with increased intratumoral microbiome content, particularly in STAD and ESCA datasets (<a href="#fig-13" class="usa-link">Fig. 13C</a>). These results highlight the complex relationship between SNRPB expression, immune infiltration, and genomic alterations in the tumor microenvironment.</p>

<p>According to the World Health Organization (WHO) global report on alcohol and health and substance use disorder treatment, up to 400 million people globally suffer from alcohol use disorders, with 209 million diagnosed with alcohol dependence (<a href="#ref-16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-16">Gowing et al., 2015</a>). In recent years, there has been increasing attention on the critical role of genetic factors in regard with alcohol dependence, such as susceptible genes, aberrant gene expression, key enzymes, and critical molecular pathways (<a href="#ref-27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-27">Majeed et al., 2012</a>; <a href="#ref-3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-3">Albert &amp; Kruglyak, 2015</a>). Bioinformatics analysis, which emerged with the launch of the Human Genome Project, has gradually become an effective method for investigating the pathogenesis of complex psychiatric disorders (<a href="#ref-21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-21">Islam et al., 2023</a>). Recent studies have identified that serine protease inhibitor A3 may be involved in the development of alcohol dependence (<a href="#ref-41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-41">Zhang et al., 2022a</a>). However, to date, bioinformatics analysis and pathological studies of alcohol dependence have been limited. This study aims to address this gap by utilizing DIA mass spectrometry to conduct a comprehensive proteomic analysis of serum samples from alcohol-dependent patients and healthy controls, with the goal of revealing the differential expression of serum proteins induced by alcohol dependence and identifying potential biomarkers. By a comprehensive serum proteomics analysis, we identified 1,249 proteins in alcohol-dependent patients and 1,020 proteins in healthy controls. Among these, 996 proteins were expressed in both groups, 253 proteins were unique to alcohol-dependent patients, and 24 proteins were unique to healthy controls. Of the differentially expressed proteins, 195 showed significant differential expression, with 99 upregulated in the alcohol-dependent group and 96 upregulated in the control group. GO enrichment analysis indicated that these differentially expressed proteins are involved in various biological processes, particularly cellular metabolic processes, biological regulation, stimulus responses, and molecular functions such as binding and catalytic activity (<a href="#ref-43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-43">Zhao et al., 2014</a>). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the upregulated pathways in alcohol-dependent patients were related to ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, alcohol intoxication, viral protein interactions with cytokines and cytokine receptors, and ribosome function, while the downregulated pathways were associated with porphyrin metabolism, glycosaminoglycan degradation, glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor synthesis, and biotin metabolism (<a href="#ref-8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-8">Biernacka et al., 2013</a>). These findings provide a foundational understanding of the molecular mechanisms of alcohol dependence, particularly at the cellular and metabolic levels.</p>
<p>This study also identified the upregulation of SNRPB, which has attracted significant attention. SNRPB is a core RNA splicing protein that has been extensively studied in relation to the onset and development of various cancers, particularly hepatocellular carcinoma, where high expression of SNRPB is closely associated with poor prognosis (<a href="#ref-33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-33">Peng et al., 2020</a>). In alcohol-dependent patients, we observed a significant increase in SNRPB expression in serum, suggesting that it may play a key role in the onset and progression of alcohol dependence (<a href="#ref-2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-2">Agudelo et al., 2013</a>). Further analysis revealed that the upregulation of SNRPB was closely associated with immune responses and cell cycle regulation in alcohol-dependent patients, particularly involving important cellular functions such as the cell cycle and DNA repair (<a href="#ref-26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-26">Lewohl et al., 2011</a>). Additionally, immune cell infiltration analysis revealed that the high expression of SNRPB might be related to the infiltration of T cells, Treg cells, Tgd cells, and macrophages, suggesting that SNRPB may play a role in immune regulation associated with alcohol dependence (<a href="#ref-42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-42">Zhang et al., 2022b</a>).</p>
<p>Alcohol dependence, which is closely linked to the detrimental habit of chronic heavy drinking, represents a chronic pathological state. It triggers progressive liver dysfunction, exacerbates oxidative stress, and leads to systemic chronic inflammation, all of which can gradually reshape the serum protein expression profile (<a href="#ref-22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-22">Jampana &amp; Khan, 2011</a>; <a href="#ref-17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-17">Harris et al., 2022</a>). This study identified significant alterations in a series of protein markers in alcohol-dependent patients, which may be causally associated with changes in biological processes such as ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, immune pathway activation, and metabolic pathway suppression. The upregulation of SNRPB, a core splicing protein involved in cell proliferation and immune regulation, could represent either an adaptive response in the early stages of alcohol dependence or the manifestation of an irreversible pathological state developed under prolonged alcohol exposure. Although the current study could not systematically analyze disease duration as a covariate due to limitations in clinical data, the observed protein expression patterns undoubtedly reflect a molecular snapshot of the continuous pathological continuum from alcohol use disorder to long-term dependence. It is thus hypothesized that the duration of alcohol dependence is a significant factor contributing to proteomic heterogeneity among patients. Future research incorporating detailed drinking histories and clinical staging through longitudinal analysis may further elucidate the dynamic evolution of the molecular mechanisms underlying alcohol dependence.</p>
<p>The relationship between alcohol and cancer remains a noteworthy area of research. Alcohol is not only a significant risk factor for liver diseases but also a known etiological agent for various malignancies, particularly in tumor types such as hepatocellular carcinoma and esophageal cancer (ESCA) (<a href="#ref-34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-34">Rumgay et al., 2021</a>). Alcohol, through its metabolic products (such as acetaldehyde), induces DNA damage, inhibits DNA repair mechanisms, and accelerates tumorigenesis by inducing inflammatory responses (<a href="#ref-30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-30">Mizumoto et al., 2017</a>; <a href="#ref-34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-34">Rumgay et al., 2021</a>). Existing studies have shown that alcohol dependence may indirectly promote tumorigenesis by affecting DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, and cell proliferation (<a href="#ref-40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-40">Zhan et al., 2020</a>). In this study, SNRPB, as a key RNA splicing protein, is involved in tumor cell proliferation and survival (<a href="#ref-38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-38">Wang et al., 2024</a>). Our research suggests that SNRPB may, through its regulation of immune responses and cell cycle processes associated with alcohol dependence, promote the occurrence of alcohol-related tumors. Thus, SNRPB may not only serve as a potential biomarker for alcohol dependence but also play a significant role in cancer research (<a href="#ref-39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-39">Wu et al., 2022</a>). Future studies should further investigate the role of SNRPB in alcohol-related cancers and its potential clinical applications.</p>
<p>This study highlights the changes in the expression of SNRPB and other proteins in the serum of alcohol-dependent patients; however, there are certain limitations. The primary limitation is the relatively small sample size, particularly the number of control group samples, which may affect the generalizability of the results. Additionally, while we comprehensively analyzed the serum protein expression in alcohol-dependent patients using DIA mass spectrometry, we have not yet delved into the specific functions and interactions of these proteins. Future research needs to increase sample sizes and include functional experiments to authenticate the biological functions of these differentially expressed proteins. Our research on SNRPB opens new avenues for early diagnosis and treatment of alcohol dependence but requires further studies to understand its precise molecular mechanisms through <em>in vivo</em> and <em>in vitro</em> research. Through the use of DIA mass spectrometry this study analyzed serum proteins from alcohol-dependent patients and healthy controls and identified differentially expressed proteins linked to alcohol dependence which participate in various biological pathways. SNRPB upregulation provides fresh approaches to diagnosing and treating alcohol dependence. Additional research needs to confirm these results while investigating alcohol dependence at the molecular level to develop fresh treatment targets and personalized therapeutic approaches.</p></section><section id="sec32"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p>The research sheds light on molecular alterations in alcohol dependency by finding multiple proteins with changed expression levels in the serum of alcohol-dependent patients. The higher levels of SNRPB indicate that it could be a sign of alcohol-related issues due to its role in managing the immune system and cell growth. The study results enhance our understanding of the systemic molecular processes involved in alcohol dependence, which provides useful insights for developing early diagnosis methods and targeted therapies. In this study, we strictly adhered to the criteria for screening alcohol-dependent patients and healthy volunteers. The collected blood samples underwent further selection, excluding those with abnormal liver function indicators (such as ALT, GGT, and AST) or abnormal MCV levels. Highly sensitive methods were employed to further screen biomarkers including carbohydrate-deficient transferrin (CDT) and phosphatidylethanol (PEth) (<a href="#ref-1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-1">Afshar et al., 2017</a>; <a href="#ref-4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-4">Andreassen et al., 2018</a>; <a href="#ref-6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-6">Bertaso et al., 2016</a>; <a href="#ref-7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-7">Bianchi, Arfini &amp; Helander, 2008</a>; <a href="#ref-18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-18">Helander &amp; Zheng, 2009</a>; <a href="#ref-19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-19">Hock et al., 2005</a>; <a href="#ref-31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-31">Nicotra et al., 2017</a>; <a href="#ref-37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref-37">Sorio et al., 2017</a>), significantly reducing the impact of false-positive cases. Although limitations such as the small sample size exist, the clinical diagnoses and biomarker screening results are representative enough to demonstrate the validity of our findings. Subsequent research will expand the sample size and incorporate functional experiments to further validate the practical clinical significance of the current findings.</p></section><section id="supplemental-information"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title"> Supplemental Information</h2>
