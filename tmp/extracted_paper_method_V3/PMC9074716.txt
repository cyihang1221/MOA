</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="dad212286-sec-0010"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h3>
<p>It is important to understand which biological processes change with aging, and how such changes are associated with increased Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk. We studied how cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteomics changed with age and tested if associations depended on amyloid status, sex, and apolipoprotein E Ɛ4 genotype.</p></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0020"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>We included 277 cognitively intact individuals aged 46 to 89 years from Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, European Medical Information Framework for Alzheimer's Disease Multimodal Biomarker Discovery, and Metabolic Syndrome in Men. In total, 1149 proteins were measured with liquid chromatography mass spectrometry with multiple reaction monitoring/Rules‐Based Medicine, tandem mass tag mass spectrometry, and SOMAscan. We tested associations between age and protein levels in linear models and tested enrichment for Reactome pathways.</p></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0030"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>Levels of 252 proteins increased with age independently of amyloid status. These proteins were associated with immune and signaling processes. Levels of 21 proteins decreased with older age exclusively in amyloid abnormal participants and these were enriched for extracellular matrix organization.</p></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0040"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h3>
<p>We found amyloid‐independent and ‐dependent CSF proteome changes with older age, perhaps representing physiological aging and early AD pathology.</p></section></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0050"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p>With older age, the prevalence of Alzheimer's disease (AD) rises steeply.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0001">
<sup>1</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0002">
<sup>2</sup>
</a> Amyloid plaques and tau tangles in the brain are considered the pathological hallmarks of AD, and the prevalence of abnormal biomarkers levels reflective of these hallmarks increases in cognitively normal (CN) individuals with older age, preceding prevalence of dementia up to 20 years.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0003">
<sup>3</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0004">
<sup>4</sup>
</a> Possibly, other molecular processes also may become disrupted during this time. Better characterization of age effects, and the influence of AD pathology on such relationships, would give more insight into processes that increase the brain's vulnerability to neurodegeneration during aging. Proteomic analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) measures many proteins simultaneously, and can show which molecular processes may change with age and AD in vivo.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0005">
<sup>5</sup>
</a>
</p>
<p>Previous CSF proteomic studies found that approximately one tenth (30 of 248)<a href="#dad212286-bib-0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0006">
<sup>6</sup>
</a> to one third (82 of 312)<a href="#dad212286-bib-0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0007">
<sup>7</sup>
</a> of analyzed proteins show altered levels with older age in CN individuals. Those proteins showed enrichment for inflammation, response to injury, and the complement system.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0007">
<sup>7</sup>
</a> However, those studies did not address the influence of amyloid status on the CSF proteome, while an estimated 19% of 65‐year‐old individuals with normal cognition show abnormal amyloid and are at risk to develop dementia.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0003">
<sup>3</sup>
</a> It remains unclear to what extent observed age‐related alterations were influenced by the presence of AD pathology. Furthermore, sex and apolipoprotein E (<em>APOE</em>) ε4 carriership are known risk factors for AD that have been shown to either influence the CSF proteome<a href="#dad212286-bib-0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0008">
<sup>8</sup>
</a> (<em>APOE</em> ε4), or to influence specific CSF proteins, for example, tau (sex<a href="#dad212286-bib-0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0009">
<sup>9</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0010">
<sup>10</sup>
</a> and <em>APOE</em> ε4<a href="#dad212286-bib-0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0010">
<sup>10</sup>
</a>). We hypothesized that associations between protein levels and age may differ depending on amyloid status, sex, and <em>APOE</em> ε4 carriership.</p>
<p>The aim of this cross‐sectional study was to investigate the relationship of age‐ and amyloid‐related changes in the CSF proteome of cognitively intact individuals spanning an age range of 46 to 89 years, including potential effects of <em>APOE</em> ε4 carriership and sex.</p></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0060"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. MATERIAL AND METHODS</h2>
<section id="dad212286-sec-0070"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Study cohorts</h3>

<sup>11</sup>
</a> European Medical Information Framework for Alzheimer's Disease Multimodal Biomarker Discovery (EMIF‐AD MBD)<a href="#dad212286-bib-0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0012">
<sup>12</sup>
</a> and Metabolic Syndrome in Men (METSIM).<a href="#dad212286-bib-0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0013">
<sup>13</sup>
</a> All participants provided informed consent to participate in these studies. From these cohorts we selected CN individuals with available CSF proteomic data (Table <a href="#dad212286-tbl-0001" class="usa-link">1</a>).</p>

</div></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0080"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1.1. ADNI</h4>
<p>The ADNI (adni.loni.usc.edu) was launched in 2003 as a public–private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0011">
<sup>11</sup>
</a> The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early AD.</p>
<section class="bt xbox font-sm" id="boxed-text1"><section id="dad212286-sec-0090"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">RESEARCH IN CONTEXT</h5>
<ol id="dad212286-list-0001" class="list" style="list-style-type:decimal">
<li><p>
<strong>Systematic review</strong>: We reviewed the literature on effects of age on the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteome. Two studies, referenced in the text, have addressed age effects on the CSF proteome. Novel in our approach is a concomitant analysis of age and amyloid status effects on the proteome, which allows separating age‐ and Alzheimer's disease (AD)‐related effects.</p></li>
<li><p>
<strong>Interpretation</strong>: Our finding that extracellular matrix organization overlaps between proteins with amyloid‐independent and amyloid‐dependent associations with age could indicate this process contributes to the higher AD risk with older age.</p></li>
<li><p>
<strong>Future directions</strong>: While our results in cross‐sectional data suggest that general aging‐ and early AD‐related proteome changes overlap, further longitudinal studies of CSF protein levels in normal cognition are needed to better separate general aging‐ and AD‐related effects.</p></li>
</ol></section></section><p>In ADNI, normal cognition was defined as documented in the ADNI General Procedures Manual,<a href="#dad212286-bib-0014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0014">
<sup>14</sup>
</a> as (1) absence of memory complaints, (2) normal education‐adjusted memory function measured with Logical Memory II subscale (from Wechsler Memory Scaled—Revised), (3) Mini‐Mental State Examination<a href="#dad212286-bib-0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0015">
<sup>15</sup>
</a> (MMSE) score of 24 to 30, (4) Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR)<a href="#dad212286-bib-0016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0016">
<sup>16</sup>
</a> and Memory Box score of 0, and (5) absence of significant impairment in cognitive functions or activities of daily living. Both men and women were enrolled. The participants had a median age of 75 years (range 62 to 89 years).</p></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0100"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1.2. EMIF‐AD MBD</h4>
<p>The EMIF‐AD MBD study is a multicenter retrospective study to discover novel diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for AD.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0012">
<sup>12</sup>
</a> Normal cognition was defined as neuropsychological assessment within 1.5 standard deviations of age‐, sex‐, and education‐specific mean test scores. Both men and women were enrolled. The participants had a median age of 66 years (range 46 to 84 years).</p></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0110"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1.3. METSIM</h4>
<p>METSIM is an observational study on the development of diabetes.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0017" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0017">
<sup>17</sup>
</a> We selected participants without insulin resistance from METSIM (n = 30).<a href="#dad212286-bib-0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0013">
<sup>13</sup>
</a> Normal cognition was defined as living independently, absence of memory complaints, MMSE score of at least 25, and Functional Activities Questionnaire score<a href="#dad212286-bib-0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0018">
<sup>18</sup>
</a> &lt; 9. Participants were excluded if they had current (or a history of) neurological disease, significant systemic diseases, or used specific medications as described in more detail elsewhere.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0013">
<sup>13</sup>
</a> Only men were enrolled in the study. The participants had a median age of 62 years (range 55 to 69 years).</p></section></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0120"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. CSF proteomics</h3>
<p>CSF was collected and processed as described elsewhere (see ADNI 1 General Procedures Manual,<a href="#dad212286-bib-0014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0014">
<sup>14</sup>
</a> and study‐specific references<a href="#dad212286-bib-0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0012">
<sup>12</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0019">
<sup>19</sup>
</a>). ADNI proteomics data were generated using a liquid chromatography mass spectrometry with multiple reaction monitoring panel<a href="#dad212286-bib-0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0020">
<sup>20</sup>
</a> (311 peptides, from which we analyzed the normalized intensity data), Rules‐Based Medicine<a href="#dad212286-bib-0021" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0021">
<sup>21</sup>
</a> multiplex (83 proteins), and enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (four proteins). Peptides and proteins targeting the same protein were Z‐scored relative to controls and averaged if they showed a correlation of at least 0.5. EMIF‐AD MBD CSF proteomic data was generated with tandem mass tag mass spectrometry<a href="#dad212286-bib-0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0012">
<sup>12</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0022">
<sup>22</sup>
</a> resulting in 2535 proteins (deposited at the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository, dataset identifier: <a href="https://doi.org/10.6019/PXD019910" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://doi.org/10.6019/PXD019910</a>). CSF proteomic data of 4006 proteins in METSIM was measured on the SOMAscan platform (SomaLogic).<a href="#dad212286-bib-0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0019">
<sup>19</sup>
</a> Total signal intensities for the samples were median normalized by SomaLogic.</p>
<p>As we were interested in studying age effects, we pooled proteomic data across studies to ensure a wide age range. First, we harmonized natural log‐transformed proteomic data while anchoring age effects, by Z‐transformation relative to CN controls aged 60 to 75 years with normal amyloid, total tau (t‐tau), and phosphorylated tau (p‐tau; see schematic overview in Figure <a href="#dad212286-fig-0001" class="usa-link">1</a>). Next, we averaged peptides or proteins measuring the same UniProt identifier or highly correlating proteins (r at least 0.9) with different UniProt identifiers if they were translated from the same or homologous genes into protein scores and pooled the data (5039 proteins, see Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S1</a> in supporting information for different peptides and proteins per protein score). We included proteins measured in at least 25% of individuals and in at least 50 individuals with abnormal amyloid, resulting in a final dataset of 1149 proteins, of which 115 were measured in all three datasets, 538 proteins were measured in both EMIF‐AD MBD and METSIM, 42 proteins in both ADNI and METSIM, and 454 proteins in EMIF‐AD MBD only.</p>

</p></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0140"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. Determination of <em>APOE</em> genotypes</h3>
<p>In ADNI, <em>APOE</em> genotypes were determined by polymerase chain reaction amplification and enzymatic digestion as described in ADNI research plans<a href="#dad212286-bib-0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0025">
<sup>25</sup>
</a> and the resulting data were obtained from adni.loni.usc.edu. In METSIM, <em>APOE</em> genotypes were determined using TaqMan Allelic Discrimination Assays (Applied Biosystems).<a href="#dad212286-bib-0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0013">
<sup>13</sup>
</a> In EMIF‐AD MBD, <em>APOE</em> genotypes were measured using the Global Screening Array (Illumina Inc.) and TaqMan assays.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0026">
<sup>26</sup>
</a>
</p></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0150"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Statistics</h3>
<p>In all models, main effects of age, amyloid status, <em>APOE</em> ε4 carriership, and sex were included. In all analyses, <em>P</em>‐values &lt; .05 were considered significant without multiple testing correction, as we aimed to show patterns of age‐, amyloid‐, <em>APOE</em> ε4‐, and sex‐related changes in the proteome rather than identifying novel biomarkers. Interaction terms were tested using analysis of variance. Main effects of age, amyloid status, <em>APOE</em> ε4 carriership, and sex were reported in proteins that had no significant two‐ or three‐way interactions with any of the other variables, and regression coefficients for each of these were corrected for the other variables. Effect sizes were reported with estimated marginal slopes for age or estimated marginal mean differences for amyloid status, <em>APOE</em> ε4, and sex.</p>
<p>Age effects were group‐stratified for proteins showing a significant interaction between (1) age and amyloid status, (2) age and sex, or (3) age and <em>APOE</em> ε4 status. Finally, we also tested three‐way interactions between age and (1) amyloid status and <em>APOE</em> ε4 or (2) amyloid status and sex, and when significant, stratified analyses for amyloid and <em>APOE</em> or amyloid and sex subgroups. Proteins included in each of the analyses are documented in Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S2</a> in supporting information. Finally, for each of the above models, when at least 10 proteins showed an association with the outcome, we performed pathway analyses with Reactome<a href="#dad212286-bib-0027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0027">
<sup>27</sup>
</a> (Reactome Pathway Gene Set version 69 downloaded from Reactome, <a href="https://reactome.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://reactome.org/</a>
<a href="#dad212286-bib-0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0028">
<sup>28</sup>
</a>), using all Reactome categories except “Disease” (1779 pathways). <em>RITAN</em> 1.10.0 was used to test pathway enrichment with all protein‐coding genes as a background and false discovery rate correction at a <em>q</em> threshold of 0.05, and we report significant pathways if at least three proteins overlapped with the pathway. All analyses were run in R version 3.6.1 “Action of the Toes.” <em>Emmeans</em> v.1.4.2 was used to estimate effect sizes, and volcano plots were generated with <em>EnhancedVolcano</em> 1.4.0. <em>Shiny</em> v.1.4.0 was used to create a user interface allowing querying of the age associations in the CSF proteome (available at <a href="https://kwesenhagen.shinyapps.io/Aging_proteomics/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://kwesenhagen.shinyapps.io/Aging_proteomics/</a>).</p></section></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0160"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. RESULTS</h2>
<section id="dad212286-sec-0170"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Sample description</h3>
<p>In total, 277 CN participants aged 46 to 89 years were included. Of these participants, 109 (39%) had abnormal amyloid levels in CSF (Table <a href="#dad212286-tbl-0001" class="usa-link">1</a>). Relative to participants with normal amyloid, participants with abnormal amyloid were older, more often had an <em>APOE</em> ε4 genotype, and more often had abnormal t‐tau and p‐tau in CSF. The cohorts were largely comparable in demographics, but ADNI participants were on average older. The METSIM study included only men, and <em>APOE</em> ε4 carriership was more common than in the other two studies. In addition, in EMIF‐AD MBD compared to ADNI, participants with abnormal amyloid had lower MMSE scores and less often had abnormal CSF p‐tau.</p></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0180"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Main effects of age, amyloid status, APOE ε4 carriership, and sex</h3>
<p>We first investigated the main effects of age, amyloid status, <em>APOE</em> ε4, and sex as predictors of CSF protein levels (see web interface at <a href="https://kwesenhagen.shinyapps.io/Aging_proteomics/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://kwesenhagen.shinyapps.io/Aging_proteomics/</a> showing the effects for all included proteins). We tested the main effect of age in 911 of the 1149 proteins that showed no interactions between age and amyloid status, sex, and <em>APOE</em> ε4. Two hundred fifty‐two proteins (28%) had altered levels with older age, of which 194 (77% of 252) proteins had higher levels with older age and were enriched for immune system and signal transduction processes, and processes related to cellular responses to external stimuli, among others (Figures <a href="#dad212286-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2A, 2E</a>, and <a href="#dad212286-fig-0003" class="usa-link">3A</a>; Tables <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S3 and S4</a> in supporting information). Subsets of these processes were uniquely enriched among these proteins compared to the other analyses. Fifty‐eight proteins (23% of all 252 age‐related proteins) had lower levels with older age, and did not show enrichment for specific biological processes (Figures <a href="#dad212286-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2A</a> and <a href="#dad212286-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2E</a>; Tables <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S3 and S4</a>).</p>

<p>Finally, we tested the main effect of sex in 835 proteins without interactions between sex and age, amyloid status, and <em>APOE</em> ε4. Of these 835 proteins, 352 proteins (42%) had different levels in women compared to men, of which 243 (69% of 352) proteins were increased in women (Figure <a href="#dad212286-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2D</a>; Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S7</a> in supporting information) and were enriched for metabolism, metabolism of proteins, and extracellular matrix organization–related processes, among others (Figure <a href="#dad212286-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2E</a> and <a href="#dad212286-fig-0003" class="usa-link">3C</a>; Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S4</a>). The remaining 109 (31% of 352) proteins showed higher levels in men (Figure <a href="#dad212286-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2D</a>; Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S7</a>) and were enriched for metabolism of proteins, hemostasis, and immune system–related processes, among others (Figure <a href="#dad212286-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2E</a> and <a href="#dad212286-fig-0003" class="usa-link">3D</a>; Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S4</a>). Because the METSIM study consisted of only men, which potentially may have biased the results, we repeated our analyses excluding the METSIM dataset. Excluding the METSIM dataset did not influence the demographics of the combined cohort (Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S8</a> in supporting information), and the majority of proteins and pathways related to sex remained significant after excluding METSIM (342 of 352 proteins [97%] and 63 of 65 [97%] pathways; Tables <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S4 and S7</a>).</p></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0190"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3. Age‐related proteome changes depending on amyloid status</h3>
<p>Next, we looked at 39 (3% of 1149) proteins for which the association with age depended on amyloid status (Figure <a href="#dad212286-fig-0004" class="usa-link">4A</a>; Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S9</a> in supporting information). In 24 (62% of 39) of these proteins, age effects were restricted to amyloid‐positive participants, of which 7 proteins including t‐tau and p‐tau increased with age, and the remaining 17 proteins showed decreased levels with older age. Proteins that decreased with age in amyloid abnormal individuals were enriched for extracellular matrix organization, a process that was also enriched in proteins with a main effect of age. In the amyloid normal group, seven proteins were associated with age (18% of 39). Eight proteins (21% of 39 proteins) had a significant interaction between age and amyloid status but no significant age relation in either amyloid subgroup.</p>

None of these proteins were observed in the previous models of age effects. After stratification on amyloid and <em>APOE</em> ε4 status, we found most age associations in amyloid abnormal <em>APOE</em> ε4 non‐carriers, in whom 11 out of the 29 proteins (38%) were associated with age, all with lower levels with older age and without enrichment of specific biological processes. Five proteins (17% of 29 proteins) had a significant three‐way interaction, but no significant age effect in any amyloid status and <em>APOE</em> ε4 subgroup (see Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S10</a> for other amyloid status and <em>APOE</em> ε4 subgroups).</p>
<p>Finally, 85 (7% of 1149) proteins showed a three‐way interaction between age, amyloid status, and sex (Figure <a href="#dad212286-fig-0004" class="usa-link">4C</a>; Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S11</a> in supporting information). Most associations with age were found in amyloid abnormal women (25 proteins, 29%): two proteins showed higher levels with older age, and 23 proteins decreased with older age without enrichment for specific biological processes. In amyloid abnormal men, 21 proteins (25%) showed age associations, all but one with higher levels with older age, and showed no enrichment for biological processes. Twenty‐eight proteins (33% of 85 proteins) had a significant three‐way interaction, but no significant age association in any amyloid and sex subgroup (see Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S11</a> for other amyloid status and sex subgroups). We tested if the results remained similar when excluding the METSIM dataset, to test if this male‐only dataset drove the three‐way interaction between amyloid and sex. After excluding METSIM data, the age effects remained the same for the majority of proteins (56 of 85 proteins, 66%; Figure <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0001" class="usa-link">S1</a> in supporting information, Table <a href="#dad212286-supinfo-0002" class="usa-link">S11</a>).</p></section></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0200"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. DISCUSSION</h2>
<p>In this study, we investigated in cognitively intact individuals how the CSF proteome changes with older age, and whether such changes depend on AD pathology (measured by amyloid status), <em>APOE</em> ε4, and sex. We found that 252 (22% of 1149) proteins showed age‐related changes independent of amyloid status, <em>APOE</em> ε4 carriership, and sex, of which 194 proteins showed higher levels with older age and were related to processes of the immune system, signal transduction, and cellular responses to external stimuli. Another group of 39 proteins showed age‐related changes that depended on amyloid status, with enrichment of extracellular matrix organization. These results show that the CSF proteome changes substantially with older age, and that a part may reflect early AD pathology. We found that some enriched biological processes in proteins with amyloid‐dependent and amyloid‐independent age associations overlapped, suggesting these processes may contribute to the brain's higher vulnerability to neurodegeneration with aging.</p>
<p>Several of the proteins that were associated with age independent of amyloid status have been previously reported to increase with older age, including NEFL,<a href="#dad212286-bib-0029" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0029">
<sup>29</sup>
</a> YKL‐40,<a href="#dad212286-bib-0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0030">
<sup>30</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0031">
<sup>31</sup>
</a> A2M,<a href="#dad212286-bib-0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0032">
<sup>32</sup>
</a> CLU,<a href="#dad212286-bib-0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0033">
<sup>33</sup>
</a> C4<a href="#dad212286-bib-0034" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0034">
<sup>34</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0035">
<sup>35</sup>
</a>, and SNCA.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0036" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0036">
<sup>36</sup>
</a> Two previous studies analyzed age effects on the CSF proteome,<a href="#dad212286-bib-0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0006">
<sup>6</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0007">
<sup>7</sup>
</a> which reported 30<a href="#dad212286-bib-0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0006">
<sup>6</sup>
</a> and 77<a href="#dad212286-bib-0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0007">
<sup>7</sup>
</a> age‐related proteins. For 31 of the 94 age‐related proteins found in these two studies, we investigated the main effect of age in our analyses. We reproduced age associations for 10 of these proteins. The other proteins were not related to age in our study (17 proteins), or were age‐related but in opposite directions in our study (three proteins). These discrepancies could be due to methodological differences, as our results were adjusted for amyloid, <em>APOE</em>, and sex, which previous studies did not do.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0006">
<sup>6</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0007">
<sup>7</sup>
</a> Potentially, the predominantly increased protein levels with older age could be due to lower CSF turnover with older age leading to more concentrated CSF.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0037" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0037">
<sup>37</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0038" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0038">
<sup>38</sup>
</a> However, we also see groups of proteins that decreased with older age or showed interactions between age and amyloid, <em>APOE</em> ε4, or sex. This makes it unlikely that CSF turnover explains age‐related proteome changes. Both age‐related and amyloid status‐related proteins showed specific associations with various immune system processes. This involvement of the immune system both with aging<a href="#dad212286-bib-0039" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0039">
<sup>39</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0040">
<sup>40</sup>
</a> and AD pathology<a href="#dad212286-bib-0041" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0041">
<sup>41</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0042" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0042">
<sup>42</sup>
</a> has been documented before. We also found overlap between age and amyloid‐related processes for pathways related to the extracellular matrix and insulin‐like growth factor (IGF) transport and uptake, in line with previous studies showing different levels of extracellular matrix proteins<a href="#dad212286-bib-0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0005">
<sup>5</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0043" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0043">
<sup>43</sup>
</a> and IGF metabolism proteins<a href="#dad212286-bib-0044" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0044">
<sup>44</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0045">
<sup>45</sup>
</a> in AD patients compared to controls. Perhaps, these processes may contribute to the development of AD pathology in very early phases of the disease.</p>
<p>We found relatively few effects of <em>APOE</em> ε4 carriership on the CSF proteome (22 proteins). We think that <em>APOE</em> ε4 effects are probably more common than we measured here. Previous studies have shown that <em>APOE</em> ε4 is part of a network of co‐expressed genes,<a href="#dad212286-bib-0046" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0046">
<sup>46</sup>
</a> and that AD risk factors may differ in <em>APOE</em> ε4 carriers versus non‐carriers.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0047" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0047">
<sup>47</sup>
</a> Perhaps the low number of <em>APOE</em> ε4 effects is due to heterogeneity in <em>APOE</em> ε4 effects: a previous study that analyzed differences between <em>APOE</em> ε4 carriers and non‐carriers in EMIF‐AD MBD and ADNI observed a relatively low correspondence between the cohorts (58%).<a href="#dad212286-bib-0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0008">
<sup>8</sup>
</a> Nonetheless, we found 29 proteins that had a three‐way interaction between age, amyloid status, and <em>APOE</em> ε4, suggesting that the association of a proportion of CSF proteins with AD may differ in <em>APOE</em> ε4 carriers and non‐carriers.</p>
<p>Our study population contained a group of amyloid normal individuals with abnormal t‐tau or p‐tau, which may reflect suspected non‐Alzheimer's pathophysiology (SNAP). We found that in amyloid abnormal individuals, and at a trend level in amyloid normal individuals (<em>P</em>‐value &lt; 0.1), levels of t‐tau and p‐tau increased with age, as has been observed previously.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0048" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0048">
<sup>48</sup>
</a>, <a href="#dad212286-bib-0049" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0049">
<sup>49</sup>
</a> If tau levels indeed change with age also in amyloid normal individuals, tau levels could be another factor influencing the proteome, which should be investigated in future studies.</p>
<p>We found different levels of many proteins between women and men (352 proteins). So far, to our knowledge, no studies have investigated sex effects on the CSF proteome in later adult life. We found that proteins that differed between men and women were enriched for processes related to metabolism of proteins, in line with a study of sex effects on the plasma proteome.<a href="#dad212286-bib-0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="dad212286-bib-0050">
<sup>50</sup>
</a> Overall, it is important to conduct more studies of both the effects of <em>APOE</em> ε4 and sex on the CSF proteome, because therapeutic targets may be different in men and women, and perhaps also depending on <em>APOE</em> ε4, which has important implications for clinical trials and drug discovery studies.</p>
<p>A potential limitation of our study is that proteomics was measured on different platforms, which may have introduced noise in the data. Still, we think such effects on our results are small, since we observed more proteins than previous studies. In addition, we used cross‐sectional data to assess age effects on the CSF proteome, and it is therefore possible that the age effects we observed here were confounded by additional unknown factors that differed between older and younger individuals in our data. Further longitudinal studies are needed to study aging effects within individuals over time. As we analyzed patterns of proteome changes without multiple testing, caution is warranted when interpreting results for single proteins. The proteins we found should therefore be reproduced also in future studies.</p>
<p>Strong aspects of our study are the large CSF proteomic dataset spanning a wide age range, and simultaneous analysis of the changes in the CSF proteome depending on age, amyloid status, <em>APOE</em> ε4 carriership, and sex. Effects of these factors on the CSF proteome can be queried through a web interface to aid biomarker and fundamental studies into AD (<a href="https://kwesenhagen.shinyapps.io/Aging_proteomics/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://kwesenhagen.shinyapps.io/Aging_proteomics/</a>).</p>
<p>In conclusion, we provide an overview of biological processes that are generally altered in aging, versus those that depended on amyloid status, sex, and/or <em>APOE</em> genotype. The amyloid‐dependent age associations in CSF proteins we reported here, of which many had not been previously reported as related to AD, provide more insight in the biological processes that are altered in an early phase of AD, and may ultimately help to identify novel therapeutic targets.</p></section><section id="dad212286-sec-0220"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CONFLICTS OF INTEREST</h2>
