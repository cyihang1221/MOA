<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Background</h3>
<p id="Par1">Heart Failure represents the severe stage of various heart diseases. Its global morbidity and mortality are on the rise, making it a serious public health issue that imposes a heavy burden on patients’ families and society. Currently, there are relatively few systematic studies on the changes in specific metabolites and pathways in different stages of heart failure, such as Stage A, Stage B and Stage C.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Aims</h3>
<p id="Par2">Using untargeted-targeted metabolomics to explore the metabolic characteristics of Heart Failure, and screen out serum metabolic markers with potential diagnostic and prognostic value.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p id="Par3">This study is a cross-sectional study. A total of 210 heart failure patients from Xiyuan Hospital of China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences were enrolled between October 2023 and October 2024. Among them, 60 patients were selected for targeted metabolomics analysis via stratified sampling. Serum samples of the patients were collected and pretreated with methanol, then metabolites were detected using untargeted and targeted LC-MS respectively. After the raw data were processed with MSDIAL, pattern recognition was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). Differential metabolites with variable importance in projection (VIP) &gt; 1 and <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05 were screened, and relevant pathways were analyzed via enrichment analysis using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p id="Par4">Untargeted metabolomics revealed that, compared with patients in Stages A and B, those with heart failure in Stage C had decreased serum levels of alanine, creatine, and branched-chain amino acids, along with increased levels of citric acid, fumaric acid, and malic acid. The differential metabolites were primarily enriched in pathways including the citric acid cycle, central carbon metabolism, and amino acid metabolism, indicating that energy metabolism plays a crucial role in the occurrence and progression of HF. Targeted metabolomics validated the findings from untargeted metabolomics: compared with Stage A, the level of phosphoenolpyruvate in Stage B was reduced; and in comparison with patients in Stage A or B, patients in Stage C showed decreased serum levels of multiple energy metabolites (e.g., glucose-6-phosphate, fructose-6-phosphate, 3-phosphoglyceric acid, AMP, ADP and ATP) as well as increased levels of malic acid, which is consistent with the characteristics of the “hypermetabolism-energy starvation” paradox.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p id="Par5">Stage C of heart failure is characterized by energy metabolism collapse (decreased ATP and TCA compensation), and differential metabolites (such as malic acid) may serve as potential candidate biomarkers pending longitudinal validation.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h3>
<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07711-3.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Heart failure, Stage, Non-targeted metabolomics, Targeted metabolomics</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par6">Chronic Heart Failure (CHF) is at the end-stage of the development of various heart diseases, posing a serious threat to human health and having become a major global public health challenge [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>]. With the intensification of population aging and the continuous rise in the incidence of cardiovascular diseases, the prevalence and mortality of CHF have been increasing year by year [<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>]. Its pathogenesis is complex, involving multiple aspects such as activation of the neuroendocrine system, myocardial remodeling, and oxidative stress [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>]. However, there are still many limitations in the current clinical diagnosis and treatment of CHF, and there is an urgent need to explore more effective biomarkers to achieve early accurate diagnosis of the disease, dynamic monitoring of disease progression and accurate assessment of prognosis.</p>
<p id="Par7">Metabolomics, as a key component of systems biology, focuses primarily on the comprehensive qualitative and quantitative analysis of endogenous metabolites in organisms under specific physiological or pathological states [<a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>]. With remarkable progress in metabolomics technology, it can explore potential biomarkers and provide a new perspective for the in-depth analysis of disease pathogenesis [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>]. In the field of CHF research, conducting metabolomics-based analysis of serum samples is expected to reveal specific metabolic pathways and key metabolites closely associated with the occurrence and progression of the disease. These metabolic markers not only help elucidate the pathophysiological process of CHF but also may provide new targets and strategies for clinical diagnosis and treatment [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>].</p>
<p id="Par8">In this study, untargeted and targeted metabolomics techniques were utilized to conduct systematic analysis of serum metabolites in patients with CHF. The study identifies serum metabolic markers with potential diagnostic and prognostic value and provides an in-depth interpretation of their biological significance in the disease process. Through in-depth exploration of the metabolic characteristics of CHF, this study aims to reveal the close association between the onset of heart failure and various comorbidities as well as the complexity of its underlying pathological mechanisms, and to identify common pathways among the various pathological mechanisms of heart failure related to energy metabolism changes and new therapeutic targets.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study design and participants</h3>
<p id="Par9">This study is a cross-sectional study, in which a total of 210 participants were enrolled, including 24 cases of heart failure at risk stage (Stage A), 84 cases of pre-heart failure stage (Stage B), and 102 cases of symptomatic heart failure stage (Stage C). Using the stratified sampling method, 60 participants were selected from the 210 patients, with 15 cases of heart failure at risk stage (Stage A), 15 cases of pre-heart failure stage (Stage B), and 30 cases of symptomatic heart failure stage (Stage C); samples were selected from each stratum using the random number table method (see Fig. <a href="#Fig1" class="usa-link">1</a>). All participants were patients who visited Xiyuan Hospital of China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences from October 2023 to October 2024. The diagnostic criteria for heart failure staging were developed with reference to the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure [<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>]: STAGE A: At-Risk for Heart Failure, patients at risk for HF but without current or previous symptoms/signs of HF and without structural/functional heart disease or abnormal biomarkers; STAGE B: Pre-Heart Failure, patients without current or previous symptoms/signs of HF but evidence of 1 of the following: Structural heart disease; Evidence of increased filling pressures; Risk factors and increased natriuretic peptide levels or persistently elevated cardiac troponin in the absence of competing diagnoses; STAGE C: Symptomatic Heart Failure, patients with current or previous symptoms/signs of HF. Inclusion criteria for cases: Meeting the Western medicine diagnostic criteria for heart failure staging; Aged 50–90 years, regardless of gender; Patients or their family members providing consent and signing the informed consent form. Exclusion criteria for cases: Patients meeting any of the following criteria will be excluded: Heart failure caused by congenital heart disease, valvular heart disease, myocarditis, autoimmune diseases, or other conditions; Suffering from malignant tumors; Suffering from mental illnesses; Having participated in other studies within the past 3 months.</p>

<p id="Par11">Instruments and Reagents: Mass spectrometer (Manufacturer: Thermo Scientific, Model: Q Exactive Plus), mass spectrometer (Manufacturer: AB SCIEX, Model: QTRAP6500+), ultra-high performance liquid chromatograph (UPLC) (Manufacturer: Shimadzu, Model: Nexera X2 LC-30AD), chromatographic column (Manufacturer: Waters, Model: ACQUITY UPLCB@HSS T3 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm)), ultrasonic system (Manufacturer: Diagenode, Model: Bioruptor), vacuum centrifugal concentrator (Manufacturer: Eppendorf, Model: Concentrator plus), centrifuge (Manufacturer: Eppendorf, Model: Centrifuge 5424R), visible-ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Manufacturer: Thermo, Model: MultiSkan FC), acetonitrile (Manufacturer: Millipore, Model: 1.00030.4008), methanol (Manufacturer: Millipore, Model: 1.06007.4008), ammonia water (Manufacturer: Merck, Model: 105426), formic acid (Manufacturer: Millipore, Model: 111670), and ammonium acetate (Manufacturer: Sigma, Model: 73594).</p>
<p id="Par12">Untargeted LC-MS: For each participant, 2 mL of venous blood was collected in the early morning while fasting, and the blood was anticoagulated. The sample was centrifuged at 2000×g for 10 min to separate the serum, which was then numbered. After vortexing the serum to mix it thoroughly, 100 µL of each serum sample was taken, and 300 µL of pre-cooled 100% methanol solution was added. The mixture was incubated at -20 °C for 30 min, then centrifuged at 16,000×g and 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant was collected and stored for subsequent mass spectrometry injection and analysis.</p>
<p id="Par13">Targeted LC-MS: 100 µL of the sample was taken, and 400 mL of pre-cooled 100% methanol-water solution was added to it respectively. The mixture was vortexed to mix thoroughly, then sonicated in an ice bath for 20 min, and incubated at -20 °C for 1 h. After that, it was centrifuged at 16,000×g and 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant was evaporated to dryness using a high-speed vacuum centrifugal concentrator, and the sample was reconstituted in 100 µL of pre-cooled 50% methanol-water solution. Finally, it was centrifuged at 20,000×g and 4 °C for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected for subsequent mass spectrometry injection and analysis.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Untargeted LC-MS analyses</h3>
<p id="Par14">Chromatographic Separation: The chromatographic column used was ACQUITY UPLC<sup>®</sup> HSS T3 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The chromatographic mobile phases were as follows: Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution, and Mobile phase B was acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. The injection volume was 10 µL, the column temperature was 40 °C, and the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. The gradient elution conditions were: 0–2 min, 0% mobile phase B; 2–6 min, mobile phase B linearly increased from 0% to 48%; 6–10 min, mobile phase B linearly increased from 48% to 100%; 10–12 min, mobile phase B was maintained at 100%; 12–12.1 min, mobile phase B linearly decreased from 100% to 0%; 12.1–15 min, mobile phase B was maintained at 0%.</p>
<p id="Par15">Mass Spectrometry Data Acquisition: Each sample was detected in positive (+) and negative (−) ion modes using electrospray ionization (ESI). After separation by UPLC, the samples were analyzed by a QE Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) with ionization performed using a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source. The ionization conditions were as follows: Spray Voltage: 3.8 kV (+) and 3.2 kV (−); Capillary Temperature: 320 °C (±); Sheath Gas: 30 (±); Aux Gas: 5 (±); Probe Heater Temp: 350 °C (±); S-Lens RF Level: 50. The mass spectrometry acquisition settings were as follows: Total acquisition time: 15 min. Parent ion scan range: 75–1050 m/z. Full scan resolution (MS1): 70,000 @ m/z 200. AGC target value (MS1): 3e6. Maximum ion injection time (MS1): 100 ms. For MS2 analysis, data were acquired using the following method: After each full scan, MS2 spectra of the 10 parent ions with the highest intensity were triggered and collected. MS2 resolution: 17,500 @ m/z 200. AGC target value (MS2): 1e5. Maximum ion injection time (MS2): 50 ms. MS2 Activation Type: higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD). Isolation window: 2 m/z. Normalized collision energy (stepped): 20, 30, 40.</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Targeted LC-MS analyses</h3>
<p id="Par16">Chromatographic Separation: The chromatographic column used was Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm column). The chromatographic mobile phases were configured as follows: Mobile phase A was a 5% acetonitrile aqueous solution containing 10 mM ammonium acetate (pH = 9); Mobile phase B was a 95% acetonitrile aqueous solution containing 10 mM ammonium acetate (pH = 9). The injection volume was 5 µL, the column temperature was 40 °C, and the flow rate was 300 µL/min. The gradient elution conditions were: 0–2 min, mobile phase B was maintained at 95%; 2–9 min, mobile phase B linearly changed from 95% to 70%; 9–10 min, mobile phase B linearly changed from 70% to 30%; 10–11 min, mobile phase B was maintained at 30%; 11–11.5 min, mobile phase B linearly changed from 30% to 95%; 11.5–15 min, mobile phase B was maintained at 95%.</p>
<p id="Par17">Mass Spectrometry Data Acquisition: Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using a QTRAP6500 + mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX) in positive/negative ion modes. The parameters of the ESI source were as follows: For positive ion mode: Source Temperature: 550 °C; Ion Source Gas1 (GAS1): 40 psi; Ion Source Gas2 (GAS2): 50 psi; Curtain Gas (CUR): 35 psi; Ion Spray Voltage Floating (ISVF): 5500 V. For negative ion mode: Source Temperature: 550 °C; Ion Source Gas1 (GAS1): 40 psi; Ion Source Gas2 (GAS2): 50 psi; Curtain Gas (CUR): 35 psi; Ion Spray Voltage Floating (ISVF): -4500 V. MRM (multiple reaction monitoring) mode was used to detect the target ion pairs.</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolomics data processing</h3>
<p id="Par18">Raw data were subjected to peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction using MSDIAL software. For metabolite structure identification, accurate mass matching (mass tolerance &lt; 10 ppm) and MS/MS spectrum matching (mass tolerance &lt; 0.01 Da) were employed, with searches conducted in public databases including HMDB, MassBank, and GNPS, as well as the self-built Baipu Metabolite Database (BP-DB). For the extracted data, ion peaks with missing values &gt; 50% within the group were removed and excluded from subsequent statistical analysis. The positive and negative ion data were separately subjected to total peak area normalization; positive and negative ion peaks were then integrated, and Python software was used for pattern recognition. After the data were preprocessed via Unit Variance Scaling (UV), subsequent data analysis was performed.</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analyses</h3>
<p id="Par19">Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0. For missing values, they were imputed using the mean (for normally distributed variables) or median (for skewed distributed variables) of the respective variables (High-sensitivity C-reactive protein, Glycated Hemoglobin, High-density Lipoprotein Cholesterol, low-density Lipoprotein Cholesterol and E/A). Categorical data were expressed as frequency/percentage [n/(%)]. For continuous data, normality test was first performed: those conforming to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), while those not conforming to normal distribution were expressed as median and interquartile range. For continuous variables between two groups: if they showed normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, t-test was used; if they showed normal distribution but heterogeneous variance, corrected t-test was selected; if they did not show normal distribution, Mann-Whitney test was applied. For continuous variables among multiple groups: if they conformed to normal distribution, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used; if they did not conform to normal distribution, Kruskal-Wallis test was used to examine the difference distribution between groups. For data with <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05 in ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test, Bonferroni correction was used for pairwise comparisons. Categorical data were compared using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The screening threshold for differential metabolites was PLS-DA VIP &gt; 1 and <em>P</em><sub><em>adjust</em></sub><em>(FDR)</em> &lt; 0.05. The normalized peak values of differential metabolites screened by non-targeted metabolomics were divided into 4 groups according to quartiles. Taking the Q1 group as the reference group, an ordered multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed on the metabolites with inter-group differences. If the P value of the parallel line test for the metabolites was &lt; 0.05, an unordered multinomial logistic regression analysis was used. A multivariate binary logistic regression analysis was used to examine the associations between different concentration groups of metabolites and heart failure (stages A + B vs. stage C). The goodness - of - fit of the model was evaluated by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and the dose-response trend was verified by the chi - square test for trend. A <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the differential metabolites screened by targeted metabolomics, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to establish different models.</p></section><section id="Sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Ethics</h3>
<p id="Par20">This trial was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients in this study signed the informed consent form. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xiyuan Hospital of China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (No. 2023XLA112-2) and registered with the International Clinical Trial Registry Platform for Traditional Medicine (No. ITMCTR2024000120).</p></section></section><section id="Sec10"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="Sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Characteristics of study participants</h3>
<p id="Par21">Untargeted metabolomics included a total of 210 heart failure patients, consisting of 24 cases in Stage A, 84 cases in Stage B, and 102 cases in Stage C. Targeted metabolomics included 60 heart failure patients, with 15 cases in Stage A, 15 cases in Stage B, and 30 cases in Stage C (Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>). There were no significant statistical differences in gender, Body Mass Index (BMI), Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP), or Heart Rate (HR) among the three groups (<em>P</em> &gt; 0.05). Regarding past medical history, there were no significant differences in the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or hyperlipidemia among the groups (<em>P</em> &gt; 0.05). Additionally, there were no significant differences in the use of medications—including Angiotensin Converting Enzyme Inhibitors (ACEI), Angiotensin Receptor Blocker (ARB), Angiotensin-Receptor Neprilysin Inhibitor (ARNI), Calcium Channel Blocker (CCB), statins, antiplatelet drugs, oral hypoglycemic agents, and subcutaneous insulin injection—across the three groups (<em>P</em> &gt; 0.05). Levels of N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and Cardiac Troponin T (cTnT) in patients with Stage C heart failure were higher than those in patients with Stage A or B. Compared with patients in Stage A or B, those in Stage C had a lower Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF).</p>

<p id="Par28">A total of 226 differentially expressed metabolites were identified between Stage B and Stage A, among which 126 were upregulated and 100 were downregulated (Fig. <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a>b). <p id="Par39">HF is a severe stage of various heart diseases. Its global morbidity and mortality are on the rise, making it a serious public health issue [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>]. In China, due to population aging and exposure to cardiovascular disease risk factors, the prevalence rate of HF has been rising, which imposes a heavy burden on patients’ families and society [<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>]. Studying the changes in metabolites and metabolic pathways across HF Stages A, B, and C can provide precise biomarkers for early screening and enable early intervention. This plays an important role in improving patient prognosis, better assessing disease conditions and outcomes, reducing mortality, and enhancing quality of life. Although certain progress has been made in domestic and international research on metabolite and pathway changes in HF, there are still many shortcomings and gaps. Currently, most studies focus on the overall state of HF, while there are relatively few systematic studies on stage-specific metabolites and pathway changes across different phases such as HF Stage A, Stage B, and Stage C [<a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>]. In this study, we first screened differential metabolites in different stages of HF using untargeted metabolomics technology and found that abnormal energy metabolism may be a key driver of disease progression. Furthermore, we validated core metabolites and pathways using targeted energy metabolomics technology, which systematically clarified the characteristics of energy metabolism disorders in different stages of HF and their clinical significance.</p>
<p id="Par40">Patients in Stage A of HF only have high-risk factors for HF; those in Stage B develop structural changes in the heart; and those in Stage C begin to show symptoms and signs of HF. The progression from Stage A to Stage C represents a gradual development of HF [<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>]. Results from untargeted metabolomics showed that compared with patients in Stage A or B, patients in Stage C had decreased serum levels of Alanine and Carnitine, which are mainly involved in physiological processes such as the glucose-alanine cycle, energy metabolism, and oxidative stress. In the human body, Alanine not only serves as an important substrate for gluconeogenesis but also participates in energy metabolism. The decreased level of Alanine when HF progresses to Stage C may suggest disturbances in the body’s energy metabolism. Due to impaired cardiac function, the myocardium cannot meet its energy demands; Alanine then participates in gluconeogenesis to maintain blood glucose levels, thereby leading to a reduction in its serum content. This finding is consistent with the results of previous animal studies [<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>]. Carnitine plays an indispensable role in fatty acid β-oxidation: it can transport long-chain fatty acids to the mitochondrial matrix, enabling their oxidative breakdown to provide energy for cells [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>]. Additionally, Carnitine has antioxidant properties and can alleviate oxidative stress through multiple mechanisms. On one hand, it regulates the activity of antioxidant enzymes to enhance the heart’s intrinsic antioxidant defense capacity; on the other hand, it can directly scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) to reduce ROS-induced damage to myocardial cells [<a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>]. In Stage B of HF, patients may experience increased Carnitine levels due to cardiac compensatory mechanisms, which strengthens antioxidant effects. In Stage C, however, the heart loses its compensatory capacity, leading to decreased Carnitine levels. This impairs the normal oxidative metabolism of fatty acids, resulting in insufficient energy supply to myocardial cells and, to a certain extent, exacerbating the progression of HF.</p>
<p id="Par41">In addition, compared with patients in Stage A or B, patients in Stage C had increased serum levels of Citric acid, Fumaric acid, and Malic acid, and these metabolites are mainly enriched in the TCA cycle pathway. The TCA cycle is a key link in cellular aerobic respiration; while generating energy (ATP), it also provides cells with various precursor substances for biosynthesis. In Stage C HF, there is severe impairment of mitochondrial function in myocardial cells, accompanied by disorders of oxidative phosphorylation, reduced ATP production, decreased activity of respiratory chain complexes, and impaired mitochondrial quality [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>]. This energy metabolism disorder reduces the efficiency of the TCA cycle, preventing intermediate products from proceeding smoothly to subsequent reactions and thus leading to their accumulation in cells or serum. In Stage C HF, the increased levels of Citric acid, Fumaric acid, and Malic acid also represent a compensatory mechanism by which the body attempts to enhance the TCA cycle to increase energy production in response to the elevated energy demand caused by impaired cardiac function [<a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>]. However, this compensation may have limitations. Although the levels of TCA cycle intermediates increase, the effective utilization efficiency of energy does not improve correspondingly—likely due to already damaged mitochondrial function in myocardial cells. Instead, it may lead to the accumulation of metabolites, triggering oxidative stress and cellular damage [<a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>, <a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>]. As intermediate products of the TCA cycle, the levels of Fumaric acid and Malic acid are also closely related to the cellular redox status. When cells are in a state of oxidative stress, the metabolism of Fumaric acid and Malic acid may change to maintain intracellular redox balance [<a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>]. The activity of fumarate hydratase is impaired by the excessive production of free radicals induced by oxidative stress, which blocks the conversion of fumarate to malate. Meanwhile, the compensatory enhancement of the urea cycle exacerbates fumarate accumulation. This accumulation not only inhibits α-ketoglutarate-dependent demethylases to promote myocardial fibrosis but also reacts with glutathione to generate sulfides, further aggravating oxidative stress [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. In Stage C HF, myocardial cells are stimulated by various damaging factors, producing large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and disrupting the intracellular redox balance. Compared with Stages A and B, Stage C patients exhibit cumulative mitochondrial damage, and excessive neuroendocrine activation further exacerbates metabolic disorders, ultimately leading to a significant increase in serum organic acid levels [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>].</p>
<p id="Par42">Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that leucine, isoleucine, and valine were significantly associated with the progression of heart failure (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05). Leucine, isoleucine, and valine are branched-chain amino acids. Patients in Stage A have a relatively normal metabolic state: myocardial energy supply depends on fatty acid and glucose oxidation, and BCAAs catabolism is not significantly activated [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>]. In contrast, in Stages B and C, due to structural and functional damage to the heart, mitochondrial dysfunction inhibits the TCA cycle, which compels the myocardium to switch to BCAAs as an alternative energy source. BCAAs undergo catabolism to produce acetyl-CoA, thereby maintaining energy supply [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>]. Meanwhile, elevated systemic inflammatory cells and insulin resistance promote skeletal muscle protein breakdown, releasing BCAAs into the bloodstream. Additionally, although myocardial uptake of BCAAs increases, the metabolic rate is insufficient, leading to the accumulation of BCAAs in the circulation [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>, <a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>]. The lack of significant difference in BCAAs levels between Stage B and Stage C may be related to two factors: first, the persistence of metabolic remodeling in both stages (e.g., TCA cycle inhibition and the BCAAs-dependent energy supply pattern have become stable); second, the limitations of the study design (i.e., a cross-sectional study). Long-term elevation of BCAAs may promote myocardial hypertrophy, inhibit glucose oxidation, and exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction by activating the mTOR pathway, thereby forming a metabolic toxic cycle [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>].</p>
<p id="Par43">When Stage A and Stage B were combined and compared with Stage C, the alanine level in Stage C remained decreased, while the levels of citric acid, fumaric acid, and malic acid still increased; in addition, the creatine level in Stage C was decreased. Creatine plays an important energy buffer role in myocardial cells. Under normal physiological conditions, creatine can react with ATP to form phosphocreatine and ADP. As a high-energy phosphate compound, phosphocreatine can quickly transfer a phosphate group to ADP to generate ATP when the energy demand of myocardial cells increases suddenly, thereby providing energy for myocardial contraction [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>]. The decrease in creatine levels in patients with Stage C HF means a reduction in the energy reserves of myocardial cells. When the heart is under stress or faces increased load, it cannot provide sufficient energy in a timely and effective manner, leading to impaired myocardial contractile function and decreased cardiac output, which further exacerbates the symptoms of HF [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>]. Multivariate regression analysis revealed that citrate, fumarate, and malate were risk factors for heart failure (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05).</p>
<p id="Par44">From the above, it can be concluded that the metabolic differences among HF Stages A, B, and C are mainly enriched in energy metabolism. Therefore, to verify the results of untargeted metabolomics, we further conducted targeted energy metabolomics detection. The results of targeted metabolomics indicated that compared with Stage A, the level of Phosphoenolpyruvate in Stage B was decreased. As a key intermediate product in the glycolytic pathway, changes in the level of Phosphoenolpyruvate are of great significance for the energy metabolism process [<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>]. Under normal physiological conditions, the glycolytic pathway is an important way for myocardial cells to obtain energy under anaerobic or hypoxic conditions. Phosphoenolpyruvate generates pyruvate under the catalysis of pyruvate kinase, and produces ATP at the same time to provide energy for myocardial cells [<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>]. When the level of Phosphoenolpyruvate decreases in Stage B, it may mean that the flux of the glycolytic pathway is affected, leading to reduced pyruvate production, which in turn lowers the amount of ATP generated [<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>]. The results of untargeted metabolomics also showed that compared with Stage A, the serum Alanine level of patients in Stage B was decreased. Although patients in Stage B do not show symptoms of HF, the metabolic status of myocardial cells has already changed at this time, and an imbalance between energy demand and supply occurs. This inhibits the activity of key enzymes in the glycolytic pathway, thereby affecting the production and subsequent metabolism of Phosphoenolpyruvate. This suggests that the energy metabolism of patients in Stage B has been abnormal, and this abnormality may be associated with disease progression [<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>].</p>
<p id="Par45">From the above, it can be concluded that the metabolic differences among HF Stages A, B, and C are mainly enriched in energy metabolism. Therefore, to verify the results of untargeted metabolomics, we further conducted targeted energy metabolomics detection. The results of targeted metabolomics indicated that compared with Stage A, the level of Phosphoenolpyruvate in Stage B was decreased. As a key intermediate product in the glycolytic pathway, changes in the level of Phosphoenolpyruvate are of great significance for the energy metabolism process [<a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>]. Under normal physiological conditions, the glycolytic pathway is an important way for myocardial cells to obtain energy under anaerobic or hypoxic conditions. Phosphoenolpyruvate generates pyruvate under the catalysis of pyruvate kinase, and produces ATP at the same time to provide energy for myocardial cells [<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>]. When the level of Phosphoenolpyruvate decreases in Stage B, it may mean that the flux of the glycolytic pathway is affected, leading to reduced pyruvate production, which in turn lowers the amount of ATP generated [<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>]. The results of untargeted metabolomics also showed that compared with Stage A, the serum Alanine level of patients in Stage B was decreased. Although patients in Stage B do not show symptoms of HF, the metabolic status of myocardial cells has already changed at this time, and an imbalance between energy demand and supply occurs. This inhibits the activity of key enzymes in the glycolytic pathway, thereby affecting the production and subsequent metabolism of Phosphoenolpyruvate. This suggests that the energy metabolism of patients in Stage B has been abnormal, and this abnormality may be associated with disease progression [<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>].</p>
<p id="Par46">After merging the serum targeted metabolomics data of patients in Stage A and Stage B, it was compared with the serum metabolites of patients in Stage C. It was found that the levels of 3-phosphoglycerate, Glucose-6-phosphate, Fructose-6-phosphate, Phosphoenolpyruvate, AMP, ADP, and ATP in patients in Stage C were decreased. This result further emphasizes that there are significant differences in energy metabolism between patients in Stage C and those in Stages A and B. Merging the data of Stage A and Stage B allows for a more comprehensive comparison of energy metabolism changes across different disease stages. The significant decrease in multiple energy metabolites in Stage C patients reconfirms the trend that abnormal myocardial energy metabolism gradually deteriorates as HF progresses.</p>
<p id="Par47">The differences between untargeted and targeted metabolomics suggest a complex regulatory relationship between the TCA cycle and the glycolytic pathway. In untargeted metabolomics, the increased levels of TCA cycle intermediates (citric acid, fumaric acid, malic acid) in Stage C patients may reflect mitochondrial metabolic stress [<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>]. In targeted metabolomics, it was further verified that compared with the combined Stage (A + B), Stage C patients had elevated levels of fumaric acid and malic acid (<em>P</em><sub><em>adjust</em></sub>&lt;0.05, but PLS-DA VIP&lt;1). The significant downregulation of the glycolytic pathway implies that myocardial cells may undergo metabolic reprogramming, shifting energy supply from glycolysis to fatty acid oxidation [<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>]. When the glycolytic pathway is inhibited, myocardial cells may compensate for energy demands by enhancing ketone body utilization and strengthening the TCA cycle. Although energy production pathways are activated, the efficiency of ATP generation decreases due to mitochondrial dysfunction. This phenomenon is consistent with the “hypermetabolism-energy starvation” paradox commonly observed in failing hearts [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>, <a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>], which provides a new perspective for understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms of HF.</p>
<p id="Par48">In summary, Stage A of heart failure represents the energy remodeling homeostatic reserve period. Patients in Stage A only have high-risk factors for HF, with no abnormalities in cardiac structure or function, and energy metabolism maintains physiological homeostasis. At this stage, myocardial energy supply relies primarily on fatty acid oxidation and glucose oxidation. Stage B is the energy remodeling compensatory transition period. Patients in Stage B exhibit structural cardiac changes, with mitochondrial function beginning to show concealed impairment. Energy metabolism enters a compensatory transition phase. Mitochondrial dysfunction inhibits the efficiency of the TCA cycle, impairing the core energy supply pathways of fatty acid oxidation and glucose oxidation. The heart initiates multiple compensatory mechanisms: increased carnitine levels to enhance fatty acid transport and antioxidant capacity, and activation of the glycolytic pathway in an attempt to supplement energy supply [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>]. However, inhibition of key enzyme activity leads to decreased levels of phosphoenolpyruvate and alanine. BCAA metabolism begins to activate, shifting the myocardium toward BCAAs as an alternative energy source, generating acetyl-CoA through catabolism to supplement TCA cycle substrates [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>]. At this stage, systemic inflammation and insulin resistance are not yet significantly exacerbated. BCAAs released from skeletal muscle protein breakdown and their uptake and metabolic rate in the myocardium are essentially balanced, resulting in a mild elevation of circulating BCAA levels [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>]. This metabolic shift represents an adaptive adjustment by the heart in response to energy supply-demand imbalance.</p>
<p id="Par49">Stage C is the Energy remodeling decompensated failure period. Patients in Stage C exhibit typical HF symptoms, with severely impaired mitochondrial function showing cumulative effects and neuroendocrine overactivation. Energy remodeling enters a decompensated failure stage, with synergistic regulation of various metabolic pathways becoming completely disrupted. The TCA cycle exhibits a “hypermetabolism-energy starvation” paradox: mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation impairment leads to the accumulation of intermediates such as citrate, fumarate, and malate. The body attempts to enhance the TCA cycle to compensate for energy demands, but damaged mitochondria cannot effectively convert substrates into ATP. Instead, fumarate accumulation exacerbates oxidative stress and myocardial fibrosis. Exhaustion of cardiac compensatory capacity leads to decreased carnitine levels, obstructed long-chain fatty acid transport, and failure of the β-oxidation process, widening the energy supply gap. In the glycolytic pathway, products such as glucose-6-phosphate and fructose-6-phosphate decrease significantly, impairing glucose transport and metabolism; anaerobic energy supply pathways cannot compensate for the energy deficit. Decreased creatine levels lead to reduced phosphocreatine generation, depleting the heart’s energy reserve. ATP, ADP, and AMP levels decline overall, resulting in decreased cardiac pumping function. Furthermore, circulating BCAA levels (leucine, isoleucine, valine) increase significantly. This is due to the myocardium’s excessive reliance on BCAAs for energy due to TCA cycle inhibition, combined with the effects of systemic inflammation and insulin resistance [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>]. Increased BCAA release from skeletal muscle protein breakdown and decreased BCAA clearance efficiency ultimately lead to circulatory accumulation [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>, <a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>].</p>
<p id="Par50">In conclusion, the findings of this study have significant implications for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of CHF. In terms of diagnosis, energy metabolites (e.g., malic acid) provide a new approach for early diagnosis, as they can detect abnormal changes in myocardial energy metabolism. Combining these metabolites with traditional diagnostic methods can improve diagnostic accuracy. In terms of mechanisms, the results of untargeted metabolomics confirm the important role of energy metabolism in the occurrence and development of HF. Targeted metabolomics further validates these results, identifies abnormalities in multiple energy metabolites, and confirms that HF patients at different stages exhibit varying degrees of energy metabolism imbalance. In terms of treatment, changes in energy metabolomics provide a theoretical basis for new therapeutic strategies. Interventions targeting abnormal energy metabolism are expected to improve myocardial function, and novel biomarkers can be used to monitor treatment efficacy, enabling precise treatment. In terms of prognosis assessment, novel biomarkers are closely associated with patient prognosis—they can predict indicators such as mortality risk and evaluate treatment responses, providing strong support for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of CHF. Limitations of this study: This study adopted a cross-sectional design. The limitations of sample size and the lack of a healthy control group may affect the baseline assessment of metabolic changes. Future studies should use cohort designs to further explore the evolutionary relationship between the aforementioned metabolites and HF. Serum contains a large number of endogenous small-molecule metabolites, which are diverse and have a wide polarity range. The uncertainty in metabolite identification complicates sample pretreatment processes, and the untargeted approach has issues such as biased metabolite coverage. Nevertheless, this study combines the comprehensiveness of untargeted metabolomics with the precision of targeted metabolomics, enabling a more comprehensive capture of metabolic changes across different stages of HF. By focusing on the metabolic evolution from Stage A to Stage C, this study contributes to a better understanding of the metabolic mechanisms underlying HF progression.</p></section><section id="Sec16"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="Par51">Stage C of HF is characterized by energy metabolism collapse (decreased ATP levels and TCA cycle compensation), and differential metabolites (e.g., ATP, Fumaric acid, and Malic acid) may serve as potential candidate biomarkers pending longitudinal validation. Meanwhile, the study found that elevated BCAAs levels are associated with the shift in myocardial energy sources and metabolic toxic cycle, which provides a new perspective for understanding metabolic remodeling in HF.</p></section><section id="Sec17"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h2>
