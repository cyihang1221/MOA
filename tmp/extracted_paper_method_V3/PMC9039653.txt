<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h3>
<p>The functional relevance of intra-species diversity in natural microbial communities remains largely unexplored. The guts of two closely related honey bee species, <em>Apis cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em>, are colonised by a similar set of core bacterial species composed of host-specific strains, thereby providing a good model for an intra-species diversity study.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objectives</h3>
<p>We aim to assess the functional relevance of intra-species diversity of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> gut microbiota.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>Honey bee workers were collected from four regions of China. Their gut microbiomes were investigated by shotgun metagenomic sequencing, and the bacterial compositions were compared at the species level. A cross-species colonisation assay was conducted, with the gut metabolomes being characterised by LC-MS/MS.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>Comparative analysis showed that the strain composition of the core bacterial species was host-specific. These core bacterial species presented distinctive functional profiles between the hosts. However, the overall functional profiles of the <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> gut microbiomes were similar; this was further supported by the consistency of the honey bees’ gut metabolome, as the gut microbiota of different honey bee species showed rather similar metabolic profiles in the cross-species colonisation assay. Moreover, this experiment also demonstrated that the gut microbiota of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> could cross colonise between the two honey bee species.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p>Our findings revealed functional differences in most core gut bacteria between the guts of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em>, which may be associated with their inter-species diversity. However, the functional profiles of the overall gut microbiomes between the two honey bee species converge, probably as a result of the overlapping ecological niches of the two species. Our findings provide critical insights into the evolution and functional roles of the mutualistic microbiota of honey bees and reveal that functional redundancy could stabilise the gene content diversity at the strain-level within the gut community.</p></section></section><section id="s0005"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0005">Animals carry massive and diverse communities of symbiotic microbes in their gastrointestinal tracts <a href="#b0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0005">[1]</a>. Some of these communities play vital roles in host health <a href="#b0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0010">[2]</a>, <a href="#b0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0015">[3]</a>, <a href="#b0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0020">[4]</a>, <a href="#b0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0025">[5]</a>. There has been growing evidence that intra-species level or strain level diversity is omnipresent in the gut microbiota because the same species of bacteria consists of a coherent group of strains <a href="#b0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0030">[6]</a>, <a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">[7]</a>, which may display gene content variation and nucleotide polymorphism <a href="#b0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0040">[8]</a>, <a href="#b0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0045">[9]</a>. However, the functional contribution of intra-species genomic variation to the gut microbiome remains obscure in natural microbial communities. The overall functional profile (see Glossary) of the gut microbiota can change significantly, with only minor changes being displayed in the taxonomic profiles <a href="#b0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0050">[10]</a>, <a href="#b0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0055">[11]</a>, providing indirect evidence for the functional relevance of intra-species diversity <a href="#b0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0060">[12]</a>. Distinct gut microbiomes can also lead to similar microbial genetic profiles. Shan et al. <a href="#b0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0065">[13]</a> found that high-energy diets with different fat-to-sugar ratios can induce different gut microbiota that share similar genetic and metabolite compositions. Contradictory findings have shown that strain-level diversity can lead to a similar or different functional gene profile among the gut microbiomes of different hosts; however, what drives these different outcomes is still unknown. Addressing this question can significantly improve our understanding of the functional relevance of intra-species diversity.</p>
<p id="p0010">Honey bees (<em>Apis</em> spp.) have unique advantages as model organisms for gut microbiota studies <a href="#b0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0070">[14]</a>, particularly in terms of intra-species diversity. Firstly, honey bees harbour a simple and conservative gut community, mainly consisting of 8–10 phylotypes (see Glossary) <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>, <a href="#b0080" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0080">[16]</a>. Secondly, multiple studies have revealed that high strain diversity exists in the major lineages of <em>Apis mellifera</em> guts <a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">[7]</a>, <a href="#b0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0085">[17]</a>, <a href="#b0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0090">[18]</a>, <a href="#b0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0095">[19]</a>, and host-specific intra-species diversity exists among bee species <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>, <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>, <a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">[21]</a>. Finally, the honey bee gut microbiota reveals parallels with the human gut microbiota; they have both presumably coevolved with their host over millions of years and are transmitted primarily through social interactions <a href="#b0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0070">[14]</a>, <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>, <a href="#b0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0110">[22]</a>, <a href="#b0115" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0115">[23]</a>.</p>
<p id="p0015">The Eastern honey bee <em>Apis cerana</em> and the Western honey bee <em>A. mellifera</em> are the only two honey bee species that have been commercially reared for food crop pollination; they have immense economic and ecological value <a href="#b0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0120">[24]</a>, <a href="#b0125" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0125">[25]</a>. Historically, <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> were geographically isolated in Asia and Europe-Africa, respectively. As a result of allopatric speciation, these two honey bee species demonstrate different behavioural and physiological characteristics, as reviewed by Oldroyd et al. <a href="#b0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0130">[26]</a>. <em>A. mellifera</em> was introduced into Asia with the development of the modern beekeeping industry. However, in recent years, the health and populations of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> have been declining in many parts of the world <a href="#b0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0135">[27]</a>, <a href="#b0140" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0140">[28]</a>, <a href="#b0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0145">[29]</a>. While the historically geographical isolation might result in the differentiation of gut microbiota between the two honey bee species, the modern sympatry has provided opportunities for the cross-transmission of gut microbiota between the two species. The comparative study of the gut microbiota of these two bee species would provide important insights into the differentiation and host specificity of gut microbiota and also help understand the function of these gut bacteria in the health of honey bee individuals and their colonies <a href="#b0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0150">[30]</a>, <a href="#b0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0155">[31]</a>, <a href="#b0160" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0160">[32]</a>, <a href="#b0165" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0165">[33]</a>.</p>
<p id="p0020">Recently, Ellegaard et al. <a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">[21]</a> compared the composition of gut bacteria between <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em>. While compositional difference at the strain level was identified for core gut bacteria, it remains unclear whether the bacterial difference would bring about metabolic differences between the two honey bee species and then affect their physiology. Because the two honey bee species have adapted to similar niches and played similar ecological roles, we hypothesised that the gut bacteria of them are similar in overall functional profiles. To test this hypothesis, we employed shotgun metagenomic sequencing to comprehensively analyse the resident gut microbiota of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> colonies in China (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>A). Furthermore, we conducted a cross-species colonisation assay and characterised the metabolomes of workers harbouring different gut microbiota to uncover whether these strain-specific bacteria have host specificity (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>B). Our study confirmed that the core bacterial species of honey bees consist of distinctive strains, which differ significantly in their functional profiles. Interestingly, pathway reconstruction based on the overall gut microbiomes and metabolic analyses suggested that the overall gut communities of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> have similar capabilities. Our results also suggested that there is no host specificity in core gut bacteria between <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em>.</p>

<section id="s0015"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Ethics statement</h3>
<p id="p0025">The owners of the sampled apiaries gave us permissions to collect honey bee samples. No additional permits were required because the study did not involve endangered or protected species.</p></section><section id="s0020"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Field honey bee sample collection</h3>
<p id="p0030">Honey bee workers from 20 <em>A. cerana</em> colonies and 20 <em>A. mellifera</em> colonies were collected from four apiaries located in Kunming, Hangzhou, Beijing, and Guangzhou in June 2019. In each apiary, five <em>A. cerana</em> and five <em>A. mellifera</em> colonies were sampled. To collect foraging workers, colony entrances were blocked, and approximately 100 workers carrying pollen were sampled. After chilling bees at 4 °C to immobilise them, the ileum and rectum (hindgut) of individual bees were dissected using sterile forceps and iris scissors. A total of 50 dissected guts from each colony were pooled together, submerged in 50% glycerol, and stored at −80 °C.</p></section><section id="s0025"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">DNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing</h3>
<p id="p0035">Before DNA extraction, the gut bacteria were enriched according to Ellegaard et al. <a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">[7]</a>, in which the gut tissue was homogenised with a bead-beater using glass beads. The homogenates were centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min to remove debris, and the supernatant was collected into new Eppendorf tubes. The samples were then centrifuged at 10000<em>g</em> for 10 min to pellet the bacterial cells. The supernatant was removed, and the bacterial pellets were resuspended in PBS and centrifuged again at 500<em>g</em> for 5 min. Finally, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000<em>g</em> for 15 min to pellet the bacterial cells.</p>
<p id="p0040">Bacterial DNA was extracted using a CTAB-based DNA extraction protocol. For the DNA sample preparation, 200 ng DNA per sample was used as input material. First the DNA sample was fragmented by sonication to a size of 350 bp. Then, sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, USA), and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Specifically, the Chip DNA was purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, USA). After adenylation of the 3′ ends of DNA fragments, the NEBNext Adaptor with a hairpin loop structure was ligated to prepare for hybridisation. Following this, 3 µL USER Enzyme (NEB, USA) was added to the adaptor-ligated DNA, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min followed by 95 °C for 5 min. PCR was carried out with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, Universal PCR primers, and Index (X) primers provided in NEBNext Multiplex (NEB, USA). Finally, PCR products were purified (AMPure XP system) and library quality was assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq system by Novagene Co., Ltd.</p></section><section id="s0030"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sequence quality control and assembly</h3>
<p id="p0045">To obtain high-quality reads, the raw fastq data from the Illumina NovaSeq platform were trimmed to remove low-quality (30% of bases with Q &lt; 15 per read), ambiguous bases (N &gt; 5% per read) and the overlap with adapter sequences of ≥ 5 bp. For each sample, scaffolds were then assembled using Megahit v1.2 with parameters “- k-list 45,55,67,73”.</p></section><section id="s0035"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metagenome assembly and gene prediction</h3>
<p id="p0050">For scaftigs (see Glossary) &gt;500 bp in each sample, the open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using MetaGeneMark with the MetaGeneMark_v1.mod model. The CD-HIT program <a href="#b0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0170">[34]</a> was used to obtain an initial non-redundant gene catalogue (nrGC) from the predicted ORFs (&gt;100 bp) with parameters “-c 0.95, -aS 0.9”. The abundance of non-redundant genes (unigenes) was measured by realigning the reads to the nrGC using SoapAligner <a href="#b0175" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0175">[35]</a>. Genes with ≤ 2 mapped sequences in all samples were excluded from further analysis. To calculate the relative abundance of a gene, the number of reads that were aligned to the gene was scaled by the gene length and the total number of reads that were mapped to the entire non-redundant gene catalogue for each sample.</p></section><section id="s0040"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Taxonomic and functional assignment of genes</h3>
<p id="p0055">To obtain the taxonomic information of unigenes, DIAMOND v0.9 was used to search the assembly against the NCBI microNR database (including bacteria, fungi, archaea, and viruses, version 13 July 2019) with a blastp, e value ≤ 1 × 10<sup>−5</sup> threshold. The taxonomic information of each gene was determined using the lowest common ancestor-based algorithm (LCA) implemented in MEGAN (version 4, Tübingen, Baden-Württemberg, Germany). Alpha diversity (see Glossary) was calculated using vegan <a href="#b0180" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0180">[36]</a> and picante packages <a href="#b0185" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0185">[37]</a> in the R statistical software <a href="#b0190" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0190">[38]</a>. The taxonomic compositions at the genus and species levels were organised as 0/1 (presence/absence) matrix and were input into the PanGP program <a href="#b0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0195">[39]</a> for core/pan analyses with the default parameter.</p>
<p id="p0060">Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) <a href="#b0200" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0200">[40]</a> annotation and Carbohydrate-Active enzymes Database (CAZy) <a href="#b0205" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0205">[41]</a> of each gene were also performed using DIAMOND against the KEGG database and CAZy database (version: 25 November 2014), respectively. The relative abundance of the annotated functional features was calculated by summing the relative abundances of the genes annotated to the features.</p></section><section id="s0045"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Strain-level nucleotide diversity analysis</h3>
<p id="p0065">The strains of <em>Bartonella apis</em> BBC0122 (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/NZ_CP015625.1" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NZ_CP015625.1</a>) <a href="#b0210" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0210">[42]</a>, <em>Bifidobacterium asteroides</em> PRL2011 (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/NC_018720.1" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NC_018720.1</a>) <a href="#b0215" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0215">[43]</a>, <em>Frischella perrara</em> PEB0191 (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/NZ_CP009056.1" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NZ_CP009056.1</a>) <a href="#b0220" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0220">[44]</a>, <em>Gilliamella apicola</em> wkB1 (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/NZ_CP007445.1" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NZ_CP007445.1</a>) <a href="#b0225" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0225">[45]</a>, and <em>Snodgrassella alvi</em> wkB2 (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/NZ_CP007446.1" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NZ_CP007446.1</a>) <a href="#b0225" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0225">[45]</a> were selected as representatives of the five core species according to Ellegaard et al. <a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">[7]</a>. Their complete genomes were employed as references for mapping the raw reads of the five bacterial species in our study. The reference genes, which were defined in this study as the genes conserved among the five representative genomes, were identified by pairwise BLAST searches (blastp, e value ≤ 1 × 10<sup>−20</sup>) (see additional file 8 at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/SEMYG). Bowtie2 was used for mapping the raw reads against the nucleotide sequences of these reference genes <a href="#b0230" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0230">[46]</a>. Based on the alignment results, single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) were identified using Samtools, mpileup, and Vcftools <a href="#b0235" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0235">[47]</a>. The SNPs located within the reference genes were used as the input for principal component analysis (PCA) by using PLINK v2.0 with default parameters <a href="#b0240" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0240">[48]</a>. Furthermore, the dominant allele sequences of these reference genes for each sample were concatenated for their use as inputs for the subsequent evolutionary analyses. MEGA v10.0.5, was used to construct the neighbour-joining trees, with the Jukes-Cantor (JC) substitution model and 100 bootstrap replicates <a href="#b0245" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0245">[49]</a>. The pairwise nucleotide distances were also calculated by using MEGA with the JC model.</p></section><section id="s0050"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Strain-level gene content analysis</h3>
<p id="p0070">For each of the five core bacterial species, the ORFs assigned to each species were extracted according to the taxonomic assignment. Within each species, the homologous relationship among the predicted ORFs was determined by comparing the protein sequences of the ORFs with the blastclust program in the NCBI BLAST package; the parameters 0.5 length coverage (-L) and 80% protein identity (-S) were applied. After binning the ORFs into gene clusters, the abundance of each gene cluster was obtained by summing the abundances of all ORFs belonging to the gene cluster. A gene cluster with an abundance &gt; 0 was considered to be present in the sample; otherwise, it was considered absent. The pairwise similarity of gene content between samples was defined as the number of shared gene clusters divided by the number of identified gene clusters for the compared samples.</p></section><section id="s0055"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Strain-level functional analysis</h3>
<p id="p0075">For each of the five core bacterial species, the ORFs assigned to each species were extracted according to the taxonomic assignment. Subsequently, the ORFs were binned into functional units according to their functional annotations. The abundance of each functional unit was obtained by summing the abundances of ORFs belonging to the functional unit. Finally, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed on the abundances of functional units among the samples.</p></section></section><section id="s0060"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Cross-species colonisation assays</h2>
<p id="p0080"><em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> gut bacteria were prepared as follows: 10 honey bee forager workers were sampled near the entrance of a colony of each honey bee species in Hangzhou. Their hindguts were dissected immediately and homogenised in 1 mL PBS. The gut homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was removed to eliminate possible virus contamination. To prepare pollen containing gut bacteria, the isolated bacteria were resuspended in 1 mL PBS, and 100 μL suspension was mixed with sterilised pollen.</p>
<p id="p0085">Germ-free <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> workers were obtained using the protocol described by Zheng et al. <a href="#b0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0155">[31]</a>. The germ-free workers were divided into four groups for the cross-species colonisation assays: 1) Acc: <em>A. cerana</em> workers inoculated with <em>A. cerana</em> gut microbiota; 2) Amc: <em>A. mellifera</em> workers inoculated with <em>A. cerana</em> gut microbiota; 3) Amm: <em>A. mellifera</em> workers inoculated with <em>A. mellifera</em> gut microbiota; and 4) Acm: <em>A. cerana</em> workers inoculated with <em>A. mellifera</em> gut microbiota. Each group consisted of 60 workers placed in a single cage. Different groups of workers were supplied with pollen containing respective gut bacteria described for 5 days and then switched to sterilised pollen. Approximately 1 × 10<sup>5</sup> bacterial cells were fed to each honey bee worker. In addition, another cage of germ-free <em>A. cerana</em> and germ-free <em>A. mellifera</em> workers with no inoculation of gut bacteria were used as a negative control to confirm that honey bees were not contaminated by bacteria from other sources, and contamination was not observed. Honey bees were housed according to standard methods <a href="#b0250" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0250">[50]</a>. On day ten, from each cage, forty workers were sampled and pooled for metagenome sequencing as described above, ten workers were sampled and pooled for untargeted metabolomics analysis, and five workers were sampled and used for 16s rDNA quantification individually. The experiment was conducted in triplicate using three <em>A. cerana</em> and three <em>A. mellifera</em> colonies.</p>
<section id="s0065"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Quantification of the absolute bacterial loads of cross-species colonisation honey bee samples</h3>
<p id="p0090">The absolute bacterial loads of Acc, Acm, Amm, and Amc workers were determined by qPCR using universal bacterial 16S rRNA primers (F: 5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′, R: 5′-CTGCTGCCTCCCGTAGGAGT-3′) <a href="#b0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0110">[22]</a>. Workers were frozen at −20 °C until immobilised, and their guts were immediately dissected in RNase-free water and used for DNA extraction. DNA was extracted using a TIANamp Stool DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. 16 s rRNA copy numbers were quantified using the StepOne Plus real-time PCR system with thermal cycling conditions as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s, followed by a melting curve from 60 °C to 95 °C at 0.5 °C/5 s increments.</p></section><section id="s0070"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolite extraction</h3>
<p id="p0095">The replicates of each group were shipped on dry ice to Novogene Corporation (Beijing, China) for metabolomic analysis. Pooled honey bee hindguts were ground with liquid nitrogen, and the homogenate was resuspended with pre-chilled 80% methanol and 0.1% formic acid. The suspensions were cooled on ice for 5 min and then centrifuged at 15,000<em>g</em> at 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant was diluted to a final concentration containing 53% methanol using LC-MS grade water. The solution was transferred to a fresh Eppendorf tube and centrifuged at 15,000<em>g</em> at 4 °C for 10 min to obtain the supernatant for analysis.</p>
<p id="p0100">LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States) coupled with an Orbitrap Q Exactive series mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States). Samples were injected onto a Hyperil Gold column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) using a 16-min linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The eluents for the positive polarity mode were eluent A (0.1% FA in water) and eluent B (methanol). The eluents for the negative polarity mode were eluent A (5 mM ammonium acetate, pH 9.0) and eluent B (methanol). The gradient elution was set as follows: 1.5 min, 2% B; 12.0 min, 2–100% B; 14.0 min, 100% B; 14.1 min, 100–2% B; 17 min, 2% B. The Q Exactive series mass spectrometer was operated in positive/negative polarity mode with a spray voltage of 3.2 kV, capillary temperature of 320 °C, sheath gas flow rate of 35 arb, and aux gas flow rate of 10 arb.</p></section><section id="s0075"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Untargeted metabolomics data analysis</h3>
<p id="p0105">Compound Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States) was used to perform peak alignment, peak picking, and quantitation for each metabolite, with the following settings: retention time tolerance, 0.2 min; actual mass tolerance, 5 ppm; signal intensity tolerance, 30%; signal/noise ratio, 3; and minimum intensity, 100,000. The peak intensities were then normalised to the total spectral intensity. The normalised data were used to predict the molecular formula based on the additive ions, molecular ion peaks, and fragment ions. Peaks were then matched with the mzCloud, mzVault, and MassList databases to obtain accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results. The area normalisation method was applied to the data that were not normally distributed. The metabolites were annotated using the HMDB database (<a href="http://www.hmdb.ca/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.hmdb.ca/</a>), Lipidmaps database (<a href="http://www.lipidmaps.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.lipidmaps.org/</a>), and KEGG database (<a href="http://www.genome.jp/kegg/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.genome.jp/kegg/</a>). PCA and hierarchical clustering were performed using MetaboAnalyst <a href="#b0255" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0255">[51]</a>. Statistical significance (P-value) was calculated using univariate analysis (<em>t</em>-test).</p></section><section id="s0080"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="p0110">Statistical analysis was performed using the R software v3.6.1 <a href="#b0190" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0190">[38]</a>. The significant taxonomic and functional differences between <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> samples were determined by Student’s t-tests. For comparisons of the pairwise distance of SNP, gene content similarity, and 16 s rDNA copy number, Kruskal-Wallis tests were used. All P values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, and adjusted P values &lt; 0.05, were considered as statistically significant <a href="#b0260" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0260">[52]</a>. Hierarchical clustering was conducted using the hclust function in the stats package. PCA was performed using the prcomp function and PCoA based on the Bray-Curtis distance was performed using the ape library <a href="#b0265" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0265">[53]</a>. The Adonis test was used to distinguish group similarity, and the statistic R<sup>2</sup> represents effect size, which reveals the percentage of variation in distances explained by the grouping being tested. The Mantel test was used to test for differences across geographical populations. Metabolites with VIP &gt; 1, P value &lt; 0.05, and fold change ≥ 2 or fold change ≤ 0.5, were considered to be differential metabolites. The differential abundance of taxonomy and functional category was tested using Metastat <a href="#b0270" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0270">[54]</a>.</p></section><section id="s0085"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Availability of data and material</h3>
<p id="p0115">The raw sequence data from metagenome shotgun sequencing were submitted to NCBI SRA under BioProject accession numbers PRJNA599289 (field honey bees) and PRJNA599288 (cross-species colonisation assay). All data generated or analysed during this study are included in this published article and its supplementary information files at OSF, https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/SEMYG.</p></section></section><section id="s0090"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="s0095"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Characterisation of the <em>Apis cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> gut community</h3>
<p id="p0120">High-throughput sequencing metagenomics and the <em>de novo</em> assembly of paired-end reads resulted in 1,765,642 scaffolds with an average length of 1.36 kb. Detailed quality metrics, including the number of reads, reads mapped to contigs, reads that were successfully annotated, and reads that were mapped to honey bee genomes are listed in supplementary file Table S1 in the Open Science Framework, https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/SEMYG. Rarefaction analysis of our samples revealed a curve approaching saturation (Fig. S1), demonstrating that the vast majority of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> gut microbial genes were present in our gene catalogues.</p>
<p id="p0125">Consistent with previous studies <a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">[7]</a>, <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>, <a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">[21]</a>, the overall community profile showed that <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> guts were both dominated by six genera in our samples, namely, <em>Lactobacillus</em>, <em>Bifidobacterium</em>, <em>Snodgrassella</em>, <em>Bartonella</em>, <em>Gilliamella,</em> and <em>Frischella</em>. The guts of the <em>A. cerana</em> workers also harboured a significant amount of the genus <em>Apibacter</em>, whereas the <em>A. mellifera</em> gut was colonised by the genus <em>Commensalibacter</em> (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>A).</p>

Of the six core bacterial genera, <em>Bifidobacterium asteroides</em>, <em>Snodgrassella alvi</em>, <em>Bartonella apis</em>, <em>Gilliamella apicola,</em> and <em>Frischella perrara</em> were the dominant species identified in both <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> (Fig. S3), and we referred to these five bacterial species as the core bacterial species of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> throughout our study. Contrasting this, we found that the genus <em>Lactobacillus</em> was composed of multiple species, which varied significantly in the gut biota of the two honey bee species (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>B). PCA and PCoA of the bacterial communities at the species level showed that the gut communities of sympatric <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> were distinguishable at the species level (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>C, S4, Adonis test: P = 0.005). Furthermore, samples within each honey bee species were clustered together based on their geographic locations (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>C, Mantel test: P = 0.02). Interestingly, when we reanalysed the metagenome data of <em>A. mellifera</em> from the USA, which was published by Engel et al. <a href="#b0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0020">[4]</a>, this sample was grouped within <em>A. mellifera</em> samples from Guangzhou (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>C), suggesting that the geographical separation of the samples might not be solely determined by the geographical distance between the sampling locations.</p></section><section id="s0100"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Strain-level diversity of <em>Apis cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> core bacteria species</h3>
<p id="p0135">The intra-specific diversity of the <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> core bacterial species was demonstrated through the single nucleotide polymorphisms present in the <em>B. apis</em>, <em>B. asteroides</em>, <em>F. perrara</em>, <em>G. apicola</em>, and <em>S. alvi</em>, of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> species living in the same habitat in China.</p>
<p id="p0140">PCA and PCoA analyses based on the SNP patterns revealed that different hosts carry diverse strains. Specifically, <em>B. asteroides</em>, <em>S. alvi</em>, <em>G. apicola</em>, and <em>F. perrara</em> strains in <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> colonies were distinctly host-specific, and <em>B. apis</em> strains were also largely host-specific (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>A, S4, Adonis test: all P = 0.001). However, the geographical region failed to correlate with any notable difference between these five core bacterial species (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>A, Mantel test: all P &gt; 0.05). We also constructed a phylogenetic tree using the dominant alleles as representative sequences for each sample and found that the dominant strains of each <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> samples formed separate branches (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>B). The only exception was <em>B. apis</em>; the predominant strains of four <em>A. mellifera</em> samples (from three different sampling locations) and <em>A. cerana</em> samples were twined together (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>B). We then compared their SNP similarity and stratified them into intra- and inter-host comparisons (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>C). We found that the intra-host comparisons possessed more similar strains within <em>A. cerana</em> as compared to <em>A. mellifera</em>, which demonstrated that the diversity of the gut microbiota was higher in <em>A. mellifera</em> at the strain level, but with the exception of <em>G. apicola</em>. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>, <a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">[21]</a>.</p>

</h3>
<p id="p0145">The aforementioned strain-level distinction between <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> was also reflected in its accessory genome variation. First, we compared the gene content similarity, which is the percentage of shared genes between two genomes, of these five bacterial species within and between these two honey bee species. We noticed that the inter-species gene content similarities were significantly smaller than intra-species comparisons, revealing a significant differentiation between the accessory genomes of the <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> strains.</p>
<p id="p0150">We hypothesised that, at the strain-level, gene content differences would bring about functional differences. In accordance with a previous study <a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">[21]</a>, the KEGG profiles of these host-specific strains, based on the functional assignments and relative gene abundances, displayed a difference in the functions of these host-specific strains. The PCA and PCoA assessments of the KEGG orthologs (KOs) revealed a clear separation between the core bacterial species of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>B, S5, Adonis test: all P = 0.001). When we further grouped the genes into KEGG pathways, most of them revealed a significant difference between the strains hosted by <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> (Fig. S6). We also analysed the genes related to carbohydrate digestion using the CAZy database. In line with KOs, the carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) profiles of <em>B. asteroides</em>, <em>S. alvi</em>, <em>G. apicola</em>, and <em>F. perrara</em> also diverged between the host groups (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>C, S5, Adonis test: all P = 0.001). Detailed lists of KOs and CAZymes are provided in Supplementary Dataset 2.</p>

<p id="p0160">The identification of clear compositional and functional differences between <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> core bacteria at the strain level led us to speculate whether the overall gut metagenome of these two honey bee species also differed in function. Interestingly, analyses of the functional profile of the overall gut metagenome of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> revealed that the percentage of significantly different KOs was notably higher among most individual core bacteria than in the overall microbial ecosystem, except for <em>B. apis</em> (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>A). While the KO profiles of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> were still significantly different (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>B, S7, Adonis test: all P = 0.001), we noticed that the R<sup>2</sup> value of the overall gut microbiomes in <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> was much smaller than that of most of the core bacterial species (R<sup>2</sup><sub>overall gut community</sub> = 0.18, R<sup>2</sup><em><sub>B. apis</sub></em> = 0.08, R<sup>2</sup><em><sub>B. asteroides</sub></em> = 0.77, R<sup>2</sup><em><sub>F. perrara</sub></em> = 0.85, R<sup>2</sup><em><sub>G. apicola</sub></em> = 0.51, and R<sup>2</sup><em><sub>S. alvi</sub></em> = 0.71).</p>

<p id="p0170">We then attempted to reconstruct the shift from a functional distinction at the species level to functional convergence at the microbiota level. First, we summed all the significantly different KOs in each core bacteria and analysed their changes in the overall microbiome, and nearly half of these KOs (1354/2989) no longer had a significant influence at the microbiota level (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>D). We then binned the species based on the profiles of these KOs and assessed their contributions to diversity. As shown in <a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>E, 5F, these KOs were contributed by varied combinations of multiple community members in <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em>.</p></section><section id="s0115"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolite profiles of workers harbouring diverse gut microbiota</h3>
<p id="p0175">It is challenging to predict the metabolic output from only shotgun metagenomic sequencing data. Therefore, we characterised the robust metabolic profiles of workers harbouring different gut microbiota. To eliminate the possible influences and disturbances of the metabolites from host species, we attempted to colonise germ-free <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> workers with complete <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> gut microbiota (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>B), with the aim of obtaining honey bees that were harbouring gut bacteria from another honey bee species.</p>
<p id="p0180">Initially, we analysed the absolute and relative bacterial compositions and found no significant differences between Acc and Amc or between Amm and Acm (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>A, 6B, S9). In addition, alpha diversity analysis revealed that host species had no significant influence on microbiome diversity based on Chao 1 and Shannon indices (Fig. S10). These results suggested that <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> gut bacteria can successfully colonise both <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> workers.</p>

PCA and hierarchical clustering revealed that the samples were mainly separated according to their host species rather than the gut microbiota they harboured (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>C, S11). <p id="p0190">Bacterial evolution is shaped not only by mutations but also by horizontal gene transfer <a href="#b0290" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0290">[58]</a>. <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> were geographically isolated for millions of years <a href="#b0295" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0295">[59]</a>, which limited possible interaction between their gut bacteria and enabled the development of specialised gut microbiota that coevolved with their hosts. Based on targeted amplicon sequencing, host-specific strains of <em>Lactobacillus</em> Firm-5 and <em>S. alvi</em> were identified in the guts of <em>A. mellifera</em> and <em>A. cerana</em>. Ellegaard et al. <a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">[21]</a> found that the core phylotypes colonising <em>A. mellifera</em> and <em>A. cerana</em> are distinct at the sequence-discrete population level. Consistent with their findings, our results also showed that the core bacterial species of <em>A. mellifera</em> and <em>A. cerana</em> in China were composed of distinct strains.</p>
<p id="p0195">Previous genomic sequencing of several isolated strains of honey bee gut microbiota has demonstrated an extensive sequence divergence and gene content variation <a href="#b0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0085">[17]</a>, <a href="#b0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0090">[18]</a>. In addition, metagenomic comparison using <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> in Japan also revealed that <em>A. mellifera</em> displayed a more diverse gene content in the microbiota than <em>A. cerana</em>
<a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">[21]</a>. Thus, it was expected that these distinctive strains of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> in the present study possessed different functional gene profiles. Interestingly, we found that the functional profiles of the overall gut microbiomes of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> were rather similar, which was further supported by our metabolic analysis. The animal gut is populated by complex assemblages of microorganisms, and functional redundancy (see Glossary) is a common aspect of many microbial systems <a href="#b0300" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0300">[60]</a>, <a href="#b0305" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0305">[61]</a>, <a href="#b0310" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0310">[62]</a>. This functional redundancy of the gut microbiome has been mentioned by Vatanen et al. <a href="#b0315" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0315">[63]</a>, in which most gene ontology terms displayed high within-sample functional diversity in the human gut microbiome. Comparisons between the metagenomes of <em>A. mellifera</em> workers revealed age-related changes in the distribution of the core gene families, despite the variability in strain composition among honey bee individuals of the same age, which indicated functional redundancy across strains <a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">[7]</a>. In this study, we found that functional redundancy existed across the gut bacteria species in both <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em>. Most functions of the honey bee gut microbiota were fulfilled by not only one but multiple bacterial species, and the contributions of these bacteria to KOs differed between <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em>. For example, we found that <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/K00104" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">K00104</a> (glycolate oxidase) was mainly encoded by <em>G. apicola</em> in <em>A. cerana</em>, whereas <em>Lactobacillus</em> spp. were the main contributor in <em>A. mellifera</em>. Moreover, these non-core bacteria also contribute significantly to the functional redundancy of the honey bee gut community. Another example is of <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/K00101" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">K00101</a> (L-lactate dehydrogenase (cytochrome)), which is mainly encoded by <em>S. alvi</em> and <em>B. apis</em> in <em>A. cerana</em>, but by <em>B. apis</em> and non-core bacteria in <em>A. mellifera</em>. Eventually, functional redundancy, which can confer resilience and stabilise ecosystem functionality, contributes to the generation of similar functional profiles between these two honey bee species. In contrast to environmental ecosystems, the evolution of the gut microbiome is not only driven from the bottom up by interactions between microbes, but the host is also under strong natural selection to shape the microbiota from the top down and foster a community that is beneficial to it <a href="#b0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0005">[1]</a>. We speculate that the similarity between the <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> microbiomes is largely due to the similarities between these two species. Although the Eastern and Western honey bees have been geographically isolated and have developed several different behavioural traits <a href="#b0320" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0320">[64]</a>, they still share some ecological and physiological aspects in common, particularly those involving certain important factors that shape gut community function, for example, a similar diet <a href="#b0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0130">[26]</a>. Therefore, these two honey bee species have provided a stable environment and similar selective pressure on their gut microbiota, which has eventually shaped a similar metabolic potential.</p>
<p id="p0200">The colonisation ability of exogenous microbes in a host environment has been extensively studied, revealing that the host genotype exerts different selective pressures on exogenous colonisers <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>, <a href="#b0325" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0325">[65]</a>, <a href="#b0330" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0330">[66]</a>, <a href="#b0335" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0335">[67]</a>. Interestingly, our cross-species gut bacteria transplantation assay results suggested that there is no host specificity in core gut bacteria between <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em>, since <em>A. cerana</em> core bacteria could successfully colonise <em>A. mellifera</em> and vice versa, and following colonisation, the gut microbial community structure was not dramatically changed by the host. In fact, we discovered that certain strains of <em>B. apis</em> from <em>A. mellifera</em> were more similar to <em>A. cerana</em> strains in our field honey bee samples, indicating a certain degree of putative host switches in the natural environment.</p>
<p id="p0205">The above findings raise an interesting question: if there is no host specificity, how do <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> maintain a distinctive gut community while their colonies are located in the same habitat? This is of particular interest when considering the fact that <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> honey bees for this study were sampled from the same apiaries, in which they shared overlapping ranges of activity. One possible explanation is that the sociality of corbiculate bees provides a semi-closed and stable system for gut bacterial transmission between generations <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>. Honey bee workers acquire their gut microbiota within hours after emergence through the faecal-oral route and fully establish a stable gut community within 5–6 days before they start to forage outside the hive <a href="#b0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0110">[22]</a>. Moreover, it has been suggested that priority effects in the early life assembly of the gut microbiota contribute to individualised gut community profiles of <em>A. mellifera</em> workers <a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">[7]</a>. Co-inoculation trials using <em>S. alvi</em> strains revealed that strains of <em>A. cerana</em> were able to simultaneously colonise <em>A. mellifera</em> workers alongside a native strain, albeit with less efficiency <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>, suggesting that microbe competition may be essential to the assemblage of honey bee gut microbiota. In addition, differences in gene function profiles between the strains revealed in our study indicated differences in adaptability. Thus, we hypothesise that the highly conserved social transmission route between generations within the honey bee colony, along with the priority effects and the possible competition between native and foreign strains, have ensured the existence of a unique and stable gut community of <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em>. However, our knowledge concerning the transmission of gut bacteria in honey bees and the competition between different strains is still quite limited. Thus, future studies are required to determine the mechanisms involved in obtaining, assembling, and stabilising diverse honey bee microbial communities.</p></section><section id="s0125"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0210">In conclusion, through our systematic metagenomic comparison between <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> gut microbiomes, we found that the distinctive <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> gut bacterial strains possess different functions, except in <em>B. apis</em>. However, our analysis of the overall metagenomes and the metabolomes emphasises the similarity in functional capabilities of the <em>A. cerana</em> and <em>A. mellifera</em> gut microbiota, due to the functional redundancy of honey bee gut bacteria. These findings not only reveal the functional importance of strain-level diversity in host-associated bacterial communities, but also provide fundamental insights into the functional contribution of intra-species diversity to the overall gut community.</p></section><section id="sec31"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Declaration of Competing Interest</h2>

<p id="p0225">
<strong>Alpha diversity</strong>
</p>
<p id="p0230">The diversity within a particular area or ecosystem. There are several metrics for measurement of alpha diversity, such as Observed number of species, Chao1, ACE, Shannon and Simpson.</p>
<p id="p0235">
<strong>Functional profile</strong>
</p>
<p id="p0240">The profile of the function of a certain bacteria or microbiome. In this study, the functional profile was inferred by gene annotation of the Carbohydrate-Active EnZymes (CAZy) database or Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.</p>
<p id="p0245">
<strong>Functional redundancy</strong>
</p>
<p id="p0250">The ecological phenomena that multiple species representing a variety of taxonomic groups can share similar, if not identical, roles in ecosystem functionality.</p>
<p id="p0255">
<strong>Phylotype</strong>
</p>
<p id="p0260">A biological type that classifies an organism by its phylogenetic relationship to other organisms. In prokaryotic microbiology, phylotypes, often referred to as Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs), are a proxy for species.</p>
<p id="p0265">
<strong>Scaftigs</strong>
</p>
<p id="p0270">Continuous sequences within scaffolds.</p></section></section><section id="fn-group1" class="fn-group"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Footnotes</h2>
