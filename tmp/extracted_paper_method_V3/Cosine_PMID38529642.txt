LC–MS/MS Conditions   Agilent HPLC-Agilent 6546 Q-TOF   Prior to analysis, the instrument was calibrated in the low mass range mode, and the isolation window was set to narrow. The standard mix was acquired in triplicate using an Agilent, ZORBAX Extend-C18, 2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 μm column with a flow rate of 100 μL/min. Mobile phases A and B were water + 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. The gradient started from 5% B for 3 min, 90% B at 23 min, held at 90% B for 3 min, and back to 5%B at 28 min. Let the column equilibrate for 7 min, and the total run time was 35 min. The data acquisition was collected in auto MS/MS mode at 0, 10, 20, and 40 CE. Agilent HPLC-Agilent 6520 Q-TOF   Prior to the analysis, the instrument was calibrated in the low mass range mode and the isolation window was set to narrow. Each standard was infused directly into the instrument without column with 50% acetonitrile–water at the flowrate of 100 μL/min for 3 min. The data acquisition was collected in auto MS/MS mode at 0, 10, 20, and 40 CE. Agilent UHPLC-Bruker Impact II   Ten mM sodium formate (10 mM) was infused with a syringe pump at a flow rate of 180 μL/h and let the signal stabilize for 10 min, then calibrated the instrument. The standard mix was acquired in triplicate using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm column, and a flow rate of 150 μL/min. Mobile phases A and B were water + 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. The gradient started from 1% B for 2 min, ramped up 99% B for 10 min, held at 99% B for 3 min, back to 1% B for 0.1 min, and held at 1% B for 2.9 min. The data acquisition was collected in auto MS/MS mode at 0, 10, 20, and 40 eV of CE. Waters ACQUITY UPLC-Waters Synapt G2-Si   After the instrument was turned on and the signal was allowed to stabilize for 10 min, the Synapt G2-Si MS was calibrated with sodium formate (Reservoir C). The standard mix was acquired in triplicate using Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm column, flow rate 400 μL/min. Mobile phase A and B were water + 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. The gradient began with 1% B for 2 min, 99% B at 11 min, held for 3 min, and back to 1% B at 15 min. Let equilibrate for 2 min, and the total run time was 17 min. The data acquisition was performed in FastDDA mode at 0, 10, 20, and 40 CE. The leu-enk LockMass was infused with a flow rate of 10 μL/min throughout the data acquisition as an internal reference. Thermo Scientific Easy Nano LC-Thermo Scientific Q Extractive Plus   The instrument was calibrated weekly. The standard mix was acquired in triplicate using the in-house-made column, flow rate 300 nL/min. Mobile phases A and B were water + 0.1% formic acid and 80% acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. The gradient started from 1% B for 5 min, ramped up 100% B for 55 min and held at 100% B for 10 min. The data acquisition was collected in full MS data-dependent MS/MS mode at 10, 20, and 40 eV of CE. Eksigent (Sciex) Ekspert NanoLC 425-Sciex TripleTOF 5600   Mobile phases A and B were 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile, respectively (Honeywell, Burdick & Jackson). A 3 μL volume of the sample, or the beta galactosidase digest for calibration, was loaded onto the trap column at 2 μL/min flow rate, preceded and followed by approximately 2 min of wash with mobile phase A. For the samples, the elution gradient at 270 nL/min flow rate began at 1% mobile phase B, held for 1 min, ramped up to 99% over 70 min, held for 5 min, and ramped back down over 3 min. For calibration runs, the ramp ran from 5% to 35% over 15 min and washed at 80% for 9 min. For each sample, data were acquired 3 times in data-dependent mode at 0, 10, 20, and 40 eV of CE. Results and Discussion   Cosine Scoring across Collision Energies and Platforms   Cosine scoring ( 9 − 11 , 18 − 20 ) is a prevalent scoring algorithm for comparing MS/MS data, which calculates the cosine of the angle between two vectors—here, representing experimental and reference mass spectra—to assess similarity. Scores range from 0 (no similarity) to 1 (very high similarity), with a higher score indicating closer alignment and, by implication, greater spectral similarity. However, given the sensitivity of MS/MS, even subtle misalignments can substantially impact molecular identification. Consequently, despite the analytical value of cosine scoring, visual verification against known standards remains imperative to mitigate the risk of false positives.   MS/MS data generation, while consistent in its fundamental principles, can exhibit variability across different instrumentation. This variability stems from distinctive features of each platform, including ion source design, mass analyzer configurations, and detector sensitivities, which collectively influence the resulting spectra. 1 − 4  The central challenge lies in determining whether observed spectral differences are intrinsic to the specific instrument or reflective of the sample’s properties 6 − 17  this is where cosine scoring proves useful. By offering a quantifiable measure of spectral comparison, it enables researchers to systematically investigate and attribute the sources of disparity in the data.   In this study, we implemented cosine scoring as well as the number of fragments observed to analyze the compatibility of data across different instrument platforms. Each data set was compared to a collection of reference spectra from METLIN generated on an Agilent QTOF, allowing for a standardized benchmark of similarity. In this process,  m / z  ratio serves as a separate dimension, with the intensity of each  m / z  ratio populating the value within that dimension. The cosine similarity is then derived from the dot product of the experimental and reference spectra vectors; this method is extensively utilized in metabolomics to match compounds with established spectral libraries, facilitating compound identification.   We collected data from over 100 standard compounds using five different instrument manufacturers’ platforms: Bruker QTOF, Sciex QTOF, Waters QTOF, Agilent QTOF, and Thermo QE with each set of results cosine scored against the data generated on an Agilent QTOF instrument. The analysis was designed to provide comparability of the platforms and the reliability of cosine scoring as a tool for cross-platform spectral analysis. The Agilent QTOF was used as a reference since the METLIN database (currently containing experimental MS/MS data on 931,401 molecular standards) has been built using this instrument thus facilitating direct comparison of the data.   In the comparative analysis of MS/MS data across the instruments ( Figure  1 A–D), there were two examples of MS/MS data at high and low similarity scores ( Figure  1 C,D). Cosine similarity scores were utilized as a metric to gauge the agreement of spectral data with reference spectra obtained from an Agilent QTOF instrument. These scores were plotted against collision energy values (0, 10, 20, and 40 eV) to determine the influence of collision-induced dissociation on spectral congruence.   Figure 1. The data reveal a subtle relationship between the number of fragments and the average cosine score across the instruments tested. It was observed that the average cosine scores did not necessarily increase with a greater number of fragments, and instead, the scores plateaued or slightly decreased beyond a certain number of fragments, which may indicate an optimal range of fragmentation for maximizing the spectral similarity.   Moreover, this plateau suggests that very little fragmentation is necessary to achieve informative spectra; beyond this point, additional fragments may not contribute to an increased match quality with the reference spectrum. It is possible that when the number of fragments becomes too high, the resulting spectral complexity could lead to discrepancies in the matching process, potentially due to the increased likelihood of noise or the presence of nondiagnostic fragments.   This analysis also underscores the importance of fragment ion optimization in MS to ensure that high-quality, comparable spectra are obtained. The average cosine scores across different instruments imply a degree of consistency in spectral quality, with variations likely reflecting the inherent characteristics and performance of each mass spectrometer for particular molecules. This indicates that even with a low number of fragments, the MS/MS spectra are sufficiently distinctive. The higher collision energies of 20 and 40 eV also provided high cosine scores across all instruments examined. A result further suggests that the number of fragments is not necessarily important for the identification process.   Table 1.
