</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ABS1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="P33">Molecular networking has become a key method to visualize and annotate the chemical space in non-targeted mass spectrometry data. We present Feature-Based Molecular Networking (FBMN) as an analysis method in the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) infrastructure that builds on chromatographic feature detection and alignment tools. The FBMN method brings quantitative analyses, isomeric resolution, including from ion-mobility spectrometry, into molecular networks.</p></section><hr class="headless">
<p id="P34">Introduced in 2012<sup><a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">1</a></sup>, molecular networking has become an essential bioinformatics tool to visualize and annotate non-targeted mass spectrometry data<sup><a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">2</a></sup>. The first application of molecular networking was described by Traxler and Kolter<sup><a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a></sup> as holding “<em>great promise in providing the next quantum leap in understanding the fascinating world of microbial chemical ecology</em>”. Molecular networking goes beyond spectral matching against reference spectra, by aligning experimental spectra against one another and connecting related molecules by their spectral similarity. In a molecular network, related molecules are referred to as a “molecular family”, differing by simple transformations such as glycosylation, alkylation, and oxidation/reduction. Molecular networking became publicly accessible in 2013 through the initial release of the Global Natural Product Social Molecular Networking (GNPS), a web-enabled mass spectrometry knowledge capture and analysis platform (<a href="http://gnps.ucsd.edu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://gnps.ucsd.edu</a>)<sup><a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a></sup>, and has been widely applied in mass spectrometry-based metabolomics to aid in the annotation of molecular families from their fragmentation spectra (MS<sup>2</sup>).</p>
<p id="P35">Powered by 3,000+ CPU cores at the Center for Computational Mass Spectrometry at the University of California San Diego and the MassIVE data repository, GNPS has provided researchers from more than 150 countries with the ability to perform molecular networking. To build upon the success of the first molecular networking method (referred to as “classical” molecular networking, classical MN) which is based on the MS-Cluster algorithm<sup><a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a></sup>, we introduce a complementary tool named Feature-Based Molecular Networking (FBMN). FBMN leverages the capability of well-established mass spectrometry processing software and improves upon classical MN by incorporating MS<sup>1</sup> information, such as isotope patterns and retention time, but also ion-mobility separation when performed. By relying on processed spectral information, molecular networks obtained with FBMN can 1) distinguish isomers producing similar MS<sup>2</sup> spectra that are resolved by chromatographic or by ion mobility separation, that may have remained hidden, 2) facilitates spectral annotation, and 3) incorporates relative quantitative information which enables robust downstream metabolomics statistical analysis. Whereas users of the classical MN would have had to perform molecular networking and MS<sup>1</sup> analysis separately before performing a cumbersome linking of the outputs, FBMN method accepts the output of feature detection and processing tools, making them directly compatible with annotation tools tand the entirety of the analysis pipeline.</p>
<p id="P36">To fully utilize the MS<sup>1</sup> and MS<sup>2</sup> content collected during a non-targeted liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry data (LC-MS<sup>2</sup>) metabolomics experiment in a streamlined fashion, we have created an online workflow (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1a</a>) infrastructure that supports the outputs of feature detection and alignment tools for FBMN analysis (<a href="https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking</a>), including the standard output format for small molecules analysis (mzTab-M)<sup><a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a></sup>. The diversity of supported software, each offering different functionalities/modules, serves experimentalists, bioinformaticians, and software developers. FBMN is already the second most utilized analysis tool within the GNPS environment (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1b</a>) with more than 6,767 jobs performed in 2019 and has already been used in more than 80 publications using FBMN during its development since Nov 2017.</p>

(<strong>a)</strong> displays the results from classical MN with the LC-MS<sup>2</sup> data of <em>Euphorbia dendroides</em> plant samples (n = 1 LC-MS<sup>2</sup> experiment per sample); classical MN resulted in one node for the ion at <em>m/z</em> 589.313, while (<strong>b)</strong> FBMN was able to detect seven isomers. The plots (<strong>g</strong> and <strong>h</strong>) are showing the distribution of the coefficient of determination (<em>R</em><sup><em>2</em></sup>) from the Ordinary least squares Linear Regression (OLR) analysis between the observed and expected relative ion abundance for molecular network nodes in classical MN (<strong>g</strong>) or in FBMN (<strong>h</strong>). The upper charts present the distribution of the <em>R</em><sup><em>2</em></sup> for the network nodes with classical MN (n = 3,367) and FBMN (n = 877), and the bottom charts show the <em>R</em><sup><em>2</em></sup> distribution <span class="text-strike">from the OLR analysis</span> for the annotated reference compounds with classical MN (n = 49) and FBMN (n = 54). While classical MN uses the clustered MS<sup>2</sup> spectral count or the sum of the precursor ions to estimate the molecular network node abundance, FBMN uses the LC-MS feature abundance (peak area or height), resulting in a more accurate estimation of the relative ion intensity.</p></figcaption></figure><p id="P38">In non-targeted LC-MS<sup>2</sup> data acquisition, the same precursor ion is frequently fragmented multiple times during chromatographic elution. While MS-Cluster is often able to cluster these spectra into one single node in classical MN, there are cases where it will fail and produce multiple nodes representing the same compound. For example, this can happen for compounds producing mostly low intensity fragment ions, or for chimeric spectra resulting from coeluting isobaric ions are isolated and fragmented together. With FBMN, a singular representative consensus MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum is attributed for the LC-MS feature (defined as the detected ion signal for an eluting molecule)<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup>. The benefit of using FBMN in such a case can be illustrated with the metal chelating agent ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) observed in the LC-MS<sup>2</sup> analysis of plasma samples (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig 2e</a>), in which it is used as an anticoagulant agent. Classical MN resulted in 13 duplicated nodes with identical precursor <em>m/z values</em> in one molecular family, ten of which have spectral library matches to EDTA reference MS<sup>2</sup> data (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2e</a> and <a href="#F2" class="usa-link">f</a>). On the contrary, FBMN displays a unique representative MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum that matches EDTA spectra in the library. The reduction of redundancy within the resulting molecular network simplifies the discovery of structurally related compounds.</p>
<p id="P39">While classical MN uses the spectral count or the sum precursor ion count, FBMN uses the LC-MS feature abundance (peak area or peak height), resulting in a more accurate estimation of the relative ion intensity. The method of FBMN simplifies, organizes the data, and adds relative quantitative information and precursor isotope patterns. FBMN enables robust statistical analysis by providing relative ion intensities across a dataset. This capacity is demonstrated with a serial dilution series dataset of the NIST1950 serum reference standard, containing 150 spiked standards. Here, the LC-MS<sup>2</sup> were processed with MZmine<sup><a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a></sup> or OpenMS<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup> for FBMN (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig 2g</a>–<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">h</a>). A linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the relative quantification between classical MN and FBMN. <a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2h</a> shows that for FBMN, relative quantification has a coefficient of determination (<em>R</em><sup><em>2</em></sup>) value distribution mostly above 0.7, while this was not the case when the precursor ion abundance was obtained from classical MN via spectral counts (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig 2g</a>). The improved distribution of correlation coefficients towards 1 indicates a more linear response between molecular concentration and ion abundance, which improves the accuracy and precision of quantification results. FBMN facilitates the direct application of existing statistical, visualization, and annotation tools, such as QIIME2<sup><a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">12</a></sup>, MetaboAnalyst<sup><a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">13</a></sup>, ili<sup><a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a></sup>, SIRIUS<sup><a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">15</a></sup>, DEREPLICATOR<sup><a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">16</a></sup>, MS2LDA<sup><a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">17</a></sup>, and Qemistree<sup><a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">18</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="P40">FBMN further enables the creation of molecular networks from ion mobility spectrometry experiments coupled with LC-MS<sup>2</sup> analysis. As an orthogonal separation method, the use of ion mobility offers additional resolving power to differentiate isomeric ions in the molecular network. The integration of ion mobility with FBMN on GNPS can currently be performed with MetaboScape, MS-DIAL<sup><a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">19</a></sup>, and Progenesis QI. An example of such isomer separation using trapped ion mobility spectrometry (TIMS) coupled to LC-MS<sup>2</sup> is shown in <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 1</a>.</p>
<p id="P41">Available on the GNPS web platform at <a href="https://gnps.ucsd.edu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gnps.ucsd.edu</a>, FBMN is ideally suited for advanced molecular networking analysis, enabling the characterization of isomers, the incorporation of relative quantification, and the integration of ion mobility data. FBMN is the recommended way to analyse a single LC-MS<sup>2</sup> metabolomics study, but its applicability is limited when applied across multiple studies due to different experimental conditions and possible batch effects. Moreover, the use of FBMN for the analysis of very large datasets (containing several thousand samples) is limited by the scalability of most feature detection and alignment software tools. Thus, while FBMN offers an improvement upon many aspects of molecular networking analysis, classical MN remains essential for repository-scale meta-analysis large dataset processing, and is convenient for rapid analysis of LC-MS<sup>2</sup> data with less user defined parameters: one important aspect of molecular networks obtained with FBMN is the use of adequate processing steps and parameters, which otherwise could negatively impact the resulting molecular networks. To facilitate dissemination, education of the FBMN method, and the supported processing software, we created detailed tutorials and step-by-step instructions, available at <a href="https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking</a>.</p>
<p id="P42">The FBMN workflow offers not only automated spectral library search and spectral entry curation, but is also integrated with other annotation tools available on GNPS environment, such as MASST<sup><a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a></sup>, while promoting data analysis reproducibility by saving the FBMN jobs on the user’s private online workspace. The GNPS environment conveniently enables the user to evaluate different parameters and enables the sharing of the results via a web URL for publication.</p>
<section id="S1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Online Methods</h2>
<section id="S2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Development of Feature-based Molecular Networking (FBMN)</h3>
<p id="P43">The FBMN method consists of two main steps: 1) LC-MS feature detection and alignment, then 2) a dedicated molecular networking workflow on GNPS. Our first prototype for FBMN was developed with the Optimus workflow<sup><a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a>,<a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a></sup> that uses OpenMS tools<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup>. Following step 1 (feature detection and alignment), two files are exported: a <em>feature quantification table</em> (.TXT format) and a <em>MS</em><sup><em>2</em></sup>
<em>spectral summary</em> (.MGF format). The <em>feature quantification table</em> contains information about LC-MS features across all considered samples including a unique identifier (<em>Feature ID</em>) for each feature, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value, retention time, and intensity. The <em>MS</em><sup><em>2</em></sup>
<em>spectral summary</em> contains a list of MS<sup>2</sup> spectra, with one representative MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum per feature. The mapping of information between the <em>feature quantification table</em> and the <em>MS</em><sup><em>2</em></sup>
<em>spectral summary</em> is stored in these files using the feature ID and scan number, respectively. This simple mapping enables to relate LC-MS feature information or statistically derived results to the molecular network nodes. This approach was also used for the integration of other tools with FBMN, and does not require third party software like it was proposed in the past<sup><a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a>,<a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a></sup>. Finally, the FBMN workflow also supports the mzTab-M format<sup><a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a></sup>, a standardized output format designed for the report of metabolomics MS-data processing results. In this case, the mzTab-M file is used instead of <em>feature quantification table</em> and requires the input of the mzML files instead of the <em>MS</em><sup><em>2</em></sup>
<em>spectral summary</em> file. Support for the mzTab-M format enables the possibility to perform FBMN with any existing and future processing tools that support this standardized format.</p>
<p id="P44">The FBMN workflow has been integrated into the GNPS ecosystem and thus benefits from the connection with other GNPS features, e.g. the possibility to perform automatic MS<sup>2</sup> spectral library search, the direct addition and curation of library entries, the search of a spectrum against public datasets with MASST<sup><a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a></sup>, and the visualization of molecular networks directly in the web browser<sup><a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">24</a></sup> or with Cytoscape<sup><a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">25</a></sup>. The FBMN workflow is available on the GNPS platform (<a href="https://gnps.ucsd.edu/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gnps.ucsd.edu/</a>) via a web interface (See <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 2</a>). Jobs are computed and stored on the computational infrastructure of the Center for Computational Mass Spectrometry at the University of California San Diego. Each finished job is saved in the private user space for future examination and has a permanent static link that enables data sharing and collaborative analyses. We strongly recommend the sharing of this static link along with publications using GNPS workflows to facilitate results accessibility and data analysis reproducibility. Instructions to perform FBMN with the supported tools and input file format requirements are provided in the GNPS documentation (<a href="https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 3</a>).</p></section><section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Processing Mass Spectrometry Data for FBMN</h3>
<p id="P45">FBMN supports the output from several feature detection processing softwares. Depending on the type and size of mass spectrometry data, and the intended user (e.g. bioinformatician, mass spectrometrists, biologists, etc.) different software might be more appropriate. In general, tools with a Graphical User Interface (GUI) (e.g., MZmine<sup><a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a></sup>, MS-DIAL<sup><a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">19</a></sup>, MetaboScape, and Progenesis QI) are convenient for data visualisation and empirical parameters optimisation, but have often limited scalability that might prevent their usage for large datasets (more than 500 files). For these large datasets, tools that were designed to operate on a cluster/cloud computer should be prefered (XCMS<sup><a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a></sup>, OpenMS<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup> and MZmine to some extent). Regardless of the software or application, the processing steps and parameters should be performed as recommended by tool developers and experienced users through community feedback. Finally, automated optimisation modules can be used to finely tune parameters, which is particularly valuable when using command-line interface tools<sup><a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a>,<a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">28</a></sup>. While we acknowledge that there are many tools and configurations to analyze mass spectrometry data, we provide a summary of processing steps on the supported tools in the FBMN documentation (<a href="https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking</a><span class="text-underline">)</span>. These constitute an aggregation of institutional knowledge from tool developers and experienced tool users that do not encompass all possible applications, but rather provides a starting point for new users.</p></section><section id="S4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">FBMN after MZmine processing</h3>
<p id="P46">MZmine<sup><a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a></sup> is a popular open-source cross-platform software for mass spectrometry data processing with an advanced GUI that enables the users to visually optimize parameters and examine the results of each processing step. Moreover, MZmine allows for the export of a batch file containing all the steps and parameters used in the processing, thus enabling its reproducibility. To support FBMN in MZmine, the feature detection step (peak “Deconvolution module”) was modified to provide the ability to pair a feature with its MS<sup>2</sup> scans using an <em>m/z</em> and retention time range defined by the user (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 4</a>). Due to a new data structure and to support older projects (created with release &lt; 2.38), an additional specific filtering module (<em>Group MS</em><sup><em>2</em></sup>
<em>scans with features</em>) was developed to assign all MS<sup>2</sup> scans to the features of existing peak list (see this video for instructions: <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EL5pmFvpTFE" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EL5pmFvpTFE</a>). Moreover, a GNPS export and direct submission module was created (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 5</a>) which offers two modes: 1) Export of the <em>feature quantification table</em> and the <em>MS</em><sup><em>2</em></sup>
<em>spectral summary</em> file and 2) Direct FBMN analysis on the GNPS web platform (release 2.37+). The direct GNPS job submission generates all the files and uploads them together with an optional metadata table and default parameters (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 6</a>) to the FBMN workflow on GNPS. By providing the user’s GNPS login credentials (optional), a new job can be created in the personal user space (<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vFcGG7T_44E&amp;list=PL4L2Xw5k8ITzd9hx5XIP94vFPxj1sSafB&amp;index=4&amp;t=0s" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vFcGG7T_44E&amp;list=PL4L2Xw5k8ITzd9hx5XIP94vFPxj1sSafB&amp;index=4&amp;t=0s</a>). Otherwise, the user can be notified by email or directly redirected to the job webpage after the submission. With the option “<em>most intense</em>”, the GNPS Export uses the most intense MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum as a representative spectrum for each LC-MS<sup>2</sup> feature. When using the “merge MS/MS” spectra option (release 2.40+), a representative high quality MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum is instead generated from all spectra and exported as a representative spectrum (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Note 1</a>). The detailed documentation is available at <a href="https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking-with-mzmine2/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking-with-mzmine2/</a>.</p></section><section id="S5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">FBMN after OpenMS processing</h3>
<p id="P47">OpenMS is an open-source cross-platform software specifically designed for the flexible and reproducible analysis of high-throughput MS data analysis, including more than 200 tools for common mass spectrometric data processing tasks<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup>. Building on our experience with the Optimus development, the integration of OpenMS and FBMN was achieved by creating a <em>GNPSExport</em> tool (TOPP tool) as a part of the OpenMS tool collection (<a href="https://github.com/Bioinformatic-squad-DorresteinLab/OpenMS" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://github.com/Bioinformatic-squad-DorresteinLab/OpenMS</a>). A detailed description of the <em>GNPSExport</em> module and how to use it for FBMN is available at the following webpage <a href="https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking-with-openms/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking-with-openms/</a>. In brief, after running an OpenMS non-targeted metabolomics pipeline, the <em>GNPSExport</em> TOPP tool can be applied to the consensusXML file resulting from <em>FeatureLinkerUnlabeledKD</em> or <em>FeatureLinkerUnlabeledQT</em> tools (alignment step), and the corresponding mzML files. For each consensusElement (LC-MS<sup>2</sup> feature) in the consensusXML file, the <em>GNPSExport</em> generates one representative consensus MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum that will be exported in the <em>MS</em><sup><em>2</em></sup>
<em>spectral summary</em> file (using either the option “most intense” or “merged spectra”, see <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Note 1</a>). The <em>TextExport</em> tool is applied to the same consensusXML file to generate the <em>feature quantification table</em>. Note that the <em>GNPSExport</em> requires the use of the <em>IDMapper</em> tool on the featureXML files (from the feature detection step) prior to feature linking, in order to associate MS<sup>2</sup> scans [peptide annotation in OpenMS terminology] with each feature. These MS<sup>2</sup> scans are used by the GNPSExport for the generation of the representative MS<sup>2</sup> spectrum. Additionally, the FileFilter has to be run on the consensusXML file, prior to the <em>GNPSExport</em>, in order to remove consensus Elements without associated MS<sup>2</sup> scans. The two files exported (<em>feature quantification table</em> and <em>MS</em><sup><em>2</em></sup>
<em>spectral summary</em>) can be directly used for FBMN analysis on GNPS. The OpenMS-GNPS workflow for metabolomics data processing was implemented as a python wrapper around OpenMS TOPP tools (<a href="https://github.com/Bioinformatic-squad-DorresteinLab/openms-gnps-tools" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://github.com/Bioinformatic-squad-DorresteinLab/openms-gnps-tools</a>), and released as a workflow (<a href="https://github.com/Bioinformatic-squad-DorresteinLab/openms-gnps-workflow" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://github.com/Bioinformatic-squad-DorresteinLab/openms-gnps-workflow</a>) on the GNPS/MassIVE web platform. OpenMS version 2.4.0 was used<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup>. The OpenMS + GNPS workflow can be accessed and run here: <a href="https://proteomics2.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://proteomics2.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/</a>.</p></section><section id="S6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">FBMN after XCMS processing</h3>

The two exported files (<em>feature quantification table</em> and <em>MS</em><sup><em>2</em></sup>
<em>spectral summary</em>) can be directly used for FBMN analysis on GNPS. <p id="P52">The examination of the LC-MS<sup>2</sup> data (<span class="text-underline">MSV000080502</span>) from the <em>Euphorbia dendroides</em> plant extract showed the presence of numerous chromatographic peaks for ions in the range <em>m/z</em> 500–900, corresponding to diterpene ester derivatives. These specialized metabolites consist of a polyhydroxylated diterpene core acylated with various acidic moieties, that are typically found as positional isomers based on their acylation pattern. The extracted ion chromatogram (EIC) for the ion <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 589.31 in the <em>Euphorbia dendroides</em> extract data (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 7</a>) shows the presence of at least seven distinct LC-MS peaks between 24.5 and 27.3 min, including five peaks with an associated MS<sup>2</sup> spectra. The analysis of the extract and the fractions where these molecules were originally isolated (fractions 13 and 14) with <span class="text-underline">classical MN</span> resulted in a <span class="text-underline">molecular network</span> with two nodes for the <em>m/z</em> 589.31 ions (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2a</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 8</a>). These MS<sup>2</sup> spectra (cluster index <span class="text-underline">5352</span> and <span class="text-underline">5354</span>) resulted from merging 96 fragmentation spectra spanning from 23.6 to 26.5 min by MS-Cluster (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2b</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 9</a>). Close examination of the clustered spectra revealed that while all MS<sup>2</sup> spectra for the precursor <em>m/z</em> 589.31 present fragment ions <em>m/z</em> 501.26, 423.21, 335.16, and 295.17, three distinct spectral types could be established based on the ions relative intensities (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 10</a>). FBMN of the dataset with MZmine processing (<span class="text-underline">see the GNPS job</span>) enabled the differentiation of the MS<sup>2</sup> spectra of seven isomers (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2b</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 11</a> for the <span class="text-underline">molecular network view</span>). A detailed discussion on the differences observed between the two methods can be found in the <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supporting Information</a> (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Note 2</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Table 1</a>). Interestingly, in the original study<sup><a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a></sup> OpenMS was used for FBMN and resulted in the observation of three different positional isomers instead of seven, which shows that different processing methods and/or parameters can lead to different results with FBMN. These three isomers were subsequently isolated and differed by the position of one double bond on the C-12 acyl chain, or from carbon C-4 configuration<sup><a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a></sup>. Because FBMN connects the accurate relative abundance of the ions across the fractions and the molecular networks, it allowed to create bioactivity-based molecular networks<sup><a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a></sup>, which were used to predict and target potentially antiviral compounds. For detailed description of the extraction, mass spectrometry analysis, and structural elucidation, see the original manuscript<sup><a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a></sup>. The MZmine project and parameters used can be accessed on the MassIVE submission (<span class="text-underline">MSV000080502</span>).</p></section><section id="S11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">FBMN resolves isomers in large scale metabolomics studies</h3>
<p id="P53">FBMN was applied on a cohort of the American Gut Project (AGP), a citizen-scientist research project that enabled the observation of the commendamide in humans, along with other new <em>N</em>-acyl amide derivatives using molecular networking<sup><a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">9</a></sup>. Commendamide is a recently discovered bacterial <em>N</em>-acyl amide that was shown to modulate host metabolism via G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) in the murine intestinal tract<sup><a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="P54">The use of FBMN for the AGP data (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2d</a>) made possible to observe the presence of two additional commendamide isomers (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 330.26) and of an analogue <em>N</em>-(hydroxyheptadecanoyl)glycine (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 344.28), while classical MN resulted in the observation of one single consensus spectrum for each compound (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2c</a>). In addition, FBMN allowed to observe a putative commendamide derivative <em>N</em>-(dehydrohexadecanoyl)glycine (<span class="text-underline">CCMSLIB00005436498</span> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. 12</a>) in the commendamide molecular network. The sample collection and mass spectrometry acquisition methods are described in the original manuscript<sup><a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">9</a></sup>. The data were downloaded from MassIVE (<span class="text-underline">MSV000080186</span>) and processed with MZmine (2.37). The MZmine project along with parameters and export files were deposited to the MassIVE repository (<span class="text-underline">MSV000084095</span>). The chromatograms for <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 330.26 and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 344.28 displayed in <a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2c</a>–<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">d</a> are from samples 43076_P3_RB9_01_314.mzML and 38131_P5_RA4_01_538.mzML, respectively. <p id="P55">The benefit of using FBMN can be illustrated with the metal chelating agent ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), widely used in beauty products, food, and scientific protocols. A search for its occurrences in public spectral datasets with the mass spectrometry search tool (MASST)<sup><a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">32</a></sup> showed that it <span class="text-underline">is frequently observed</span> in plasma samples where it is used during the sample preparation. We took one of the public human plasma datasets (<span class="text-underline">MSV00008263</span>) where EDTA was observed. For a detailed description of the protocol and mass spectrometry parameters, see <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Note 3</a>. The analysis of the data with classical MN showed that the EDTA ions are found in two molecular networks. One network consists of <span class="text-underline">[M+H]</span><sup>+</sup> spectra and the other of <span class="text-underline">[M+Na]</span><sup>+</sup> spectra. Interestingly, each of these networks have one node with a large number of clustered spectra (node 91,205 for 4,655 spectra, and node 116,470 for 571 spectra, respectively), but yet EDTA ions are represented by multiple nodes although these nodes have the same precursor ion mass and retention time. Detailed analysis showed that while the median pairwise cosine values between EDTA spectra are high (median value of 0.93 and 0.94), the spectra are not clustering into a single node. Examination of the multiple fragmentation spectra for EDTA ions showed that some 1) are chimeric spectra “contaminated” by fragment ions produced by co-eluting isobaric ions, and 2) that other spectra were dominated by low intensity fragment ions resulting from MS<sup>2</sup> spectra acquired at low intensity. The method of FBMN was applied on that same dataset using the OpenMS-GNPS workflow (<span class="text-underline">see the job</span>), and the results showed that it efficiently reduces the appearance of these redundant node patterns from the same molecule (<span class="text-underline">see the FBMN job</span>, <a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2f</a>), both for the molecular networks containing the [<span class="text-underline">M+H</span>]<sup>+</sup> and [<span class="text-underline">M+Na</span>]<sup>+</sup> spectra. FBMN recovers the molecular similarity of in-source fragments observed for EDTA, which were not displayed with classical MN, as they now fall within the top-K rank (typically set to 10) of MS<sup>2</sup> spectral similarity considered in the network topology. The parameters used for OpenMS tools can be accessed in the OpenMS-GNPS job (<span class="text-underline">see the job</span>). OpenMS ver. 2.4.0 was used<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup>.</p></section><section id="S13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">FBMN enables the use of relative quantification in the molecular networks</h3>
<p id="P56">While classical MN uses the spectral count or the sum of precursor ion intensity to estimate the ion abundance, FBMN uses the accurate ion intensities obtained from LC-MS feature detection. The FBMN method brings in ion abundance across all samples by using the value of the chromatographic peak area or peak height as determined by the LC-MS feature detection and alignment software. Using multiple dilutions (n = 5) of the NIST 1950 serum reference metabolome sample<sup><a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">33</a></sup> analyzed by LC-MS<sup>2</sup> (3 independent experiments per sample) on an Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive, ThermoFisher) and processed with MZmine or OpenMS, we show the higher linearity of the relative quantification with FBMN and the improvement compared to classical MN (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2h</a>, <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Note 4</a>, and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figures 13</a>–<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">19</a>) using Ordinary least squares Linear Regression (OLR) analysis between the feature intensity and expected relative abundance in samples of known dilution factor (serial dilution). The sample preparation and mass spectrometry methods are described in <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Note 4</a>. The files along with the parameters for MZmine are available on the following MassIVE repository (<span class="text-underline">MSV000084092</span>). The OLR analysis was performed with python 2 (ver. 2.7.15) with the <em>LinearRegression</em> function of the <em>sklearn</em> package (ver. 0.20.1)<sup><a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">33</a></sup>. <p id="P58">The MGF file format is accepted by numerous computational mass spectrometry annotation tools. <p id="P59">While the molecular networking computation of the FBMN method is performed online on the GNPS web-server (runtime = 5 min to several hours depending on the number of features and job parameters), the data processing part of the method has to be performed with the computational resources available to the researcher (laptop/desktop computer, workstation, cluster/cloud infrastructure). The computational cost of the data processing part depends on 1) the software employed, 2) the number of samples in the dataset, and 3) the parameters set. For this reason, the computational cost of the method in all scenarios cannot be established comprehensively. Nevertheless, our experience and feedback from the FBMN community with open source tools such as MZmine or MS-DIAL showed that small size datasets (&lt; 50 samples) can be processed in 10–60 minutes with a desktop/laptop computer equipped with 8–16 GB of RAM. Medium size datasets (few hundred(s) samples) require the use of a workstation equipped with 16–32 GB of RAM memory, and large size datasets (&gt; 500 samples) need 32–64 GB of RAM. For very large datasets (more than a thousand samples), it is currently recommended to use OpenMS or XCMS on a cluster/cloud infrastructure.</p></section><section id="S17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Large dataset processing with OpenMS and XCMS</h3>
