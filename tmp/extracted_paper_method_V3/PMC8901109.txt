<p>Sepsis is a heterogeneous disease state triggered by an uncontrolled inflammatory host response with high mortality and morbidity in severely ill patients. Unfortunately, the treatment effectiveness varies among sepsis patients and the underlying mechanisms have yet to be elucidated. The present aim is to explore featured metabolism-related genes that may become the biomarkers in patients with sepsis. In this study, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between sepsis and non-sepsis in whole blood samples were identified using two previously published datasets (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95233" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE95233</a> and <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE54514" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE54514</a>). A total of 66 common DEGs were determined, namely, 52 upregulated and 14 downregulated DEGs. The Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) results indicated that these DEGs participated in several metabolic processes including carbohydrate derivative, lipid, organic acid synthesis oxidation reduction, and small-molecule biosynthesis in patients with sepsis. Subsequently, a total of 8 hub genes were screened in the module with the highest score from the Cytoscape plugin cytoHubba. Further study showed that these hub DEGs may be robust markers for sepsis with high area under receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). The diagnostic values of these hub genes were further validated in myocardial tissues of septic rats and normal controls by untargeted metabolomics analysis using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Immune cell infiltration analysis revealed that different infiltration patterns were mainly characterized by B cells, T cells, NK cells, monocytes, macrophages, dendritics, eosinophils, and neutrophils between sepsis patients and normal controls. This study indicates that metabolic hub genes may be hopeful biomarkers for prognosis prediction and precise treatment in sepsis patients.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> sepsis, biomarkers, bioinformatics, immune cell infiltration, metabolomics</p></section></section><section id="s1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction induced by an exaggerated, uncontrollable immune response to an infection, is one of the major public health concerns with high hospitalization and mortality rate (<a href="#B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B39">Singer et al., 2016</a>; <a href="#B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B36">Scott, 2017</a>). According to a systematic review extrapolated from published population estimates to the global population, approximately 30 million sepsis episodes and 6 million sepsis-related mortality occur per year (<a href="#B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13">Fleischmann et al., 2016</a>). In mainland China, sepsis affects one-fifth of intensive care unit (ICU) admissions with a 90-day mortality rate of 35.5% (<a href="#B44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B44">Xie et al., 2020</a>). Despite enormous international efforts and achievements in multi-disciplinary approaches, sepsis remains a major contributor to the growing global burden of morbidity and mortality (<a href="#B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34">Rudd et al., 2018</a>). Rapid early diagnosis and proper management are necessary to improve the outcomes in patients with sepsis (<a href="#B33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B33">Rhodes et al., 20162017</a>). Besides symptoms and signs for the early diagnosis of sepsis, available biomarkers are important for the following treatment of sepsis. Some biological markers have been established such as lactate, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), and soluble RAGE (sRAGE) in sepsis, while lack of sensitivity and specificity limit their clinical application (<a href="#B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B26">Oberhoffer et al., 1999</a>; <a href="#B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28">Phua et al., 2008</a>; <a href="#B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15">Hamasaki et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7">Débora Maria da Gomes Cunha and Yoshio Hamasaki, 2019</a>). It is of prime importance to identify more effective technologies and specific biomarkers for sepsis.</p>
<p>The early organ failure of sepsis typically occurs without grossly evident histological changes, while the metabolic and immune responses to sepsis occur early and have associations with organ failure (<a href="#B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23">Michie, 1996</a>; <a href="#B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38">Singer, 2014</a>; <a href="#B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19">Lewis et al., 2016</a>). Ignorance of changes in cell metabolic and immune functions may lead to the failure of sepsis treatment (<a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">Koutroulis et al., 2019</a>). Therefore, metabolic and immune biomarkers are needed to predict prognosis of sepsis.</p>
<p>An increasing number of studies on diseases are focusing on the role of metabolic changes that occur during the development of diseases and can also lead to subsequent pathophysiological changes of diseases. The metabolome, the underlying biochemical layer of the host’s genome, transcriptome, and proteome, can reflect all the external and internal cellular activities modulated throughout all other omic layers (<a href="#B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17">Jacob et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8">Djande et al., 2020</a>). Given that the metabolome is closely associated with the phenotype, metabolism-related genes provide new opportunities for personalized diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment towards specific pathophysiological mechanisms related to metabolism in patients (<a href="#B46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B46">Zhang et al., 2015</a>; <a href="#B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16">Itenov et al., 2018</a>). Among the mechanisms of sepsis and the development of organ dysfunction, various molecules and miRNAs have been studied, such as IL-17A, TLR4, C5a, MIF, miR-132, miR-146, miR-150, and miR-155 (<a href="#B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31">Pop-Began et al., 2014</a>). Metabolic reprogramming has also been observed early in sepsis and is proposed to be adaptive to limit additional injury, maintain energy balance, preserve cellular composition, and prevent DNA damage (<a href="#B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B37">Singer et al., 2004</a>). The use of metabolomics in sepsis patients may provide new approaches to explore sepsis markers and enable precision medicine.</p>
<p>Over the last few decades, the diagnostic criteria for sepsis have being updated periodically, but the primary pathophysiology remains constant—the presence of inflammation during disease (<a href="#B25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B25">Nedeva et al., 2019</a>). However, patients with sepsis may have distinct inflammatory responses and unique profile of immune activation against the pathogen (<a href="#B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29">Pinheiro da Silva and César Machado, 2015</a>). Therefore, the desirable way to decipher the heterogeneity and advance the treatment of sepsis may be identifying companion biomarkers for better assessing immune status and stratifying patients (<a href="#B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B43">Venet and Monneret, 2018</a>).</p>
<p>The complex inflammatory responses and metabolism-related genes during sepsis have not been fully elucidated. Herein, we integrated two datasets to identify differentially expressed metabolism-related genes using a support vector machine (SVM) and further explored the value of these genes in the diagnosis of sepsis. The immune cell infiltration analysis between sepsis patients and normal controls were further performed by CIBERSORT. Metabolic mechanisms underlying the inflammatory responses during sepsis were also investigated by using LC-MS to identify potential novel therapeutic targets and pathways for patients with sepsis.</p></section><section id="s2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="s2-1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Ethics Statement</h3>
<p>The study protocol was approved by the Research Council and Animal Care and Use Committee of the Research Institute of Surgery, Daping Hospital, Army Medical University (No. DHEC-2012-069). All the animal experiments were performed in accordance with the principles of the <em>Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals</em> set forth by the United States National Institutes of Health (NIH Publications, 8th edition, 2011).</p></section><section id="s2-2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Animal Management</h3>
<p>Adult female and male Sprague–Dawley rats (<em>n</em> = 22) weighing 200–220 g were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of the Research Institute of Surgery, Daping Hospital, Army Medical University. All animals were bred in the animal facility under filtered positive-pressure ventilation on a 12:12-h dark/light cycle. The temperature and relative humidity of the breeding room were maintained constant at 23–25°C and 55%–65%, respectively. The rats were allowed to acclimatize for 3 days before use and they were solid-food restricted with water <em>ad libitum</em> for 8 h prior to the initiation of surgery.</p></section><section id="s2-3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sepsis Model Establishment and Sample Preparation</h3>
<p>The rats were randomly divided into two groups: sepsis and control group. They were anesthetized for the surgical procedure with sodium pentobarbital (45 mg/kg, i.p.), which is a short-acting anesthetic agent with limited impact on cardiovascular functions for anesthesia. Cecal ligation and puncture were performed to reproduce a previously described sepsis model (<a href="#B48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B48">Zhu et al., 2016</a>). Briefly, the cecum was carefully exposed, ligated after a midline laparotomy, and punctured 0.7 cm from the distance with a 1.5-mm-diameter triangular needle, allowing fecal matter to flow into the abdominal cavity freely. After closure of the abdomen, the rats were returned to the cages with <em>ad libitum</em> access to food and water. Myocardial tissues were harvested at 12 h after CLP and samples were stored at −80°C until analysis.</p></section><section id="s2-4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Microarray Data</h3>
<p>To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), the microarray data for sepsis were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/</a>) under accession numbers <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95233" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE95233</a> (<a href="#B40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B40">Tabone et al., 2018</a>) and <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE54514" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE54514</a> (<a href="#B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27">Parnell et al., 2013</a>). To explore a prognostic gene expression signature for sepsis, dataset <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95233" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE95233</a> was used as the training dataset and then applied to verify the performance of the SVM classifier. Background correction and quantile normalization were performed using linear models for the microarray data (LIMMA) software package. All these data were freely available online.</p></section><section id="s2-5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Screening of Differentially Expressed Metabolism-Related Genes</h3>
<p>The metabolism-related genes were obtained from the Molecular Signatures database (MSigDB) v7.4 (<a href="http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb</a>) by searching using the term “metabolism”. The C2sub-collection (c2. cp.kegg.v7.4. symbols.gmt) was selected as the reference gene set. Next, we extracted the metabolism-related gene expression matrix from the <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95233" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE95233</a> and <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE54514" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE54514</a> datasets using the merge function in R. The analysis of differentially expressed metabolism-related genes between sepsis patients and normal controls was conducted using the LIMMA package implemented in the R statistical package. The threshold for the identification of DEGs was set at a <em>p</em> value of &lt;0.05. Then, these DEGs were divided into upregulated and downregulated DEGs in each dataset.</p></section><section id="s2-6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Functional Enrichment Analysis of DEGs by DAVID, FunRich, and GSEA</h3>
<p>In order to analyze the functions of DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the DAVID online database. The biological functions of DEGs were validated by the FunRich platform. The cellular components, molecular function, biological process, and biological pathway of these DEGs were then analyzed by using FunRich platform. <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05 was considered statistically significant. At the same time, the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEA 4.1.0 software. The MSigDB of c2 (c2. cp.kegg.v7.4. symbols.gmt) was used as the reference gene set in GSEA. The number of permutations was set to 1,000 and the phenotype labels were high and low expression. FDR &lt;0.25 and <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05 were set as the cutoff criteria to confirm significant gene sets.</p></section><section id="s2-7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Analysis of Hub Genes by String and GeneMANIA</h3>
<p>The STRING database (<a href="https://string-db.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://string-db.org/</a>) focuses on collecting, scoring, and integrating known and predicted protein–protein interaction (PPI) information. We first conducted a PPI network analysis of differentially expressed hub genes to explore the potential interactions <em>via</em> STRING. Next, the most important hub genes were screened using the Cytoscape software plugin cytoHubba. GeneMANIA (<a href="http://www.genemania.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.genemania.org</a>) is an online analysis tool providing information to predict the function of genes and gene sets, protein and genetic interactions, pathways, co-expression, co-localization, and protein domain similarity of submitted gene lists. In the present study, GeneMANIA was applied to construct a gene–gene interaction network for hub genes.</p></section><section id="s2-8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Real-Time Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)</h3>
<p>RNA was extracted from rat myocardial tissues using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat#15596018, Thermo). cDNA was synthesized using an RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time) for real-time quantitative qRT-PCR (Cat#RR047A, Takara). SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (TliRNaseH Plus) (Cat#RR820B, Takara) was applied to analyze mRNA expression of hub genes. The relative RNA expression levels were calculated with the efficiency corrected 2<sup>–ΔΔCT</sup> method using β-actin as an internal control. Gene-specific primer pairs used in this experiment are listed in <a href="#T1" class="usa-link">Table1</a>.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/T1/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="s2-9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Hierarchical Clustering Analysis for Hub Genes and Support Vector Machine Classifier Construction</h3>
<p>Hierarchical clustering of hub genes was applied to explore the differences in expression patterns between sepsis patients and healthy controls in the training dataset <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95233" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE95233</a>. The average linkage method was used to conduct hierarchical clustering and Pearson correlation method was used to set the clustering distance in ClustVis (<a href="http://biit.cs.ut.ee/clustvis/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://biit.cs.ut.ee/clustvis/</a>) (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">Metsalu and Vilo, 2015</a>). SVM was used to construct a classification model on the basis of the optimal feature subset with a 10-fold cross-validation method for identifying sepsis patients from healthy controls. A machine learning method that combined SVM with recursive feature elimination (RFE) was applied to construct a classifier verifying the hub genes that can discriminate sepsis patients from normal controls. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was constructed, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated in MedCalc (version 14.10.20, <a href="http://www.medcalc.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.medcalc.org/</a>).</p></section><section id="s2-10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Analysis of Immune Cell Infiltration</h3>
<p>Based on the gene expression matrix, CIBERSORT software was used to characterize the relative proportion and corresponding <em>p</em>-value of 22 immune cells in each sample in the training dataset <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95233" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE95233</a>. The statistical significance of the deconvolution consequences of all cell subsets was reflected by the CIBERSORT <em>p</em>-value. The number of permutations was set to 1,000, and a threshold <em>p</em>-value of &lt;0.05 was the criterion for successful computation of the samples. Then, heatmaps were constructed to visualize differentially infiltrated immune cells between sepsis and healthy control. Pearson’s correlation analysis in R software was used to investigate correlation matrix in all pairs of immune cells and the association of the hub genes with infiltrating immune cells, respectively.</p></section><section id="s2-11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Cytoscape</h3>
<p>Cytoscape 3.7.2 (<a href="https://js.cytoscape.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://js.cytoscape.org/</a>) was used to the visualize biomedical networks composed of metabolic and gene interactions. MetScape 3 (<a href="http://MetScape.ncibi.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://MetScape.ncibi.org</a>) is a Cytoscape plug-in for building and analyzing networks of genes and compounds, identifying enriched pathways, and visualizing changes in metabolite data. The data obtained from metabolites and the hub genes identified between healthy control and sepsis patients were imported into MetScape to detect the metabolomics network regulated by the hub genes.</p></section><section id="s2-12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metascape</h3>
<p>Metascape (<a href="http://metascape.org/gp/index.html" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://metascape.org/gp/index.html</a>) was employed for integrated functional enrichment, gene annotation, and interactive group analysis. The hub genes were inputted into Metascape for producing enriched pathways of hub genes.</p></section><section id="s2-13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolomics Profiling</h3>
<p>Metabolomics profiling was performed in tissues of septic rats and sham control by using a UHPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) coupled to a Q Exactive HFX mass spectrometer (Orbitrap MS, Thermo, United States). The QE HFX mass spectrometer was used to acquire MS/MS spectra on information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode in the control of the acquisition software (Xcalibur, Thermo, United States). The resulting three-dimensional data involving the peak number, sample name, and normalized peak area were used as the input for performing principal component analysis (PCA) in the SIMCA14 software package (Umetrics, Umea, Sweden). The annotated differently expressed metabolites were illustrated as a Volcano plot. Chord diagram was applied for biomarker metabolites depicting distributions and links between potential metabolites. The expression levels of the significantly changed metabolites and metabolic pathway analysis between septic rats and sham group were analyzed by a heatmap and a bubble plot generated by TBtools (<a href="#B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4">Chen et al., 2020</a>), respectively.</p></section><section id="s2-14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical Analysis</h3>
<p>All statistical analyses were performed in SPSS 17.0 (IBM, United States). The differentially expressed metabolism-related genes were identified using the Limma package with <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05 as the criteria. Hierarchical clustering analysis was used for the identified featured genes using the heatmap package in R. The SVM classifier was constructed using the e1071 R package with 10-fold cross-validation. ROC analysis was performed and the AUC was calculated to evaluate the predictive performance of the classifier. The correlation of co-expression was adjusted by Pearson’s correlation, and the strength of the correlation was determined according to the following absolute value criteria: <em>r</em> = 0.00–0.19 (very weak), <em>r</em> = 0.20–0.39 (weak), <em>r</em> = 0.40–0.59 (moderate), <em>r</em> = 0.60–0.79 (strong), and <em>r</em> = 0.80–1.0 (very strong). <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant correlation. All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.0.5, <a href="http://www.r-project.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.r-project.org</a>).</p></section></section><section id="s3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="s3-1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Identification of Differentially Expressed Metabolism-Related Genes</h3>
<p>The flow chart of this study is shown in <a href="#s11" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figure S1</a>. To identify sepsis-associated genes, we first analyzed genes differentially expressed in whole blood samples between sepsis and non-sepsis patients from the GEO database. As shown in the results of the PCA, gene expression patterns were significantly different between sepsis patients and healthy people (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1A</a>). Compared with non-sepsis samples, a total of 544 DEGs were identified in <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95233" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE95233</a>, 297 of which were upregulated and 247 were downregulated (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figures 1B,C</a>). A total of 203 DEGs were observed in <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE54514" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE54514</a>, 133 upregulated and 70 downregulated (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figures 1B,C</a>). Intersection analysis of these DEGs showed 66 genes, which were simultaneously differentially expressed in the two datasets, including 52 upregulated and 14 downregulated DEGs (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1D</a>). <p>In order to elucidate the underlying functions of these differentially expressed metabolism-related genes in sepsis, enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID 6.8 enrichment analysis. GO annotation analysis showed that these DEGs were involved in metabolic processes such as small-molecule catabolic process, lipid catabolic process, and so on (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2B</a>). Cellular component analysis showed that the DEGs were mainly related to secretory granule lumen, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, lysosomal lumen, and so on (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2B</a>). Further molecular function analysis found that the common DEGs were engaged in metabolism-related nucleotidyltransferase activity, phosphotransferase activity, vitamin binding, nucleobase-containing compound kinase activity, and NAD<sup>+</sup> kinase activity (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2B</a>). KEGG analysis was further utilized to define the possible pathways linked to the functions of these DEGs. The metabolic pathways were remarkably enriched, including pyrimidine metabolism, purine metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, and glutathione metabolism (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2A</a>). The above enrichment results were validated by the FunRich software v.3.1.3 that showed that the mitochondrial respiratory chain, catalytic activity, hydrolase activity, metabolism, and energy pathways were significantly enriched among DEGs (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figures 2C–F</a>). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was then performed to further validate the pathways that were differentially enriched among DEGs. The significantly enriched signaling pathways were presented based on their normalized enrichment score (NES). As expected, “carbohydrate derivative metabolism”, “cellular lipid metabolism”, “lipid metabolism”, “organic acid metabolism”, “organonitrogen compound biosynthesis”, “organophosphate metabolism”, “oxidation reduction”, and “small molecule biosynthesis” were differentially enriched in the sepsis group compared to those in healthy controls (<a href="#s11" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figure S2</a>). These results indicated that metabolism-related pathways may be involved in the pathophysiology of sepsis.</p>

<p>Previous studies demonstrated that immune disorder plays a vital role in the occurrence of sepsis and that metabolic profile determines the immune state (<a href="#B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12">Fitzpatrick and Young, 2013</a>). Therefore, we analyzed the immune cell infiltration proportions, correlation coefficients, and immune-cell proportion comparisons between sepsis patients and healthy controls. We first presented the different immune cell infiltrations by using CIBERSORT in each sample of the training dataset <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95233" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE95233</a> as shown in <a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7A</a>. Next, the distribution of 22 immune cells between sepsis and healthy control was visualized by a heatmap (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7B</a>). Compared to the healthy control group, the fractions of naïve B cells (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001), CD8 T cells (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001), CD4 naïve T cells (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.001), CD4 memory-activated T cells (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.001), resting NK cells (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001), M2 macrophages (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001), and resting dendritic cells (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) had a lower abundance in patients with sepsis, while the fraction of T cells and plasma cells (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001), resting CD4 memory (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05), gamma delta T cells (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001), monocytes (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001), M0 macrophages (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001), eosinophils (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001), and neutrophils (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001) was significantly higher than those in the control group (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7D</a>). In order to further evaluate whether 22 kinds of immune cells showed convergent effects during infiltration, correlation analysis was used to elucidate the potential one-to-one association. The proportions of different immune cell subpopulations exhibited weak to moderate correlation with each other (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7C</a>). Next, we explored whether the mRNA expression level of identified hub genes was associated with the infiltrations of immune cells in sepsis. The results showed that the expression level of most candidate hub genes was significantly positively correlated with the infiltrating levels of macrophages and significantly negative correlations with B cells, CD8 T cells, cytotoxic cells, eosinophils, NK cells, and most subtypes of T cells. In addition, these hub genes showed weak correlations with DC cells, mast cells, Th17 cells, Th2 cells, and Tregs (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7E</a>). In conclusion, these results revealed the clinical significance of heterogeneous immune cell infiltration in sepsis. Additionally, the association analysis between identified hub genes and immune cell infiltrations indicated an immunosuppressive and exhausted microenvironment in patients with sepsis, which may provide evidence and strategies for immunotherapy.</p>

<p>To date, early cognition of sepsis, prompt completion of the 1-h bundle, and timely administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics have been contributing to the largest reductions in mortality (<a href="#B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11">Evans et al., 2018</a>). Despite significant investments in research and allocation of resources, effective treatments to reduce mortality related to sepsis are still lacking. The challenge in developing strategies to manage sepsis is expected to be related to variability in sepsis presentation due to the complex underlying pathophysiology (<a href="#B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21">Martin-Loeches et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#B30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30">Plevin and Callcut, 2017</a>). This is probably one of the reasons why a single biomarker cannot adequately stratify the septic syndrome. Although sepsis is fundamentally defined as an inflammatory disease, it has recently been established that metabolism-related processes can be involved in the pathogenesis of sepsis (<a href="#B10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10">Englert and Rogers, 2016</a>). However, the potential role of metabolism-related genes in sepsis remains complex and obscure.</p>
<p>To identify the metabolism-related genes and explore molecular mechanisms contributing to sepsis, we first integrated two gene datasets (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95233" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE95233</a> and GSE541514) to screen DEGs between sepsis patients and normal controls, and found 66 metabolism-related genes that were differentially expressed in these two datasets (52 upregulated and 14 downregulated DEGs), suggesting that metabolism-related genes may play critical roles in the occurrence of sepsis. GO term analysis indicated that these DEGs were mainly enriched in metabolic processes and pathways. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed alterations in several metabolites of pyrimidine, purine, carbon, sphingolipid, glycerophospholipid, pyruvate, glutathione, and biosynthesis of antibiotics, resulting in the pathogenesis of sepsis. Consistent with functional enrichment analysis of DEGs, these featured DEGs were generally enriched in metabolism-related processes according to the GSEA analysis. Furthermore, a total of 8 hub genes were found in the module with the highest score from the PPI network of all metabolic DEGs, and their functions were mainly associated with nucleoside-diphosphatase activity based on GeneMANIA. Next, these 8 featured metabolism-related genes were identified to allow for good classification distinguished sepsis samples from normal samples using the SVM-RFE algorithm. Therefore, the present study exhibits the potential prognostic value of these featured genes in sepsis, which may provide useful strategies for predicting the outcomes of patients with sepsis.</p>
<p>Previously, inflammatory components of sepsis have been most widely studied and sepsis has been considered primarily as dysregulated immune response to infection. <em>Matrix metallopeptidase 9</em> (<em>MMP9</em>) and <em>Complement C3a Receptor 1</em> (<em>C3AR1</em>), immune infiltration-associated biomarkers of sepsis, have been reported to be related with the prognosis of sepsis patients (<a href="#B45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B45">Xu et al., 2020</a>). However, recent studies have shown that the pathogenesis of sepsis is clearly influenced by profound changes in metabolic homeostasis (<a href="#B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42">Van Wyngene et al., 2018a</a>). Therefore, novel sepsis biomarkers associated with metabolism need to be explored. In our study, the metabolomics network of the compound, gene, enzyme, and reaction regulated by the hub genes was first conducted, and it indicated that the featured hub genes play a pivotal role in the interplays among the dysregulated metabolites in sepsis. We further found that the metabolic pathways of hub genes were essentially involved in purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, GAP-filling DNA repair synthesis and ligation in TC-NER, nucleobase-containing small-molecule biosynthetic process, and HTLV-I infection, which was further validated by the LC-MS-based untargeted metabolomics analysis between septic rat myocardial tissues and normal myocardial tissues.</p>
<p>
<em>NME1</em> plays a role in lipid metabolism. <em>NME1</em> has been found to bind to porcine ST8 alpha-N-acetyl-neuraminide alpha-2,8-sialyltransferase 1 (pST8SIA1), inhibiting ganglioside GD3 synthesis and neuronal differentiation (<a href="#B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5">Cho et al., 2021</a>). As lipid metabolism is impaired in sepsis (<a href="#B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6">Chung et al., 2017</a>), <em>NME1</em> may be involved in the pathophysiology of sepsis, in accordance with our finding showing that <em>NME1</em> expression is elevated in sepsis patients compared with healthy controls. <em>NME6</em>, another member of NME family, is associated with ATP metabolism (<a href="#B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32">Rewcastle et al., 1996</a>). ATP release occurs during sepsis and activates macrophages, exacerbating prognosis of sepsis (<a href="#B9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9">Dosch et al., 2019</a>). Therefore, <em>NME6</em> may influence the outcome of sepsis by involving ATP metabolism.</p>
<p>
<em>POLD4</em> encodes the smallest subunit of DNA polymerase delta and is a metabolism-related gene (<a href="#B20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20">Liang et al., 2018</a>). The genomic instability caused by <em>POLD4</em> may exacerbate the prognosis of sepsis patients. <em>POLE4</em> is a histone H3–H4 chaperone that maintains chromatin integrity during DNA replication (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">Bellelli et al., 2018</a>), and may be involved in DNA metabolism (<a href="#B1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1">Abdelmohsen et al., 2012</a>). However, the exact role of <em>POLE4</em> in the pathophysiology of sepsis remains unclear.</p>
<p>
<em>POLR2J</em> and <em>POLR2L</em> encode two of the subunits of RNA polymerase II, which is responsible for synthesizing messenger RNA in eukaryotes. <em>POLR2J</em> and <em>POLR2L</em> are associated with purine metabolism and pyrimidine metabolism in sepsis patients (<a href="#B47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B47">Zhang et al., 2017</a>). In the present study, we also identified <em>POLR2J</em> and <em>POLR2L</em> as two of the hub genes of sepsis, indicating the important role of these two metabolism-related genes in the pathophysiology of sepsis.</p>
<p>
<em>Adenylate cyclase 3 (ADCY3)</em> is a membrane-associated enzyme and catalyzes the formation of the secondary messenger cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), being involved in pathophysiological metabolic processes. <em>ADCY3</em> has been found to be increased in human dendritic cells in the condition of inflammation (<a href="#B24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B24">Midttun et al., 2018</a>). However, the exact role of <em>ADCY3</em> in the metabolic changes in sepsis has not been identified.</p>
<p>
<em>Ectonucleoside Triphosphate diphosphohydrolase 1 (ENTPD1)</em> hydrolyzes extracellular ATP and ADP to AMP and is related to ATP metabolism and purine metabolism (<a href="#B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2">Bastid et al., 2013</a>). We identified <em>ENTPD1</em> as one of the hub genes of sepsis, in accordance with a study that shows that <em>ENTPD1</em> increases extracellular ATP metabolism, inhibits inflammatory signaling, and attenuates sepsis-associated liver injury (<a href="#B35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B35">Savio et al., 2017</a>).</p>
<p>These metabolic-related genes may become potential biomarkers of sepsis. Whether these genes participate in the pathophysiology of sepsis needs to be further studied. Targeting these genes may improve outcomes of sepsis patients.</p>
<p>As immune cells can switch back and forth between different metabolic states and preferentially utilize specific metabolites to sustain the relative functions of their effectors (<a href="#B14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14">Ganeshan and Chawla, 2014</a>), we then determined the inferred immune cell infiltration proportions, correlation coefficients, and immune-cell proportion comparisons between sepsis patients and healthy controls. Compared with the control group, the fractions of naive B cells, CD8 T cells, naive CD4 T cells, memory-activated CD4 T cells, resting NK cells, M2 macrophages, and resting dendritic cells had a lower abundance in patients with sepsis, while the fractions of T cells, plasma cells, resting CD4 memory, gamma delta T cells, monocytes, M0 macrophages, eosinophils, and neutrophils were significantly higher than those in the control group, indicating that acute inflammation and cell immunity play a crucial role in the pathophysiology of sepsis. Reprogramming of metabolism has been shown to be associated with the changes of immune cell infiltration proportions (<a href="#B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B41">Van Wyngene et al., 2018b</a>). However, the exact metabolic pathways through which the 8 hub genes affect immune cell infiltration in sepsis patients need to be further studied. All in all, the clinical significance of heterogeneous immune cell infiltration will expand our understanding of the molecular mechanism underlying the occurrence of sepsis and may provide novel evidence and strategies for immunotherapy.</p>
<p>In conclusion, using a bioinformatics analysis of two transcriptomic datasets (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE95233" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE95233</a> and <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE54514" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE54514</a>), we identified the immune characteristics and the metabolic mechanism of sepsis. The heterogeneities of immune cell infiltration could provide new insights into sepsis immunotherapy. Meanwhile, the present study identified eight metabolism-related genes (<em>NME1, NME6, POLD4, POLE4, POLR2J, POLR2L, ADCY3</em>, and <em>ENTPD1</em>) in patients with sepsis and the identified metabolism-related genes may represent diagnostic and therapeutic biomarkers of sepsis. Knowledge of these genes will improve our understanding of the molecular mechanism underlying the occurrence of sepsis.</p></section><section id="s5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Data Availability Statement</h2>
