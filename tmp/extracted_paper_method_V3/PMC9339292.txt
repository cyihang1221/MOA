</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Poor maternal nutrition during gestation can result in reduced offspring muscle growth and altered muscle metabolism. We hypothesized that over- or restricted-nutrition during gestation would alter the longissimus dorsi muscle (<strong>LM</strong>) proteome of offspring. Pregnant ewes were fed 60% (restricted), 100% (control), or 140% (over) of National Research Council requirements for total digestible nutrients from day 30 of gestation until parturition. Fetal (RES, CON, OVER) LM were collected at days 90 and 135 of gestation, or from offspring within 24 h of birth. Sarcoplasmic proteins were isolated, trypsin digested, and subjected to multiplexed, label-based quantitative mass spectrometry analysis integrating tandem mass tag technology. Differential expression of proteins was identified by ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc tests, and regularized regression via the elastic net. Significance was set at <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05. Over-represented pathways containing differentially expressed proteins were identified by Reactome and included metabolism of proteins, immune system, cellular response to stress/external stimuli, developmental biology, and infectious disease. As a result of maternal diet, a total of 312 proteins were differentially expressed (day 90 = 89 proteins; day 135 = 115 proteins; birth = 131 proteins). Expression of eukaryotic initiation factor (<strong>EIF</strong>) 2S3, EIF3L, and EIF4G2 was lower in OVER fetuses at day 90 of gestation (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05). Calcineurin A and mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 were greater in RES fetuses at day 90 (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.04). At day 135 of gestation, pyruvate kinase and lactate dehydrogenase A expression were greater in OVER fetuses than CON (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.04). Thioredoxin expression was greater in RES fetuses relative to CON at day 135 (<em>P</em> = 0.05). At birth, proteins of the COP9 signalosome complex were greater in RES offspring relative to OVER (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05). Together, these data indicate that protein degradation and synthesis, metabolism, and oxidative stress are altered in a time and diet-specific manner, which may contribute to the phenotypic and metabolic changes observed during fetal development and postnatal growth.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> maternal nutrition, muscle, proteome, sheep</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><hr class="headless">
<p>The abundance of proteins related to protein synthesis and degradation, stress responses, metabolism, and oxidative stress are altered in the longissimus from offspring of poorly nourished dams.</p></section><section id="s1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Poor maternal nutrition during gestation, either over- or restricted-feeding of the dam, can alter offspring growth and development (<a href="#CIT0023" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0023">Long et al., 2010a</a>, <a href="#CIT0024" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0024">2010b</a>; <a href="#CIT0034" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0034">Reed et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#CIT0017" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0017">Hoffman et al., 2016</a>; <a href="#CIT0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0013">Gauvin et al., 2020</a>). Offspring of ewes fed a restricted diet weighed less at birth than offspring of control- or over-fed ewes (<a href="#CIT0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0032">Pillai et al., 2017</a>). At mid-gestation, fetuses from restricted- and over-fed ewes had reduced secondary:primary muscle fiber ratios in the longissimus dorsi muscle (<strong>LM</strong>; <a href="#CIT0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0013">Gauvin et al., 2020</a>). Similarly, maternal nutrient restriction in ewes from 18 d before until 6 d after ovulation resulted in the formation of fewer myofibers and a reduced secondary:primary muscle fiber ratio at mid-gestation (<a href="#CIT0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0033">Quigley et al., 2005</a>). Altered fiber development in offspring of poorly nourished ewes was coupled with changes in gene expression and metabolite abundance in the LM (<a href="#CIT0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0026">Martin et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#CIT0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0013">Gauvin et al., 2020</a>). In fetal offspring of Wagyu cattle, methionine, a proteinogenic amino acid, was reduced in the offspring of restricted-fed dams relative to over-fed dams (<a href="#CIT0044" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0044">Zhang et al., 2021</a>). Further, both over- and restricted-nutrition during gestation reduced post-natal muscle growth (<a href="#CIT0034" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0034">Reed et al., 2014</a>), increased post-natal adiposity, and reduced muscle mass (<a href="#CIT0043" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0043">Yan et al., 2011</a>), but importantly, appear to do so through different mechanisms (<a href="#CIT0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0018">Hoffman et al., 2014</a>; <a href="#CIT0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0015">Govoni et al., 2019</a>).</p>
<p>Because fetal development is a critical period for muscle fiber formation, insults during primary or secondary myogenesis or the prenatal maturation period can impact fiber development and the potential for postnatal muscle metabolism and growth (<a href="#CIT0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0012">Funston et al., 2010</a>). Prenatal development starts early in gestation with primary myogenesis, the formation of nascent primary myofibers from fetal myoblasts. These primary myofibers serve as a scaffold for secondary myofiber development during secondary myogenesis (<a href="#CIT0001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0001">Ashmore et al., 1972</a>). The majority of fibers are formed by mid-gestation, and during the second half of gestation, myofibers mature, gaining contractile function and their initial metabolic and functional phenotypes (reviewed in <a href="#CIT0041" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0041">Wigmore and Dunglison (1998)</a>. This occurs such that, at birth, in non-rodent eutherian species, an animal has a full complement of functional muscle fibers, and no additional muscle fibers are formed postnatally (<a href="#CIT0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0031">Oksbjerg et al., 2004</a>). Postnatal growth then occurs through hypertrophy via protein accretion and the action of muscle satellite cells (<a href="#CIT0036" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0036">Schiaffino et al., 2013</a>).</p>
<p>The mechanisms that lead to altered muscle development as a result of poor maternal diet are not fully understood. Thus, we hypothesized that maternal over- or restricted-feeding during gestation would result in changes in the LM proteome, specifically in pathways that could impact muscle development during mid and late gestation in sheep.</p></section><section id="s2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="s3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Animals and sample collection</h3>
<p>All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the University of Connecticut Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (A13-059).</p>
<p>Animal procedures have been previously described (<a href="#CIT0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0032">Pillai et al., 2017</a>). Briefly, multiparous Western White-faced ewes (<em>N</em> = 78) were estrus synchronized using a controlled intravaginal drug release device (Easi-Breed CIDR Sheep Insert, Zoetis, Inc., Parsippany, NJ) and PGF<sub>2α</sub> (Lutalyse, 5 mg/mL; Zoetis Inc.; <a href="#CIT0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0022">Knights et al., 2001</a>). Ewes were bred by one of four related Dorset rams. Day 0 of pregnancy was determined by a rump mark with no following remarking. Ewes were moved to 3 × 1 m individual pens at day 20 of gestation and transitioned onto a complete pelleted feed based on National Research Council requirements (100% NRC) over a 7-d period. Ewes were confirmed pregnant via transabdominal ultrasound on day 28.5 ± 0.4 (<a href="#CIT0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0020">Jones et al., 2016</a>) and were assigned to a diet and time point of necropsy. Pregnant ewes were fed a restricted-fed (RES; 60% NRC; <em>n</em> = 27), control-fed (CON; 100% NRC; <em>n</em> = 25), or over-fed diet (OVER; 140% NRC; <em>n</em> = 26) based on the NRC requirements for TDN (<a href="#CIT0029" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0029">National Research Council, 2007</a>) starting on day 30.2 ± 0.2 of gestation. A subset of ewes was euthanized at days 45, 90, or 135 of gestation (corresponding with primary myogenesis, secondary myogenesis, and hypertrophy, respectively) by i.v. injection of Beuthanasia-D Special (Merck Animal Health, Summit, NJ). Fetuses were removed, weighed, and fetal tissues were collected. The remaining ewes were allowed to undergo parturition (birth; <em>n</em> = 5 to 7 per dietary treatment). Lambs at birth were allowed to nurse for up to 24 h before euthanasia to ensure adequate colostrum intake. Within 24 h of parturition, lambs were weighed and euthanized as described earlier for tissue collection. For the experiments described in this manuscript, a portion of the LM from days 90, 135, and birth was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for proteomics analysis. The ewe number, fetal sex, and litter size distribution have been extensively described in <a href="#CIT0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0032">Pillai et al. (2017)</a>. In brief, this study used 20 ewes at day 90 of gestation (CON = 7, RES = 7, OVER = 6), 19 ewes at day 135 (CON = 6, RES = 6, OVER = 7), and 18 ewes at birth (CON = 5, RES = 7, OVER = 6). At day 90, there were 39 total fetuses (CON = 14, RES = 14, OVER = 11), at day 135, there were 34 total fetuses (CON = 11, RES = 12, OVER = 11), and at birth, there were 35 total offspring (CON = 10, RES = 13, OVER = 12). Because fetal sex was not able to be accurately predicted, the ratio of males to females was not evenly distributed in any treatment within any time point (<a href="#CIT0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0032">Pillai et al., 2017</a>). Six singleton fetuses (two per treatment) were collected at day 90 of gestation, four singleton fetuses were collected at day 135 of gestation (CON = 1, RES = 1, OVER = 2), and two singleton offspring were collected at birth (CON = 1, OVER = 1). Fetuses from five triplet litters were collected at day 90 (CON = 2, RES = 2, OVER = 1), from two triplet litters at day 135 (CON = 1, RES = 1), and offspring from four litters were collected at birth (CON = 2, RES = 1, OVER = 1). The remaining litters were all twin litters.</p></section><section id="s4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample preparation</h3>
<p>Frozen LM samples (approximately 250 mg) were homogenized using the bead beating technique (TissueLyzer, Qiagen) in protein extraction buffer (50 mM Tris HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0). Protein lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × <em>g</em> for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through sterile cheesecloth, Halt protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA) was added, and the protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific).</p></section><section id="s5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Proteomic analysis</h3>
<section id="s6"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample preparation for multiplexed quantitative proteomic analysis</h4>
<p>Purified protein samples were prepared using an adapted filter-aided sample preparation method as described previously (<a href="#CIT0042" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0042">Wisniewski et al., 2009</a>). Briefly, aliquots of lysates (0.1 mg protein) were diluted to a final volume of 300 µL UA buffer (8 M urea, 0.1 M Tris HCl, pH 8.5) and subjected to Cys reduction using 25 mM dithiothreitol in UA buffer at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Afterward, each protein solution was added to a Microcon YM-10 MWCO filter device (MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO) that was preconditioned overnight with 10 mg/mL bovine serum albumin and subsequently washed with 200 μL aliquots of Fisher Optima LC/MS grade water via pipetting and centrifugation at 14,000 × <em>g</em> at 20 °C for 40 min. All following centrifugation steps were performed applying the same centrifugal force. When Cys reduction was complete, the reduced proteins were concentrated using a 40 min centrifugation step, followed by resuspension in 200 μL of UA buffer. The resuspended proteins were mixed via trituration and the device was centrifuged for 40 min. Subsequently, 100 μL of 0.05 M iodoacetamide in UA buffer was added to the concentrate and mixed on a shaker at 37 °C for 15 min to allow carbamidomethylation on all Cys residues. The filters were then centrifuged for 30 min. The resulting concentrate was diluted with 100 μL UB buffer (8 M urea in 0.1 M triethylammonium bicarbonate [TEAB] pH 8.0) and centrifuged for 30 min. This step was repeated and then 40 μL UB buffer was added to resuspend the concentrate, mixed via trituration, and the entire solution was transferred to a new 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. The filter was washed twice more to remove any remaining proteins with 40 μL aliquots of 0.1 M TEAB and both solutions were added to the first UB buffer resuspension. An additional 10 μL of 0.1 M TEAB was added and the filter was placed into a new Microcon receiving tube and centrifuged at 1,000 × <em>g</em> for 3 min. This final solution was pooled with the previous three solutions. The concentrates were subjected to proteolytic digestion by adding Pierce endoproteinase Lys-C (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 1:60 (wt:wt) enzyme:substrate and incubated on a thermal shaker plate at 37 °C for 16 h. The digests were then diluted with 0.1 M TEAB to a final urea concentration of less than 1 M. Sequencing grade, porcine-modified trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI) was added at 1:50 (wt:wt) enzyme:substrate concentration and incubated on a thermal shaker at 37 °C for 6.5 h. Digests were quenched by the addition of concentrated formic acid (Optima LC/MS grade, Fisher Scientific) to yield a final pH 2.5 and desalted on Pierce C18 Peptide Desalting spin columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific, P/N 89852) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The peptide content was determined by resuspending the desalted and fully dried peptides in 0.1% formic acid in water and then measuring the UV light spectral density at 280 nm using an extinction coefficient of 1.0 of 0.1% (g/L) solution.</p></section><section id="s7"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Tandem mass tag labeling</h4>
<p>Tandem mass tag (<strong>TMT</strong>) labeling was conducted from a modified protocol as described previously (<a href="#CIT0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0028">Murphy et al., 2021</a>). Desalted peptides were dried completely and then resuspended in 100 μL of 0.1 M TEAB and labeled using TMTsixplex isobaric label reagent kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturer’s instruction. The resulting mixture was desalted using Pierce peptide C18 desalting spin columns (P/N 89852), according to manufacturer’s instructions for TMT labeled peptides and dried using a speedvac concentrator. The labeled peptides were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid and a small aliquot from each reaction was analyzed by LC-MS/MS for labeling efficiency using the MaxQuant software package (<a href="#CIT0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0005">Cox and Mann, 2008</a>]), as described previously (<a href="#CIT0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0019">Huang et al., 2017</a>). All samples were verified to exceed 98% labeling efficiency. Equal amounts of TMTsixplex-labeled peptides from each of 6 individual proteomic samples were pooled to create 12 unique TMTsixplex sample groups using A280 absorbance by a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).</p></section><section id="s8"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Quantitative proteomic analysis via liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry</h4>
<p>All 12 TMTsixplex sample groups were dried, resuspended, and diluted to a total peptide concentration of 500 ng/µL using 0.1% formic acid in water and subsequently analyzed using an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (<strong>UPLC</strong>) system coupled directly to a Q Exactive HF Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). From each sample group, 1 μL of the sample was injected onto a 25 cm PepMap RSLC C18 Easy-Spray analytical column (100 Å pore, 2 µm particle size, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated over a 300 min reversed-phase UPLC gradient at 300 nL/min flow rate. Peptides were directly eluted into the Q Exactive HF via positive mode electrospray ionization using a capillary voltage of 1.5 kV. A Top 15 data-dependent tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) acquisition method was used and included the following parameters for MS full scans: 120,000 resolution, 375 to 1,400 m/z scan range, 1e6 AGC target, 50 ms maximum ion injection time, and 1 microscan per spectrum. MS/MS scans included the following settings: 30,000 resolution, fixed first mass of 100 m/z, 1e5 AGC target, 100 ms maximum ion injection time, 0.7 m/z isolation window, charge state exclusion “on” for charge states of unassigned, +1, and &gt;+8, normalized collision energy of 33, and 1 microscan per spectrum.</p></section></section><section id="s9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Bioinformatics and statistical methods</h3>
<p>Raw data files were uploaded into Proteome Discoverer (v2.2, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Sequest HT search engine was used to search raw files against the Uniprot <em>Ovis aries</em> reference proteome database (UP000002356, accessed 20180719) using the following parameters: trypsin enzyme specificity with 2 maximum missed cleavages, minimum and maximum peptide lengths of 6 and 144, respectively, static modifications of TMT6plex on peptide N-termini and Lys side chains plus carbamidomethylation on Cys, and dynamic modifications of oxidation on Met, deamidation on Asn and Gln, phosphorylation on Ser, Thr, and Tyr, plus protein N-terminal acetylation. Precursor and fragment mass tolerances of 10 ppm and 0.02 Da were used, respectively. All data were filtered to a strict 1% false discovery rate at the peptide spectrum matches, peptide, and protein levels. TMTsixplex-based quantitation included the following parameters: quantitative value corrections set to “true,” a co-isolation threshold of 50, total peptide amount normalization “on,” and protein ratio calculation was set to “Summed Abundance Based.” Equal mixing amounts within each TMT group were verified by monitoring the median abundance ratio for all conditions relative to the embedded control in each sample group. This median value was verified to be within 1.00 ± 0.03 across all 12 sample groups. Following removal of proteins with greater than 60% of missing values and imputation with the within treatment minimum, data were analyzed using ANOVA in R (version 4.0.5). Tukey post hoc analyses were used to determine the significance of differential protein abundance between treatments. Because of the relatively small sample size and imbalance of litter sizes and fetal sex, these were not included in the analyses. Significance was determined at <em>P</em> ≤ 0.05. Gene ontology categories were identified using <a href="#CIT0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0040">UniProt (2021)</a> and over-represented pathways were identified by Reactome (<a href="https://reactome.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://reactome.org/</a>).</p></section></section><section id="s10"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<p>As previously reported in <a href="#CIT0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0032">Pillai et al. (2017)</a>, RES offspring weighed 18.4% less than CON and 13.1% less than OVER offspring. However, there were no differences detected in the loin-eye area at day 135 of gestation or birth (<a href="#CIT0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0032">Pillai et al., 2017</a>). A total of 2,726 proteins were quantified by TMT-based quantitative mass spectrometry. Of those proteins, 312 were differentially expressed as a result of maternal diet (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>). Among the 89 differentially expressed proteins at day 90 of gestation, the top molecular functions included binding, catalytic activity, structural molecule activity, and molecular function regulator (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2A</a>). Top biological processes included cellular process, biological regulation, metabolic process, and localization (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2B</a>). Using Reactome, over-represented pathways were identified (<a href="#T1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). The pathways with the most entities include metabolism of proteins, immune system, and cellular responses to stress/external stimuli. Differentially expressed proteins related to metabolism, development, the immune system, and cellular responses to stress are presented in <a href="#T2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>. Expression of eukaryotic initiation factor (<strong>EIF</strong>) 2 subunit 3, EIF3L, and EIF4G2 were 15.5%, 5.8%, and 13.5%, respectively, less in OVER than CON fetuses (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05). Fetuses from OVER and RES ewes had 8.6% and 7.3% less EIF3B expression, respectively, than CON (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.04). 26S proteasome non-ATPase regulatory subunit (<strong>PSMD</strong>) 1, PSMD6, and PSMD14 were 7.1%, 6.1%, and 8.8% less, respectively, in OVER fetuses than CON (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05), while RES fetuses were similar to CON. Expression of proteins involved in protein ubiquitination, including ubiquitin-like modifier-activating enzyme 6 (UBA6) and ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase FAF-X (USP9X) were 5.4% and 5.5% less, respectively, in OVER fetuses than RES fetuses (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.04). Calpastatin (CAPNS1) protein expression was 11.8% less in OVER fetuses than CON (<em>P</em> = 0.03). Further, proteins involved in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (<strong>MAPK</strong>) signaling pathway were also altered in response to maternal diet at day 90 of gestation. Dual specificity mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (MAP2K1, or MEK1) was 10.6% greater in RES fetuses than CON (<em>P</em> = 0.03). Similarly, MAPK1 (ERK2) was 4.9% greater in RES fetuses than OVER fetuses (<em>P</em> = 0.04). Calmodulin-dependent calcineurin A subunit alpha isoform (PPP3CA, or calcineurin A) was increased 6.8% in fetuses than OVER fetuses (<em>P</em> = 0.04). Thioredoxin (TXN) was 10.2% greater in OVER fetuses than CON (<em>P</em> = 0.03).</p>

<div class="fn" id="T2Fn2"><p>Longissimus muscle was collected at day 90 of gestation from the offspring of ewes fed CON (100%), OVER (140%), or RES (60%) of NRC recommendations based on TDN for pregnant ewes.</p></div>
</div></section><p>Among the 115 differentially expressed proteins at day 135 of gestation, the top molecular functions included binding, catalytic activity, molecular function regulator, and structural molecule activity (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2C</a>). Top biological processes included cellular process, biological regulation, metabolic process, and developmental process (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2D</a>). Over-represented pathways with the most entities found include metabolism, innate immune system, apoptosis, and signaling by interleukins (<a href="#T3" class="usa-link">Table 3</a>). Differentially expressed proteins related to metabolism, the innate immune system, and cellular responses to stress are presented in <a href="#T4" class="usa-link">Table 4</a>. Lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) and mitochondrial ATPase subunit 8 (mtATP8) expression was 19.7% and 48.8% greater in OVER fetuses than RES fetuses (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.04), with CON intermediate and similar to both. Apolipoprotein C3 (APOC3) was 23.9% less in OVER fetuses than CON (<em>P</em> = 0.04). Mitochondrial creatine kinase 2 (CKMT2) expression was 18.9% less in RES fetuses than CON (<em>P</em> = 0.02). Muscle pyruvate kinase (PKM) expression in OVER fetuses was 15.2% and 12.9% greater than CON and RES fetuses, respectively (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.03). Calmodulin (CALM1), catalase (CAT), and copine (CPNE1) were 10.6%, 8.6%, and 7.1%, respectively, greater in RES fetuses than OVER fetuses (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.04). Fetuses from over-fed ewes had 19% less expression of CPNE1 than RES fetuses (<em>P</em> = 0.03). Thioredoxin expression was 11.9% greater in RES fetuses than CON and OVER fetuses (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05). Similarly, inosine-5ʹ-monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH) was 5.7% and 5.0% greater in RES fetuses than CON and OVER, respectively (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.01). Triosephosphate isomerase (TPI1) expression was 14.8% less in RES fetuses than OVER fetuses (<em>P</em> = 0.02). Serpin family member B 1 (SERPINB1) expression in RES fetuses was 25.1% and 15.8% greater than CON and OVER fetuses, respectively (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.001).</p>

Top biological processes included cellular process, metabolic process, biological regulation, and developmental process (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2F</a>). Over-represented pathways with the most entities found include metabolism of proteins, immune system, post-translational protein modification, and disease (<a href="#T5" class="usa-link">Table 5</a>). Differentially expressed proteins related to the immune system, cellular responses to stress, metabolism, protein metabolism, and the cell cycle are presented in <a href="#T6" class="usa-link">Table 6</a>. Expression of proteins involved in glucose metabolism, including aldolase (ALDOA), phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1), phosphoglucomutase-1 (PGM1), and PKM were lower in RES offspring than OVER (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.04). Phosphoglycerate kinase 1 was also less in RES offspring compared with CON (<em>P</em> = 0.02). Peroxiredoxin (PRDX5) was 7.2% greater in RES offspring than OVER offspring (<em>P</em> = 0.04). Proteins involved with protein ubiquitination, including Cullin-associated NEDD-8-dissociated protein 1 (CAND1), COP9 signalosome complex subunit 2 (COPS2), COPS3, COPS4, COPS7A, Cullin-3 (CUL3), CUL4A, DNA-damage-binding protein 1 (DDB1), ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase 13 (USP13), and UPS14 were 6.1% to 19.0% greater in RES offspring than OVER offspring (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05), often with CON intermediate. Similarly, proteins associated with the proteasome complex were 7.9% to 19.4% greater in RES offspring than CON offspring (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05; <a href="#T6" class="usa-link">Table 6</a>), with CON similar to both. Expression of DNA methyltransferase 3B (DNMT3B) was greater in RES offspring compared OVER (<em>P</em> = 0.01), with CON intermediate. Sirtuin 2 (SIRT2) was 18.4% and 16.6% greater in RES offspring relative to both CON and OVER, respectively (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.01).</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/T7/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="s11"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p>Poor maternal diet during gestation alters offspring growth and development (<a href="#CIT0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0015">Govoni et al., 2019</a>). Skeletal muscle is important as a mediator of metabolism and glucose homeostasis, as well as a protein source in livestock products. Muscle fiber number is determined by prenatal myogenesis, with fiber number set at birth (<a href="#CIT0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0031">Oksbjerg et al., 2004</a>). Changes in protein expression in developing muscle at mid-gestation (day 90) during secondary myogenesis, late gestation (day 135) during hypertrophy and maturation, or at birth likely impact muscle metabolism and growth. Within the changes associated with the maternal diet, themes have emerged that are similar between the proteomics analysis presented here and previous analyses in our experiments (<a href="#CIT0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0032">Pillai et al., 2017</a>; <a href="#CIT0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0026">Martin et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#CIT0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0013">Gauvin et al., 2020</a>; <a href="#CIT0037" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0037">Smith et al., 2021</a>). Changes in protein synthesis and degradation, metabolism, oxidative stress, and epigenetic modifications are of particular interest because of their impact on prenatal muscle development and postnatal muscle growth and metabolism.</p>
<section id="s12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Protein synthesis and degradation</h3>
<p>The balance between protein degradation and protein synthesis allows for maintenance of muscle mass, while greater protein synthesis leads to hypertrophy and greater degradation leads to atrophy (<a href="#CIT0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0009">Egerman and Glass, 2014</a>; <a href="#CIT0039" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0039">Torriglia et al., 2017</a>). In the present work, less expression of protein synthesis initiation factors eIF2S3, eIF3B, eIF3L, and eIF4G2 in OVER fetuses at day 90 of gestation is indicative of repressed protein synthesis. The eukaryotic translation initiation factor protein eIF-2 complexes with tRNA, the 40S ribosomal subunit (40S), and initiation factors eIF-1, eIF-1A, and eIF3 to form the 43S pre-initiation complex (43S PIC; <a href="#CIT0016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0016">Hinnebusch and Lorsch, 2012</a>). The 43S PIC can associate with mRNA through a cap-dependent mechanism that involves eIF-4A, eIF4G, and eIF4E (<a href="#CIT0016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0016">Hinnebusch and Lorsch, 2012</a>). Following binding to mRNA, the GTP bound to eIF-2 is hydrolyzed by eIF2B, which allows 40S to bind with the 60S ribosomal subunit, forming a competent 80S initiation complex. Reduction in the expression of eIF-2, eIF-3, and eIF-4 may reduce the capacity for translation initiation, and subsequently, protein synthesis at mid-gestation. For example, <a href="#CIT0027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0027">Miyake et al. (2017)</a> reported that inhibition of the 43S pre-initiation complex assembly in mice led to the accumulation of intracellular free amino acids in the muscle, demonstrating reduced protein synthesis.</p>
<p>The ubiquitin-proteasome system consists of activating (E1), conjugating (E2), and ligating (E3) ubiquitin enzymes that target proteins for translocation to the 26S proteasome for degradation. The SKP1, cullin, and F box (SCF) ubiquitin ligase complex is one of the most common ubiquitin ligase systems in mammalian cells and cullin proteins associated with RING ubiquitin ligases (Cullin-RING ubiquitin ligases; <strong>CRL</strong>) are among the most common E3 ligases (<a href="#CIT0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0002">Baek et al., 2021</a>). The E3 ligase activity of the CRL requires the association of NEDD8 with Cul1 (<a href="#CIT0004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0004">Cope and Deshaies, 2003</a>). In contrast, deneddylation by the COP9 signalosome complex allows CAND1 to occupy part of the SKP1 binding site, blocking the binding of the F box and thus, inactivating the SCF ubiquitin ligase (<a href="#CIT0014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0014">Goldenberg et al., 2004</a>). After ubiquitination and transport to the proteasome by the associated ubiquitin ligases, PSMD4, a subunit of the 19S regulatory particle of the 26S proteasome, assists with the docking of ubiquitinated proteins. Another subunit of the 19S regulatory particle, PSMD14, cleaves the ubiquitin chain from the protein substrate. This allows the recycling of ubiquitin chains into the cytoplasm and allows the protein substrate to enter the 20S core of the proteasome for degradation (<a href="#CIT0021" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0021">Kleiger and Mayor, 2014</a>). Less expression of PSMD1, 6, and 14 in OVER fetuses at day 90 of gestation, and PSMD1, 2, 3, 8, 12, and 13 at birth are indicative of reduced protein degradation through reduced ability to dock ubiquitinated proteins and recycle the ubiquitin chains. In RES offspring at birth, greater expression of proteins associated with the COP9 signalosome complex and CAND1 indicate reduced protein degradation. In contrast, the greater expression of Cullin proteins and USP proteins suggest increased protein turnover in RES offspring at this early postnatal time point. However, 50% maternal nutrient restriction from days 35 to 135 of gestation did not alter the presence of ubiquitin-labeled proteins at gestational day 135 in the gastrocnemius or soleus muscles in sheep (<a href="#CIT0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0035">Sandoval et al., 2020</a>). Similarly, we did not identify differences in proteins associated with ubiquitination at day 135 of gestation in the present study.</p>
<p>Calpastatin is a calcium-dependent inhibitor of calpains, which hydrolyze substrates leading to proteolysis (<a href="#CIT0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0006">Dargelos et al., 2008</a>). Calpain activity is required for myogenesis, including cell migration and myoblast fusion. Cytoskeletal reorganization by m-calpain allows for myoblast fusion but is also tightly regulated by calpastatin during development (<a href="#CIT0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0003">Balcerzak et al., 1998</a>; <a href="#CIT0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0007">Dourdin et al., 1999</a>; <a href="#CIT0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0008">Dulong et al., 2004</a>). Reduced calpastatin expression in OVER fetuses at day 90 of gestation may help balance protein synthesis and degradation but may also alter myoblast fusion and subsequent fiber development.</p>
<p>Greater expression of SERPINB1 may be a protective mechanism in the LM of RES fetuses at day 90 of gestation. The serpin family of proteinase inhibitors neutralize proteinase activity, in particular inhibitors associated with inflammatory damage (<a href="#CIT0039" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0039">Torriglia et al., 2017</a>). This is consistent with the lower expression of PSMD family members in RES fetuses at day 90 of gestation, supporting a reduction in overall protein degradation.</p>
<p>While the proteomics analysis identified changes in proteins associated with protein synthesis and degradation, there were no detectable differences in muscle fiber cross-sectional area or fiber numbers per field in the LM at day 90 or 135 of gestation, or birth in animals from the same experiment (<a href="#CIT0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0013">Gauvin et al., 2020</a>). However, the secondary:primary fiber ratio was reduced in fetuses from over- and restricted-fed ewes compared with controls (<a href="#CIT0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0013">Gauvin et al., 2020</a>), indicating reductions or delays in secondary myogenesis. This could lead to a reduction in the total number of muscle fibers formed, limiting postnatal muscle growth. The overall reduction in proteins associated with both degradation and synthesis may contribute to the reduced or delayed secondary myogenesis that occurs in this model. Postnatal muscle fiber growth was reduced in offspring of OVER and RES ewes between birth and 3 mo of age (<a href="#CIT0034" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0034">Reed et al., 2014</a>).</p></section><section id="s13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolism</h3>
<p>Offspring of restricted-fed ewes exhibited reduced expression of proteins involved in both oxidative and glycolytic metabolism, indicating an overall reduced metabolic capacity. Mitochondrial ATPase 8 is responsible for the final step in oxidative phosphorylation in the electron transport chain, catalyzing ATP synthesis. Mitochondrial creatine kinase transfers the high energy phosphate produced in the mitochondria to creatine, the cytosolic carrier. Together, the less expression of these proteins in RES fetuses at day 135 of gestation indicates reduced mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. Alternatively, the lower presence of LDHA, which catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, and TPI1, which, in turn, catalyzes the isomerization of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (G3P) and dihydroxy-acetone phosphate (DHAP) in glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, indicate reduced glycolysis in RES offspring. In OVER offspring, greater expression of TPI1 at day 135, and PKM at day 135 and birth supports increased ATP generation through glycolytic metabolism. PKM catalyzes the transfer of a phosphoryl group from phosphoenolpyruvate to ADP, generating ATP and pyruvate. Muscle metabolism contributes to overall feed efficiency (<strong>FE</strong>) in livestock. In pigs, high FE was also negatively associated with mitochondrial energy metabolism (<a href="#CIT0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0011">Fu et al., 2017</a>). Further, TPI1 and PKM protein expression were greater in the high FE pigs relative to the low FE pigs. However, in a model similar to ours, offspring of ewes receiving 140% of requirements from day 50 of gestation through parturition had decreased ADG and G:F compared with control lambs and lambs born to dams fed 60% of requirements (<a href="#CIT0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0030">Neville et al., 2010</a>). These discrepancies may represent differences between ruminant and nonruminant species, or different mechanisms that impact overall FE.</p>
<p>The reduced expression of proteins associated with metabolism is also present at birth, where RES offspring exhibit less ALDOA, PKM, PGM1, and PGK1 expression. Phosphoglucomutase catalyzes the interconversion of glucose 1-phosphate (G-1-P) and glucose 6-phosphate (G-6-P), forming the first intermediate in glycolysis. Aldolase catalyzes the reversible conversion of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate to G3P and DHAP as part of glycolysis. Phosphoglycerate kinase is a glycolytic enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of 1,3-diphosphoglycerate to 3-phosphoglycerate, producing ATP. Pyruvate kinase catalyzes the transfer of a phosphoryl group from phosphoenolpyruvate to ADP, generating ATP and pyruvate as the final step in glycolysis. Reductions in these protein enzymes in RES offspring compared with CON at birth indicates reduced glycolysis. This is consistent with increases in 3-hydroxybutyrylcarnitine and acetyl carnitine at birth in these animals (<a href="#CIT0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0026">Martin et al., 2019</a>). Acetyl carnitine shuttles fatty acids into the mitochondria, supporting oxidative phosphorylation. 3-hydroxybutyrylcarnitine is increased during fasting-induced ketosis and fatty acid oxidation in men (<a href="#CIT0038" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0038">Soeters et al., 2012</a>). Thus, increases in acetyl carnitine and 3-hydroxybutrylcarnitine metabolites, coupled with reductions in aldolase, PKM, PGM1, and PGK1 indicate that RES offspring are programmed toward greater oxidative metabolism at birth. This may translate into reduced FE, as oxidative metabolism is greater in lower FE livestock (<a href="#CIT0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0011">Fu et al., 2017</a>).</p></section><section id="s14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Oxidative stress</h3>
<p>Thioredoxin is a core component of the thioredoxin antioxidant system, one of two major thiol-dependent antioxidant systems in mammalian tissues (the other being the glutathione antioxidant system, <a href="#CIT0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0025">Lu and Holmgren, 2014</a>). The thioredoxin system, compromised of NADPH, thioredoxin reductase, and TXN, provides electrons to a large range of enzymes and is critical for defense against oxidative stress (<a href="#CIT0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0025">Lu and Holmgren, 2014</a>). Greater expression of TXN in OVER fetuses at days 90 and 135, and increased catalase at day 135 indicates increased presence of reactive oxygen species and oxidative stress. In previous work, we identified increased glucose-6-phosphate, coenzyme A, and reduced adenosine and adenosine monophosphate in the same experimental animals (<a href="#CIT0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0026">Martin et al., 2019</a>). These may also contribute to protective mechanisms against oxidative stress, through the generation of glutathione. Similarly, maternal obesity increased TXN activity in the placentas of obese women carrying male fetuses (<a href="#CIT0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CIT0010">Evans and Myatt, 2017</a>). Thus, over-feeding during gestation increases oxidative stress in the fetuses at mid-gestation. Fetuses from restricted-fed ewes exhibited greater catalase and thioredoxin expression at day 135 of gestation, which indicates changes related to the management of reactive oxidative species in the LM during late gestation. Catalase is a key antioxidant enzyme that mitigates oxidative stress by converting hydrogen peroxide to water and oxygen. The increased presence of these antioxidants indicates that offspring are exposed to greater oxidative stress and may be primed to handle additional oxidant load postnatally.</p></section></section><section id="s15"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Poor maternal nutrition during gestation has both acute and long-term effects on offspring development and growth. Together, these data demonstrate that the effects of poor maternal diet on the muscle proteome are diet specific, and reflect changes in muscle protein synthesis and degradation, metabolism, and signaling.</p></section><section id="a1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
