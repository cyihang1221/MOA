</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Purpose</h3>
<p>Endophthalmitis is a severe inflammatory condition due to intraocular infections that often leads to irreversible blindness. This study aimed to understand the age-dependent metabolic alterations in the vitreous of patients with bacterial endophthalmitis.</p></section><section id="sec2_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>The study included the vitreous metabolome of patients with bacterial endophthalmitis (group 1, <em>n</em> = 15) and uninfected controls (group 2, <em>n</em> = 14), which were further stratified into three groups according to their age: young (0–30 years), middle (31–60 years), and elderly (&gt;60 years). Vitreous samples were subjected to untargeted metabolomic analysis using high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS)m and acquired mass spectrometry data were analyzed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. The altered metabolites with log<sub>2</sub>FC of ≥2/≤2, <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05, and variable importance in projection &gt; 1 were considered significant.</p></section><section id="sec3_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>In a total of 109 endogenous metabolites identified, young and elderly patients with endophthalmitis showed 52 (elevated, 25; reduced, 27; <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) and 27 (elevated, 19; reduced, 8; <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) significantly altered metabolites, respectively, compared to their age-matched controls. Additionally, 27 metabolites were differentially expressed in young patients with endophthalmitis compared to the older group. The crucial metabolic pathways dysregulated in the older infected population were de novo purine synthesis and salvage, carnitine, polyamine (spermidine), lipids (prostaglandins), and amino acid (taurine, methionine, histidine) which could possibly be attributed to the increased disease severity and inflammation observed in a clinical setting.</p></section><section id="sec4_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h3>
<p>Despite the erratic metabolic changes observed in the younger group infected with endophthalmitis when compared to age-matched controls, dysregulation in the specific pathways such as purine, carnitine, arachidonic acid, and polyamine metabolism could possibly alter the immunological exacerbation observed in the older group.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> metabolomics, bacterial endophthalmitis, aging, vitreous humor, intraocular infection, inflammation</p></section></section><hr class="headless">
<p>Endophthalmitis is a serious sight-threatening inflammation of the intraocular fluids and is predominantly due to infection.<a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1"><sup>1</sup></a> Although it occurs primarily after ocular surgery or trauma,<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2"><sup>2</sup></a> endophthalmitis can rarely occur due to endogenous causes.<a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3"><sup>3</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4"><sup>4</sup></a> Its clinical presentation often involves pain and redness, along with corneal edema, hypopyon, and vitreous inflammation, leading to poor visual acuity and clinical outcome.<a href="#bib5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib5"><sup>5</sup></a> In some patients, it can result in enucleation of the globe.<a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6"><sup>6</sup></a> The extent of severity and disease outcome are related to the bacterial load and to the virulence of the organism.<a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7"><sup>7</sup></a> Nevertheless, seminal studies show that host immune responses play an equal role in disease severity, further influenced by age-related changes. Singh and co-workers<a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8"><sup>8</sup></a> reported an impact of age but not gender, as well as genetic diversity, in the pathogenesis of experimental bacterial endophthalmitis, with older mice exhibiting increased intraocular inflammation and greater bacterial burden resulting in enhanced disease severity as compared to the younger group of mice. Several other studies have also reported the association of age with many ocular diseases, including Fuch's dystrophy, dry eye syndrome, cataracts, presbyopia, age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, and glaucoma.<a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9"><sup>9</sup></a> Moreover, with the increased rate of cataract surgeries, particularly among the aging population, management of the risk of endophthalmitis becomes a challenge.<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10"><sup>10</sup></a></p>
<p>In the past two decades, metabolomics has emerged as an invaluable tool to identify disease biomarkers, to understand the altered metabolic signatures during disease pathologies, and to uncover novel drug candidates targeting the significantly altered pathways.<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11"><sup>11</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12"><sup>12</sup></a> Although several reports have demonstrated the utility of other high-throughput omics tools, such as transcriptomics and proteomics, to unravel endophthalmitis,<a href="#bib13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib13"><sup>13</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14"><sup>14</sup></a> there exists a significant void in understanding the altered vitreous humor metabolome in patients with endophthalmitis. Utilizing an untargeted multiomics approach and integrating both transcriptomics and metabolomic analysis, Singh et al.<a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8"><sup>8</sup></a> reported the upregulation of immune responsive gene 1 (<em>IRG1</em>) and elevated itaconate levels in the vitreous humor of Staphylococcus <em>aureus</em> endophthalmitis in mice. Such findings prompted us to perform this exploratory metabolomics study in the vitreous humor of patients with bacterial endophthalmitis, in anticipation of newer insights into the metabolic pathways involved in endophthalmitis in the context of aging. In order to differentiate the age-dependent alterations in the host metabolic response during endophthalmitis, we categorized our patients and controls into three cohorts: group A (young), 0 to 30 years; group B (middle), 30 to 60 years; and group C (elderly), &gt;60 years. By doing so, we demonstrated the role of aging in the pathobiology of bacterial endophthalmitis.</p>
<section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>
<section id="sec2-1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Ethical Statement</h3>
<p>This study was conducted according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study design and protocol were approved by the Institutional Review Board of LV Prasad Eye Institute, Hyderabad, India (LEC-BHR-P-05-23-1045). Prior to the patient's participation, written informed consent was obtained.</p></section><section id="sec2-2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study Design</h3>
<p>The study included 29 eyes of 29 subjects who underwent pars plana vitrectomy for the diagnosis and/or treatment of presumed infectious endophthalmitis between May 2022 and March 2023 at the LV Prasad Eye Institute. The age of the subjects ranged from 2 to 74 years. All of the recruited cases underwent complete ophthalmological examinations, including B-scans, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and visual acuity (VA) measurements at the time of diagnosis and at the 6-month follow-up. The best-corrected visual acuity measurements were analyzed, and cases with uncertain diagnoses or uncharacterized clinical features of infection, a history of autoimmune disease, hypertension, or steroid usage were excluded. The study group consisted of 15 patients diagnosed with bacterial endophthalmitis (10 Gram-positive and 5 Gram-negative microbiologically proven organisms), and the control group included 14 patients undergoing pars plana vitrectomy for non-infectious retinal conditions such as retinal detachment, macular hole, and vitreous hemorrhage during the study period. The recruited patients from both groups were further subdivided into three age categories: group A (young), 0 to 30 years; group B (middle), 31 to 60 years; and group C (elderly), &gt;60 years (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>).</p>

<figcaption><p>Overall study design.</p></figcaption></figure></section><section id="sec2-3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Collection and PreProcessing of the Vitreous Humor Samples</h3>
<p>A total of 500 to 1000 µL of the vitreous humor from all of the samples, including the control and infected groups, was taken as per the institute clinical protocol from the anterior vitreous just behind the lens with a 2-cc syringe plugged into the suction tubing of the vitreous cutter on cutting mode and before starting the infusion fluid during vitrectomy, of which 300 µL was transferred into a presterilized microcentrifuge tube, snap frozen within 30 minutes using liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for the metabolomic analysis using high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS). The remaining volumes of the vitreous samples were examined under direct microscopy, and microbiological evaluations were carried out by inoculating the samples on bacterial media (including blood agar, chocolate agar, brain heart infusion broth, and thioglycolate medium) and fungal media (Sabouraud dextrose agar and potato dextrose agar), which were incubated at 37°C and 25°C, respectively, for 7 days as per our standardized institutional protocol.</p></section><section id="sec2-4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Untargeted Metabolomic Analysis of Vitreous Humor from Endophthalmitis Patients</h3>
<section id="sec2-4-1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry Conditions</h4>
<p>Reverse-phase liquid chromatography was performed using a Zorbax SB-Aq Rapid Resolution High Definition column (#883975-314; 3.0 × 150 mm, 5.0 µm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA); mobile phase A consisted of 18.2 mΩ ultra-pure water with 0.2% acetic acid, and phase B consisted of liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS)–grade methanol with 0.2% acetic acid. Gradient programmed elution was carried out using the Shimadzu CMB-20A system controller (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan); a mobile phase initiated with 5% phase B for 2 minutes was increased to 35% for 15 minutes, further increased to 95% for 5 minutes, and maintained at 95% from 20 to 25 minutes, when it was then reversed back to the initial conditions from 25.1 minutes and maintained until 32 minutes. A flow rate of 0.5 mL/min was maintained throughout the total runtime of 32 minutes; the autosampler and column oven temperatures were maintained at 15°C and 40°C, respectively. Eluent from the LC was passed into the duo spray ion source of the TripleTOF 5600+ System (Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA). A MS scan range of 50 to 1000 m/z ran in both positive and negative ion modes separately. The Sciex DuoSpray ion source temperature was maintained at 550°C. An information-dependent acquisition (IDA) method was employed to acquire high-resolution MS and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) levels, with a collision energy of 35.</p></section><section id="sec2-4-2"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Data Acquisition and Processing</h4>
<p>The data were acquired using Sciex TF1.6 software. The acquired mass spectra were matched with the National Institute of Standards and Technology spectral library database using Sciex OS software.</p></section><section id="sec2-4-3"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample Treatment</h4>
<p>Vitreous samples were thawed from −80°C to 4°C, and 150 µL of sample was added to the 600-µL extraction solvent comprised of 50:50 ethanol:methanol with 100-ng/mL epicatechin as the internal standard. The mixture was vortexed for 20 seconds, incubated in ice for 10 minutes, and then placed in an incubator shaker for 15 minutes at 100 rpm. Samples were centrifuged at 10,000<em>g</em> for 5 minutes; 600 µL of supernatant was transferred in a vial and was kept on ice. Samples were vacuum dried at room temperature with the addition of 100 µL of methanol. The dried samples were then stored on ice for MS use or at −20°C if there was a delay in analysis. Before injection in LC-MS, the samples were prepared by adding 60 µL of reconstitution solvent and sonicated for 10 seconds; they were then transferred to high-performance liquid chromatography vials containing inserts.</p></section><section id="sec2-4-4"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Postprocessing Statistics and Pathway Analysis</h4>
<p>Both univariate and multivariate statistical analyses were performed following data normalization using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (<a href="https://www.metaboanalyst.ca" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.metaboanalyst.ca</a>). The data files were subjected to two sets of comparative analyses.<a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15"><sup>15</sup></a> Set 1 compared the metabolomic variations in the young, middle, and elderly endophthalmitis groups versus their respective age-matched uninfected controls. Set 2 compared the metabolomic alterations among the different age groups with those in the endophthalmitis group (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>). In the univariate statistical analysis, a volcano plot was developed to differentiate the significantly elevated or reduced metabolites with a fold change (FC) threshold of log<sub>2</sub>FC of ≥2. The unpaired Student's <em>t</em>-test or Mann–Whitney test was used to derive the significance between the groups, and <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05 was considered significant. The differential distribution pattern of metabolites was visualized by multivariate statistical analysis models, including unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) for set 1 and supervised partial least-squares discriminate analysis (PLS-DA) for set 2 comparisons. Further, the variable importance in projection (VIP) scores and heatmap analyses were used to analyze the important variables (metabolites) responsible for the differential cluster patterns of the principal components between the compared groups. The final significant metabolites were selected based on the observations made in both univariate and multivariate analyses with the criteria of log<sub>2</sub>FC of ≥2, <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05, and VIP &gt; 1. Alterations in the global metabolic pattern associated with the infection and senescence were identified using the pathway analysis module present in MetaboAnalyst 6.0.</p></section></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="sec3-1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Patient Demographics</h3>
<p>In this study, vitreous samples were collected from 15 patients with bacterial endophthalmitis and 14 age-matched uninfected controls. The clinical demographics of all of these patients are summarized in <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>. Among the 14 control patients, seven of the 14 controls had undergone retinal detachment surgeries, three had macular holes, two had epiretinal membrane complications, and two samples were obtained from retinal vasculitis and retinitis. The two controls with retinal vasculitis and retinitis were idiopathic and non-infective. Of the 15 endophthalmitis cases, two were positive for <em>Bacillus cereus</em>, two for <em>Streptococcus mitis</em>, and two for <em>Pseudomonas stutzeri</em>. The other nine samples were found positive for <em>Staphylococcus epidermidis</em>, <em>Pasteurella canis</em>, <em>Pseudomonas fluorescens</em>, <em>Staphylococcus gallinarum</em>, <em>Enterococcus faecalis</em>, <em>Bacillus licheniformis</em>, <em>Staphylococcus</em> lugdunensis, <em>Staphylococcus kloosii</em>, and <em>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</em>. The clinical demographics of the three age-based groups with endophthalmitis are shown in <a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>. A total of 109 endogenous metabolites were identified from the subjected vitreous humor samples.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/tbl2/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="sec3-2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolomic Alterations in the Vitreous Humor of Endophthalmitis Patients Versus Their Age-Matched Controls</h3>
<p>The obtained data were quantile normalized, cube-root transformed, and pareto scaled to reduce the skewness in the biological data and to obtain the Gaussian distribution. The volcano plot obtained from the comparative analysis of the infected and control groups in the younger age group highlighted 52 differentially altered metabolites (25 elevated and 27 reduced; <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) with a FC threshold of log<sub>2</sub>FC of ≥2 (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>a). In multivariate analysis, PCA score plots demonstrated well-differentiated cluster patterns of metabolites between the groups, explaining 59.3% and 15.7% of the differences in component 1 (PC1) and component 2 (PC2), respectively (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>b). Similarly, the infected and control groups for the older population showed a total of 27 dysregulated metabolites (nine elevated and 18 reduced; <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) with a FC threshold of log<sub>2</sub>FC of ≥2 as shown in <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>c. The PCA score plot showed two differential cluster patterns with PC1 and PC2 at 33.1% and 27.6%, respectively (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>d). Heatmap analysis also highlighted the clearly segregated clusters between the infection groups versus their respective age-matched controls (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figs. 2</a>e, <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>f). The comparative data for the infected versus control groups for group B patients are provided in <a href="#iovs-65-13-6_s001" class="usa-link">Supplementary Material S1</a>.</p>

<p>The acquired data were quantile normalized, cube-root transformed, and pareto scaled to reduce the skewness in the biological data and to obtain the Gaussian distribution. Comparative univariate analysis of the infected and control age groups showed 27 altered metabolites (17 elevated and 10 reduced) with a FC threshold of log<sub>2</sub>FC of ≥2 (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>a). Heatmap analysis also revealed a distinctly segregated cluster pattern between the groups (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>b). In multivariate analysis, the PLS-DA score plot clearly exhibited discrimination between two groups with 11.5% and 20.4% in PC1 and PC2, respectively (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>c). The metabolites that contributed to the differential cluster patterns between the groups are highlighted as VIP scores with the threshold of &gt;1 (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>d). We observed the differential expression of metabolites such as 2,3-dinor-6-keto-prostaglandin F<sub>1</sub><sub>α</sub>, leukotriene B4 ethanolamide, dihydrouracil, spermidine, uric acid, kynurenine, <em>N</em>-acetyl-glutamic acid, hippuric acid, 2-hydroxybutyric acid, and butyrylcarnitine, all of which are associated with pathogenesis of endophthalmitis and aging.</p>

<p>Pathway analysis revealed several altered pathways in the vitreous humor of endophthalmitis patients compared to their age-matched controls. Differentially altered metabolic pathways in response to endophthalmitis were noted among the different age groups. The metabolism of amino acids (tyrosine, arginine, proline, phenylalanine, tryptophan, aspartate, and glutamate), nucleic acids (purine), steroids, and transsulfuration was highly altered in younger patients with endophthalmitis compared to their age-matched controls (<a href="#fig5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>a), whereas the metabolism of biotin, nucleic acids, amino acids, and cysteine sulfinic acid was altered in older patients (<a href="#fig5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>b) Additionally, pathways such as amino acid, propanoate, nucleic acid, and cysteine sulfinic acid metabolism were altered in older patients with endophthalmitis as compared to the younger patients with endophthalmitis (<a href="#fig5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>c).</p>

The size of the circle is directly proportional to the impact value of the pathway.</p></figcaption></figure></section></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p>Aging is a well-understood risk factor for several ocular complications, including age-related macular degeneration, cataract, diabetic retinopathy, and dry eyes.<a href="#bib16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib16"><sup>16</sup></a> Several studies have shown that the complexity of the host immune response is also influenced by age-related changes that, in turn, increase its susceptibility to infections.<a href="#bib17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib17"><sup>17</sup></a> Although the severity of ocular infections depends on an organism's virulence factor, host–pathogen interactions, and individual host immunological responses, understanding the host metabolism in endophthalmitis is crucial to obtaining insights into signature molecules that can lead to the rational design of therapeutic targets.<a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18"><sup>18</sup></a> Our exploratory work on metabolomics was conducted with two goals: (1) compare the vitreous metabolome of endophthalmitis patients with their age-matched uninfected controls by categorizing them in three different age groups (young, middle, and elderly) to study the altered metabolome in endophthalmitis and decode the age-dependent variations in metabolic response for endophthalmitis; and (2) to compare the altered metabolomes among the different age groups (young, middle, and elderly) within endophthalmitis patients to relatively quantify age-dependent metabolome variations in infection.</p>
<p>In the control group, we observed a significant upregulation of several metabolites, including pyroglutamic acid, tyrosine, propionylcarnitine, <em>N</em><sup>2</sup>-acetylglutamine, 3-hydroxyisovalarylcarnitine, threonic acid, dimethylarginine, and lysine. These metabolites play crucial roles in various metabolic pathways, such as amino acid metabolism, oxidative stress regulation, and energy production.<a href="#bib19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib19"><sup>19</sup></a></p>
<p>Pyroglutamic acid and <em>N</em><sup>2</sup>-acetylglutamine are involved in glutamine metabolism, which is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis and antioxidant defenses.<a href="#bib19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib19"><sup>19</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20"><sup>20</sup></a> Upregulation of these metabolites in the control group could reflect a well-balanced cellular environment, contrasting with the dysregulated metabolic states seen in diseased conditions. Tyrosine is a precursor for neurotransmitters and plays a role in stress response mechanisms.<a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21"><sup>21</sup></a> Elevated levels in controls may indicate a stable physiological state, free from infection-induced metabolic disruptions. Similarly, the increase in propionylcarnitine and 3-hydroxyisovalarylcarnitine highlights enhanced fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial function, further supporting normal cellular energy dynamics in the control group.<a href="#bib22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib22"><sup>22</sup></a> Additionally, the elevated levels of threonic acid and dimethylarginine suggest an efficient regulation of nitric oxide and oxidative stress pathways, and lysine is crucial for protein synthesis and immune function.<a href="#bib23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib23"><sup>23</sup></a> The upregulation of these metabolites in the control samples underscores their role in maintaining metabolic integrity and possibly protecting against inflammatory or infectious insults, such as those seen in endophthalmitis.</p>
<p>In the endophthalmitis group, the majority of the metabolites associated with the purine, carnitine, arachidonic acid, TCA cycle, cholic acid, amino acid, and polyamine metabolic pathways were found to be significantly altered compared to their age-matched controls, with a log<sub>2</sub>FC of ≥2, <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05, and VIP &gt; 1, as shown in <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a> and <a href="#iovs-65-13-6_s001" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S1</a>.</p>
<p>Dysregulation in purine metabolism was found to be primarily associated with the endophthalmitis group regardless of age, thereby impacting cell survival and proliferation. Notably, xanthine (elevated in all age groups) and hypoxanthine (elevated in the middle and elderly group but reduced in the young) were found significantly altered (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) in endophthalmitis patients compared to uninfected controls (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>, <a href="#iovs-65-13-6_s001" class="usa-link">Supplementary Material S1</a>). The de novo purine synthesis and salvage metabolic pathways are essential for maintaining the supply of purine nucleotides, which are crucial for various cellular processes including DNA and RNA synthesis, energy transfer (ATP), and signaling (cAMP).<a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24"><sup>24</sup></a> The downregulation of such metabolites as adenine, adenine 5′-monophosphate, and guanosine 5′-monophosphate, which are associated with purine metabolism, in older patients with endophthalmitis in comparison to the younger patients could be attributed to the age-related decline in cellular metabolism and repair mechanisms affecting the synthesis and recycling of purine nucleotides, thereby elevating the cellular damage and weakening the immune response during infection. This could also render older individuals more susceptible to infections.<a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25"><sup>25</sup></a> An interesting finding worthy of discussion is the significant elevation of uric acid in the older patients, which is considered a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) during infection and stress conditions, which in turn activates a series of inflammatory cascades responsible for activation of the inflammasome complex via the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway.<a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26"><sup>26</sup></a> In our previous studies, we have highlighted the prominent involvement of the NF-κB pathway in exacerbation of inflammation, which could contribute to higher inflammation levels observed in older patients.<a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27"><sup>27</sup></a> Additionally, reduced levels of L-carnitine are associated with elevation of inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which are responsible for neutrophil activation. The significant downregulation of L-carnitine in our study also further confirms the presence of heightened inflammation in affected older patients (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>, <a href="#iovs-65-13-6_s001" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S1</a>). Other metabolites such as propionyl carnitine, isovaleryl carnitine, 3-hydroxyisovaleroylcarnitine, and butyrylcarnitine were also significantly reduced. Furthermore, decreased levels of L-carnitine and several acylcarnitines are also associated with compromised fatty acid oxidation, leading to impaired energy production in cells, which can exacerbate tissue damage and accumulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines,<a href="#bib28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib28"><sup>28</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib29"><sup>29</sup></a> contributing to the pathology of endophthalmitis. Isovalerylcarnitine is a metabolic product of L-leucine catabolism and a potent activator of Ca<sup>2</sup><sup>+</sup>-dependent proteinases (calpains); it is also associated with inflammation regulation<a href="#bib30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib30"><sup>30</sup></a> and is reported to be downregulated in bacterial endophthalmitis, as per our previous study.<a href="#bib31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib31"><sup>31</sup></a></p>
<p>The metabolism of asymmetric dimethyl arginine (ADMA) and related metabolites, including arginine and citrulline, was also significantly altered (<em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) in endophthalmitis group compared to the uninfected controls. ADMA is an endogenous inhibitor of nitric oxide synthase (NOS); thus, it regulates NO production.<a href="#bib32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib32"><sup>32</sup></a> During infection and inflammation, immune cells (such as macrophages) upregulate inducible NOS (iNOS), leading to increased NO production from arginine. Several studies have reported that inflammation, especially IL-6, has the ability to induce the dimethylarginine dimethylaminohydrolase (DDAH) enzyme, which is responsible for degrading ADMA. Enhanced DDAH activity can lead to reduced ADMA levels.<a href="#bib33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib33"><sup>33</sup></a><sup>–</sup><a href="#bib35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib35"><sup>35</sup></a> The increased NO production from arginine results in more citrulline being produced as a byproduct.<a href="#bib36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib36"><sup>36</sup></a> This could explain the elevated citrulline levels observed in the younger patients with endophthalmitis. Oxidative stress, common in infections, can further deplete arginine levels due to increased demand for NO production to combat pathogens.<a href="#bib37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib37"><sup>37</sup></a> DDAH is sensitive to oxidative stress, which can enhance its activity, leading to reduced ADMA levels.<a href="#bib33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib33"><sup>33</sup></a></p>
<p>Aging, along with the associated cellular senescence, significantly influences vitreous metabolism, contributing to changes in immune response and inflammation.<a href="#bib38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib38"><sup>38</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib39"><sup>39</sup></a> In older individuals (group C), we observed significant dysregulation of metabolites such as spermidine and uric acid, which are known to modulate oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways.<a href="#bib40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib40"><sup>40</sup></a><sup>–</sup><a href="#bib43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib43"><sup>43</sup></a> These alterations may exacerbate susceptibility to infections such as endophthalmitis, as senescent cells often exhibit a proinflammatory senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Moreover, kynurenine and <em>N</em>-acetyl-glutamic acid, both associated with immune dysregulation and neuroinflammation, could potentially be critical markers of age-related changes that can further compromise the vitreous environment, promoting pathogen survival and inflammation in the setting of endophthalmitis.<a href="#bib44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib44"><sup>44</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib45"><sup>45</sup></a></p>
<p>Within the endophthalmitis group, significant increases in inflammatory mediators such as 2,3-dinor-6-keto-prostaglandin F<sub>1</sub><sub>α</sub> and leukotriene B4 ethanolamide in the elderly and middle aged patients compared to the young patients indicates that older patients with endophthalmitis often exhibit a state of chronic low-grade inflammation (inflammaging). These inflammatory mediators also play pivotal roles in the recruitment of immune cells and regulation of the inflammatory response.<a href="#bib46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib46"><sup>46</sup></a> These metabolites are particularly significant in older populations, for whom the efficacy of the immune system declines, further complicating the resolution of infections. During infections, this baseline inflammation can amplify the production of inflammatory mediators, which could possibly account for the elevated inflammation in the older group. Furthermore, elevated levels of bile acids in the vitreous humor might reflect an attempt to regulate the local immune response and inflammation during endophthalmitis. As the elevation of taurine is associated with neuroprotective and repair mechanisms in retinal cells, its upregulation in the older patients indicates the need to protect the retina from further inflammation-associated damage.<a href="#bib47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib47"><sup>47</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib48"><sup>48</sup></a></p>
<p>Mosevoll et al.<a href="#bib49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib49"><sup>49</sup></a> reported that Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria trigger distinct immune responses and metabolic pathways, but our analysis did not reveal any significant clustering of metabolites. The extensive overlap of several significant metabolites such as leukotriene B4 ethanolamide, dihydrouracil, lipoxin B4, (2R)-3-hydraoxyisovalerylcarnitine, glutarylcarnitine, and <em>N</em><sub>1</sub>-acetylspermine in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative infected cases suggests that their expression is more likely due to aging rather than bacteria.</p>
<p>The strength of this study is its utilization of an exploratory approach to study the human vitreous metabolome for a detailed understanding of metabolic perturbations during bacterial endophthalmitis across different age groups, an area not explored before. Additionally, studies have reported that the differential lens and retinal physiology and pathology are accompanied by very specific changes in the vitreous metabolome. Although time-dependent partial exchange of metabolites such as propionate, <em>N</em>-acetylaspartate, and acetate occurs across the anterior and posterior vitreous, the described metabolome readout is not substantially mirrored by the adjacent tissue.<a href="#bib50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib50"><sup>50</sup></a><sup>,</sup><a href="#bib51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib51"><sup>51</sup></a> The consistent anterior vitreous sampling from the controls and infected cases in our study eliminated any sampling bias and potential technical interference.</p>
<p>Although the study limitations include small sample size and inherent biological variability among the enrolled subjects (shaped by genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors, which could contribute to observed differences), the consistent expression of metabolites in all of our biological replicates in each group validates the influence of age in the pathobiology of endophthalmitis. Furthermore, our study revealed a disparity in the mechanism of endophthalmitis across age groups. Historically, the incidence of endophthalmitis is primarily associated with cataract surgery in older age groups, and penetrating ocular trauma is the most frequent cause of endophthalmitis in younger age groups. However, obtaining equal proportions of each type of endophthalmitis across the various age groups would be challenging.</p>
<p>Sebag<a href="#bib52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib52"><sup>52</sup></a> reported that the vitreous undergoes notable structural and compositional alterations as humans age which are accompanied by metabolic shifts due to the degradation of collagen, hyaluronic acid, and other biochemical components leading to the release of metabolic byproducts. However, we did not observe these components in our current study, probably because we eliminated the non-significant metabolites that were present in both controls and infected samples and focused only on the differentially expressed metabolites. Additionally, Vaughan-Thomas et al.<a href="#bib53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib53"><sup>53</sup></a> investigated the role of proteolytic enzymes in the aging vitreous and found elevated levels of these enzymes in older individuals. Their increased activity in the aging vitreous accelerates the breakdown of structural proteins and the byproducts of this degradation process, including amino acids and peptides. Similarly, in our study, we observed the elevated presence of metabolites such as hippuric acid, taurine, phenylalanine, and <em>N</em>-acetyl-<span class="font-variant-small-caps">l</span>-aspartic acid in aged individuals with endophthalmitis. Understanding these metabolic shifts can provide insights into the pathophysiology of age-related diseases while also opening up additional avenues for developing new diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. Dysregulation in metabolites belonging to the purine, carnitine, TCA cycle, polyamine, and amino acid (arginine, histidine, and taurine) metabolic pathways was commonly altered in older patients with endophthalmitis. Targeting these metabolic pathways and addressing the weakened metabolic responses or chronic inflammation associated with aging could lead to the development of innovative therapeutic strategies, such as anti-apoptotic or senolytic agents, ultimately providing potential therapeutic insights and enhancing clinical outcomes (<a href="#fig6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>).<a href="#bib54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib54"><sup>54</sup></a></p>
