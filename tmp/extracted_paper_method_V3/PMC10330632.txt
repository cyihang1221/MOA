<p id="P1">Osteogenesis is modulated by multiple regulatory networks. Recent studies showed that RNA modifications and their reader, writer, and eraser (RWE) proteins are involved in regulating various biological processes. Few studies, however, were conducted to investigate the functions of RNA modifications and their RWE proteins in osteogenesis. By using LC-MS/MS in parallel-reaction monitoring (PRM) mode, we performed a comprehensive quantitative assessment of 154 epitranscriptomic RWE proteins throughout the entire time course of osteogenic differentiation in H9 human embryonic stem cells (ESCs). We found that approximately half of the 127 detected RWE proteins were down-regulated during osteogenic differentiation, and they included mainly proteins involved in RNA methylation and pseudouridylation. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis unveiled significant associations between the down-regulated epitranscriptomic RWE proteins and osteogenesis-related proteins. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of publicly available RNA-seq data obtained from osteogenesis imperfecta patients suggested a potential role of METTL1 in osteogenesis through the cytokine network. Together, this is the first targeted profiling of epitranscriptomic RWE proteins during osteogenic differentiation of human ESCs, and our work unveiled potential regulatory roles of these proteins in osteogenesis. LC-MS/MS data were deposited on ProteomeXchange (PXD039249).</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> targeted proteomics, parallel-reaction monitoring, osteogenesis, epitranscriptomics, RNA modifications, stem cells, differentiation</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="ABS2"><h2>Graphical Abstract</h2>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/bc4e/10330632/f4c1be024e91/nihms-1912108-f0005.jpg" loading="lazy" height="253" width="640" alt="graphic file with name nihms-1912108-f0005.jpg"></p></section><section id="S1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p id="P3">Osteogenesis, i.e., the process of bone formation, usually begins at about six weeks after conception and continues into early adulthood, where growth in bone length stops at around age 25.<sup><a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">1</a></sup> However, in response to fractures or stress from accidents or physical activities, bone development continues throughout adulthood to repair damage or increase bone thickness for meeting physiological needs. Each year millions of individuals suffer from osteo-degenerative diseases, including osteopenia, osteoporosis, Paget’s disease, and osteogenesis imperfecta (OI).<sup><a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">2</a>,<a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a></sup></p>
<p id="P4">Existing treatments for most bone diseases involve two strategies, namely, reducing bone reabsorption and promoting bone formation.<sup><a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a>–<a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a></sup> These treatments, however, only relieve symptoms and do not cure the diseases, not to mention that there are no effective treatments in some cases.<sup><a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a></sup> As an emerging therapeutic approach, stem cell therapy has been employed for treating neurodegenerative disorders, ocular disease, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, etc.<sup><a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a>–<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup> Considering their ability to regenerate bone, stem cells are also used in clinical trials for the treatment of osteoporosis and OI.<sup><a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a>,<a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">12</a></sup> In this vein, embryonic stem cells (ESCs), owing to their immortality and pluripotency, are strong candidates in stem cell therapies for bone diseases.<sup><a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">13</a></sup></p>
<p id="P5">Osteogenesis is regulated by a sophisticated, multilayered network, which involves various epigenetic and epitranscriptomic mechanisms.<sup><a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a>,<a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">15</a></sup> In the latter respect, epitranscriptomic RNA modifications assume important roles in regulating numerous biological processes.<sup><a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">16</a></sup> So far, more than 150 modified ribonucleosides have been detected in RNA.<sup><a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">17</a></sup> Among them, <em>N</em><sup>6</sup>-methyladenosine (m<sup>6</sup>A) stands as the most prevalent internal modification found within mRNAs. Multiple lines of evidence support that m<sup>6</sup>A, together with its reader, writer, and eraser (RWE) proteins, coordinates with other proteins to regulate osteogenesis.<sup><a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">15</a></sup> For instance, the m<sup>6</sup>A reader protein YTHDF1 regulates zinc-finger protein 839 (ZNF839) in an m<sup>6</sup>A-dependent manner, where the interaction between ZNF839 and Runx2, a master transcription factor of osteogenic differentiation, promotes the osteogenesis of human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs).<sup><a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">18</a></sup> In addition, loss of METTL3, the catalytic subunit of the main m<sup>6</sup>A methyltransferase complex, suppressed the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.<sup><a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">19</a></sup> Moreover, FTO, an m<sup>6</sup>A demethylase and a component of the GDF11–FTO–PPAR<em>γ</em> signaling axis, exhibits a regulatory role in cell fate decision of BMSCs.<sup><a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a></sup> We reason that, aside from m<sup>6</sup>A, other modified nucleosides in RNA and their RWE proteins may also regulate osteogenesis.</p>
<p id="P6">Here, we induced osteogenic differentiation of H9 human ESCs and performed temporal profiling of epitranscriptomic RWE proteins during the entire time course of osteogenesis. Our results yielded new knowledge about the functions of epitranscriptomic RWE proteins in osteogenesis and advanced the understanding of the complex regulatory networks governing osteogenesis.</p></section><section id="S2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">EXPERIMENTAL SECTION</h2>
<section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Cell Culture and Osteogenic Differentiation</h3>
<p id="P7">H9 Human embryonic stem cells (WiCell) were cultured with mTeSR plus medium (Stem Cell Technologies) on a Matrigel (BD Bioscience)-coated surface, as described recently.<sup><a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a></sup> The initiation of differentiation followed previously established protocols.<sup><a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a>–<a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">24</a></sup> In brief, ESCs were withdrawn from pluripotency by culturing in Dulbecco’s Modified Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (Atlanta Biologicals), 0.5% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), 0.1 mM <em>β</em>-mercaptoethanol (Sigma), and 1% nonessential amino acids (Gibco) for 5 days. The cells were subsequently cultured in the same medium but supplemented with 50 <em>μ</em>g/mL sodium ascorbate (Sigma), 10 mM <em>β</em>-glycerophosphate, and 0.12 <em>μ</em>M vitamin D3 for an additional 15 days to further induce osteogenesis. Under these conditions, the cells were differentiated into osteoprogenitor cells at around day 10 postinduction and fully differentiated into mature osteoblasts at days 15–20, as confirmed by Western blot analysis of an osteogenesis marker (i.e., OCN) and calcification measurement.<sup><a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a></sup> Confluent cells prior to differentiation induction were collected as Day-0, and the cells after differentiation/osteogenesis induction were collected as indicated.</p></section><section id="S4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">LC-PRM Analysis</h3>
<p id="P8">LC-PRM analyses of 154 epitranscriptomic RWE proteins were conducted on a Q Exactive Plus quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer equipped with a Nanospray Flex ionization source and coupled to a Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano UPLC system. The peptide mixture was first loaded and trapped on a home-packed column (150 <em>μ</em>m ID × 40 mm) with 5 <em>μ</em>m Reprosil-Pur C18-AQ resin (Dr. Maisch GmbH HPLC), at a flow rate of 2 <em>μ</em>L/min within 8 min. The peptides were then eluted onto an analytical column (75 <em>μ</em>m ID × 200 mm) packed in-house with 3 <em>μ</em>m Reprosil-Pur C18-AQ resin (Dr. Maisch GmbH HPLC), at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Formic acid (0.1%, v/v) in water and in acetonitrile/H<sub>2</sub>O (80:20, v/v) were used as mobile phases A and B, respectively. Peptide separation was conducted with a linear gradient of 6–43% B in 125 min. The electrospray voltage was 1.8 kV, and the ion transport tube temperature was 320 °C. The isolation width for precursor ions was 1.6 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>, and the fragmentation method was HCD, where the normalized collision energy was 28. After the calibration of the precursor ions’ retention times using tryptic peptides of bovine serum albumin (BSA) as references, MS/MS for the precursor ions on the inclusion lists were acquired in scheduled PRM mode with a 10-min retention time window.</p>
<p id="P9">The raw LC-PRM data were imported to Skyline version 21.2<sup><a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">25</a></sup> for processing. The acquired MS/MS of each precursor ion was compared with that in the spectral library, where spectral similarity was gauged using dot product (dotp) value.<sup><a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a></sup> The presence of 4–6 concurrent fragment ions and a dotp value greater than 0.7 were considered a positive identification. The Skyline data were exported to Excel for further data processing (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Table S1c</a>–<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">f</a>). Briefly, the ratio of each peptide derived from RWE protein was calculated using the following normalization procedure: (1) the peak area of the endogenous peptide was divided by that of its corresponding stable isotope-labeled (SIL) peptide or a surrogate standard with a similar retention time; (2) the resulting ratio was further normalized against the ratio of the sum of peak areas for all the light peptides over that for all the heavy peptides in each LC-PRM run. The relative ratios of the peptides were then normalized to that in the Day-0 samples. The peptide ratios in each sample, averaged from the quantification results of two technical replicates, were represented by mean ± SD. The relative ratios of the epitranscriptomic RWE proteins in one biological replicate were calculated by the mean of the relative ratios of the peptides derived from the same protein. The final ratios of the RWE proteins were averaged from the quantification results of two biological replicates (except Day-5 and Day-6, which contained only one replicate). Hierarchical clustering with <em>Z</em>-score standardization was performed with Perseus 2.0.3.1.<sup><a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a></sup>
<em>Z</em>-score standardization was used to standardize the alterations in protein expression during the differentiation process on the same scale. Proteins were clustered using the Euclidean distance.</p></section></section><section id="S5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS AND DISCUSSION</h2>
<p id="P10">Previous studies showed that osteogenic differentiation of ESCs could be elicited <em>in vitro</em> by incubating these cells with <em>β</em>-glycerophosphate, ascorbic acid, and the active form of vitamin D<sub>3</sub> or dexamethasone.<sup><a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a>,<a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">28</a></sup> We induced osteogenic differentiation of H9 human ESCs by using a previously developed vitamin D<sub>3</sub>-based monolayer overgrowth culture approach,<sup><a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a>,<a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29">29</a></sup> with some minor modifications.<sup><a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a></sup> Confluent cells prior to differentiation induction were collected as Day-0, and the cells were also collected at 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, and 20 days following differentiation/osteogenesis induction<sup><a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a></sup> for temporal profiling of epitranscriptomic RWE proteins during osteogenesis (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>). We employed LC-PRM, along with the use of synthetic stable isotope-labeled (SIL) peptides,<sup><a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">30</a></sup> to examine the relative expression of 154 epitranscriptomic RWE proteins in cells collected at different time points following osteogenic induction (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>). Compared to the discovery approaches, e.g., data-dependent acquisition (DDA) and data-independent acquisition (DIA), PRM-based targeted proteomics provides sensitive and reproducible quantification results for specific proteins of interest, especially those of low abundance. We conducted the experiments in two biological replicates except Day-5 and Day-6, where only one biological replicate was analyzed, owing to inadequate amounts of protein samples available for the other replicate. To obtain reliable quantification results, we analyzed each biological replicate by LC-PRM twice.</p>

Considering that more than half of the RWE proteins were down-regulated during osteogenesis, we focused our subsequent investigation on these proteins.</p>

<p id="P15">The LC-PRM quantification results also showed that the abundances of the major detected pseudouridine synthases exhibited steady diminutions during the entire time course of osteogenic differentiation (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S3</a>). In this regard, RNA pseudouridylation was shown to modulate cell differentiation. For instance, DKC1-catalyzed pseudouridylation of small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) enhanced the expression of OCT4 and SOX2, which are important transcription factors for the pluripotency and self-renewal of stem cells.<sup><a href="#R39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R39">39</a></sup> Additionally, PUS7-mediated pseudouridylation of tRNA-derived fragments was found to regulate downstream translation and control stem cell fate decision.<sup><a href="#R40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R40">40</a></sup> These findings suggest potential roles of pseudouridine synthases in osteogenesis.</p>
<p id="P16">We next investigated the interrelationship of the epitranscriptomic RWE proteins during osteogenesis (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S4</a>). The hub proteins are ranked in the following order of importance: METTL1, TRMT11, NSUN2, TMRT6, PUS7, TRMT112, TRMT1, WDR4, NAT10, and NSUN6 (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S4b</a>). LC-PRM quantification results of these proteins are shown in <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S4c</a>. These hub proteins with the highest interconnectivity to other nodes in the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network are expected to be functionally the most important. Most hub proteins are associated with RNA methylation. Aside from the aforementioned RNA m<sup>5</sup>C methyltransferases, many other methyltransferases are also on the list. For instance, METTL1/WDR4, which are the major components of human <em>N</em>7-methylguanosine (m<sup>7</sup>G) methyltransferase complex, are essential regulators in ESCs, and their loss impaired self-renewal and neural differentiation of mouse ESCs.<sup><a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">41</a></sup> As the only acetyltransferase in the top 10 hub proteins, NAT10 displays a strong correlation with osteogenic differentiation. In particular, NAT10 could promote osteogenic differentiation of human BMSCs by mediating the formation of <em>N</em><sup>4</sup>-acetylcytidine (ac<sup>4</sup>C) in RUNX2 and gremlin 1 mRNAs.<sup><a href="#R42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R42">42</a>,<a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43">43</a></sup></p>
<p id="P17">To visualize the relationship between the osteogenesis-related proteins and epitranscriptomic RWE proteins, we obtained the osteogenesis gene set from the harmonizome database.<sup><a href="#R44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R44">44</a></sup> Based on the GeneRIF biological term annotations,<sup><a href="#R45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R45">45</a></sup> the osteogenesis gene set harbors 107 genes co-occurring with the biological process term of osteogenesis. The results of our enrichment analysis of the PPI network showed that several pseudouridine synthases, i.e., RPUSD3, RPUSD4, DKC1, and PUS7, are highly connected with the proteins mediated by the MAPK cascade, including BMP2, CDC42, IGF1, IL6, TGFB3, and TNF in the MCODE_2 cluster (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Figure 4A</a>, <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>). BMP2 is a potent osteogenesis activator, where inflammatory mediators IL6 and TNF positively and negatively modulate the BMP2-induced osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs, respectively.<sup><a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">41</a>,<a href="#R46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R46">46</a></sup> Other proteins associated with mRNA methylation and bone development were included in the MCODE_1 cluster with a high connectivity score. For instance, collagen type I <em>α</em>1 and <em>α</em>2 (COL1A1 and COL1A2) are highly correlated, directly or indirectly, with RNA-modifying proteins in the MCODE_1 cluster (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Figure 4B</a>). COL1A1 and COL1A2 are important components of extracellular matrix in humans and are especially abundant in the bone, skin, and connective tissues.<sup><a href="#R47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R47">47</a></sup> Autosomal dominant mutations in <em>COL1A1</em> and <em>COL1A2</em> genes are the major causes of OI, where approximately 85–90% of OI patients carry mutations in these two genes.<sup><a href="#R48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R48">48</a></sup> The association between COL1A1/COL1A2 and RNA-modifying proteins suggests that aberrant expression or activities of RNA-modifying proteins may be associated with OI.</p>

Here, we used previously published RNA-seq data of 10 OI patients for the subsequent analysis. We stratified the RNA-seq data for the patients into the groups of high and low mRNA expression of <em>METTL1</em> using its median value as a cutoff. <p id="P19">Results from the GSEA between stratified RNA-seq data and the hallmark gene sets from MsigDB showed that 34 out of 50 gene sets were up-regulated in phenotype with low expression of <em>METTL1</em> mRNA. Among these up-regulated gene sets, 10 were significantly up-regulated with FDR being &lt; 25%. The interferon <em>α</em> and <em>γ</em> (IFN-<em>α</em> and IFN-<em>γ</em>) response gene sets were the most significantly enriched gene sets (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Figure S6c</a>,<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">d</a>). IFN-<em>α</em> and IFN-<em>γ</em> are cytokines involved in regulating osteoblast differentiation, an important step in osteogenesis, and they are considered as osteoblastogenic cytokines.<sup><a href="#R49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R49">49</a></sup> These findings suggest the role of METTL1 in regulating osteogenesis through the cytokine network.</p></section><section id="S6"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CONCLUSIONS</h2>
<p id="P20">In summary, we conducted temporal profiling of 154 epitranscriptomic RWE proteins during osteogenic differentiation of H9 human ESCs. We were able to quantify the alterations in expression levels of 127 epitranscriptomic RWE proteins throughout the process of osteogenic differentiation in human ESCs. They represent 82% of the RWE proteins in the PRM library, and we found that nearly half of the epitranscriptomic RWE proteins were down-regulated during osteogenic differentiation. GO analysis showed that the down-regulated RWE proteins are mainly associated with RNA methylation and pseudouridylation. PPI network analysis indicated the significance of METTL1 among the down-regulated RWE proteins and a strong association between down-regulated RWE proteins and bone formation. GSEA results of previously published RNA-seq data from OI patients revealed a potential role of METTL1 during osteogenesis. Together, our results unveiled potential regulatory roles of epitranscriptomic RWE proteins during osteogenic differentiation of human H9 ESCs, and our work offered novel insights into the mechanisms of osteogenesis process and may benefit future development of therapeutic approaches for treating osteo-degenerative diseases.</p></section><section id="SM1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
