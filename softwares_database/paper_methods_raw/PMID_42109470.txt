
Methods:
The Kii Fios First Entry system was used in 100 consecutive cases of
renal surgery. The camera is placed within the trocar and advanced
under direct vision through the abdominal wall layers with
simultaneous insufflation. Patient body mass index (BMI), time to
pneumoperitoneum, and complications were recorded.
Results:
The cohort consisted of 59% females, a mean BMI of 31.2 kg/m2,
and 19% with a history of previous abdominal surgery. The primary
indications were pelvic ureteric junction obstruction (49%) and
nonfunctioning kidney (26%). The mean time to
pneumoperitoneum was 29.4 seconds (range 5–180 seconds). First-
attempt success occurred in 90 cases, with 10 cases requiring more
than one attempt. Entries were performed by consultants (65%) and
by trainees (35%). When stratified by operator grade, there was no
significant difference in entry time between consultants and
trainees (median 25 vs 26 seconds; Mann–Whitney P = .35) or in
first-attempt success rates. Prior abdominal surgery did not affect
the success rate of the first attempt. BMI group (<30 vs ≥30) and
American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade were also not
associated with success.
Conclusion:
The Kii Fios First Entry system represents a safe, easy, and
inexpensive alternative for establishing pneumoperitoneum in
laparoscopic surgery, with very short entry times in this series.

INTRODUCTION
Establishing pneumoperitoneum is the first step in laparoscopic
surgery. The 2 most common entry techniques are the closed Veress
needle and the open Hasson method.1,2 Both are widely used but
each has recognized limitations. The Veress technique requires
blind insertion, which carries a risk of visceral or vascular injury,
particularly in obese patients or those with prior abdominal
surgery.3,4 The Hasson open technique provides direct access under
vision, but it is more time-consuming, technically demanding, and
may result in gas leakage during surgery.1 Regardless of the method
employed, complications at the time of entry, although infrequent,
account for a substantial proportion of serious laparoscopic injuries,
highlighting the importance of developing safe and reliable
alternatives.5
Direct optical entry systems with integrated insufflation have been
introduced as an alternative approach. These trocars allow the
laparoscope to be placed within the port and advanced under
continuous vision through the abdominal wall layers, with
insufflation commencing immediately on peritoneal entry. This
technique theoretically combines the safety of open entry with the
speed of closed methods.6–8 Initial reports from general surgery and
gynecology have suggested that direct optical entry is feasible and
safe, with low complication rates and short access times.9–11
However, adoption has been slow, possibly due to limited published
evidence and lack of familiarity among surgeons.
In this study, we describe our institutional experience using the Kii
Fios First Entry system for establishing pneumoperitoneum in
laparoscopic urological surgery. The Kii Fios First Entry system is a
single-use direct optical entry device. The simultaneous insufflation
and nonbladed entry tip further increases the safety of entry. We
report on the safety, efficiency, and outcomes in our first 100
consecutive patients.
Methods
Study design
A retrospective review of a prospectively maintained database of
laparoscopic urological procedures. Between February 2020 and
August 2025, 100 consecutive patients underwent surgery in which
pneumoperitoneum was established using the Kii Fios First Entry
system. All laparoscopic cases during this period were included,
with no exclusions. Ethical approval for this study was obtained
from the institutional research ethics committee, and all patients
provided informed consent for inclusion in the database.
Surgical technique
Patients were positioned in the lateral decubitus position. The
camera port was placed between the umbilicus and the costal
margin, medial to the midclavicular line. The laparoscope was
inserted into the optical trocar (Image 1), which was advanced
under direct vision through the abdominal wall with simultaneous
insufflation (Image 2). After a skin incision, insert the visual
obturator trocar perpendicular to the abdominal wall, holding the
trocar in the dominant hand and the camera in the nondominant
hand. Ensure CO2 insufflation is running throughout insertion.
Advance using gentle pressure with slow rotational movements,
maintaining continuous visualization of each abdominal wall layer.
Proceed cautiously through the peritoneum, advancing slowly to
avoid sudden loss of resistance. On entering the abdominal cavity,
pause to allow insufflation before directing the trocar toward an
open intra-abdominal space to complete positioning.
Pneumoperitoneum was defined as adequate peritoneal distension
with an intra-abdominal pressure of 12–15 mmHg. In the first 10
cases, a 10-mm optical trocar and laparoscope were used;
subsequently, a 5-mm system was adopted to reduce fascial
openings without loss of visualization. Fascial closure was
performed for all 10-mm ports with a single figure of 8°F-0 Vicryl
suture on a J-needleh but was not required for 5-mm ports.

Data collection
Data collected included patient demographics (age, sex, body mass
index [BMI]), operative indication, port size, and time to
pneumoperitoneum. Entry time was defined as the interval between
skin incision and establishment of stable pneumoperitoneum.
Intraoperative complications, including visceral or vascular injury,
were recorded and graded using the Clavien–Dindo system.
Postoperative outcomes included wound infection, port-site
complications, and other early adverse events. Patients were
routinely reviewed at 6 and 12 weeks to assess for port-site
complications.
Statistical analysis
Continuous variables were summarized as mean (standard
deviation) or median (interquartile range), depending on
distribution, and categorical variables as counts and percentages.
Comparisons of entry time between groups were performed using
the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical outcomes were analyzed
using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. A P-value <.05 was considered
statistically significant.
Results
Patient demographics
A total of 100 consecutive patients underwent laparoscopic
urological surgery in which pneumoperitoneum was established
using the Kii Fios First Entry system. The cohort included 59%
females and 41% males, with a mean BMI of 31.2 kg/m2 (range 22–
65 kg/m2). American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grades
were I in 32%, II in 49%, and III in 19% of cases. Nineteen patients
(19%) had undergone previous abdominal surgery (Table 1). The
most frequent surgical indication was pelvi-ureteric junction
obstruction (PUJO), accounting for 49% of cases, followed by
nonfunctioning kidney (26%), renal cell carcinoma (8%),
symptomatic renal cyst (6%), and upper tract urothelial carcinoma
(6%). Less common indications included intra-abdominal testis
(3%), retrocaval ureter (1%), and urachal remnant (1%). Surgery
was performed on the left side in 51 patients (51%), right side in 48
(48%), and midline in 1 (1%).

Entry performance
Pneumoperitoneum was achieved in all cases. Median port size was
5 mm (range 5–10 mm). The mean time to pneumoperitoneum was
29.4 seconds (range 5–180 seconds). First-attempt success occurred
in 90 cases (90%), with 10 (10%) requiring more than one attempt
(maximum 3 attempts) (Table 2). Entries were performed by
consultants (65%) and by trainee (35%). When stratified by
operator grade, there was no significant difference in entry time
between consultants and trainees (median 25 vs 26 seconds; Mann–
Whitney P = .35) or in first-attempt success rates (Figure 1). Prior
abdominal surgery did not affect first-attempt success. BMI group
(<30 vs ≥30) and ASA grade were also not associated with
success.
