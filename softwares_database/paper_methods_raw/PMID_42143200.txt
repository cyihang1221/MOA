
Methods
We used a dynamic two-compartment cardiac phantom
(myocardium and left ventricular [LV] lumen) with adjustable
stroke volume and heart rate. Static and gated acquisitions were
performed on cadmium–zinc–telluride equilibrium radionuclilde
angiocardiography (CZT-ERNA) and gated positron emission
tomography myocardial perfusion imaging (PET-MPI) under
identical volumes and heart-rate settings. Computed tomography
(CT) served as the reference for end-diastolic volume (EDV) and
end-systolic volume (ESV). LVEF was derived from these volumes
for all modalities. We also assessed the impact of phantom position
relative to detectors, as well as the effect of attenuation correction
via a thorax phantom.
Results
CZT-ERNA overestimated static LV volumes relative to CT, whereas
PET-MPI underestimated them. Both modalities underestimated
LVEF compared with CT-derived values, with a larger bias for PET-
MPI. On PET-MPI, attenuation correction produced similar volumes
with and without the thorax phantom. CZT-ERNA volumes
decreased as detector distance increased.
Conclusion
Under controlled phantom conditions, CZT-ERNA and PET-MPI
show inverse biases for LV volumes and both underestimated LVEF
versus CT. These findings point to the influence of reconstruction
and contouring algorithms, attenuation correction, and camera
positioning. The dynamic phantom enables direct comparison of
perfusion- and blood-pool-based measurements and may support
modality-specific calibration when translating absolute values
across techniques.
Keywords: Dynamic cardiac phantom, CZT-ERNA, PET-MPI, Left
ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), Cross-modality comparison
Introduction
Cardiovascular imaging plays an essential role in assessing cardiac
function, particularly left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), in
patients at risk of declining pump function during cancer treatment.
According to the 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidelines
for heart failure, 3D-echocardiography (3DE) or cardiac magnetic
resonance (CMR) are recommended as first-line modalities.
Multigated acquisition (MUGA), also known as equilibrium
radionuclide angiocardiography (ERNA), serves as a third-line
option when 3DE and CMR are unavailable or inconclusive [1].
Cadmium–zinc–telluride ERNA (CZT-ERNA), a type of 3D-MUGA
using cadmium-zinc-telluride detectors, is an efficient tool for serial
LVEF monitoring [2]. In patients, CZT-ERNA shows good
correlation with CMR for assessing both LVEF and right ventricular
ejection fraction (RVEF) [3, 4]. However, LVEF measured by CZT-
ERNA correlates less strongly with 2D-echocardiography [5].
Cardiac phantom studies are valuable for evaluating and ensuring
quality control in nuclear imaging techniques. For instance,
myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) with CZT-detectors or
conventional Anger cameras has been tested for LVEF assessment.
These studies highlight that CZT-MPI provides superior spatial
resolution, sensitivity, and shorter acquisition times [6]. Yet,
conflicting results include larger left ventricular (LV) volumes,
especially end-systolic volume (ESV) [7], and decreased LVEF
compared to Anger cameras [8]. To our knowledge, most cardiac
phantoms estimate LVEF using MPI. Only one study has used blood-
pool imaging with a static phantom to compare positron emission
tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT), single-photon
emission computed tomography-ERNA (SPECT-ERNA), and CMR
[9]. No prior work has directly compared CZT-ERNA and PET-MPI.
The aim of this study is to address this gap by comparing CZT-
ERNA and PET-MPI for the assessment of LVEF using both static
and gated phantom acquisitions.
Background
Cardiac phantoms simulate the anatomy and/or function of the
human heart and can be generally described as static or dynamic.
Static cardiac phantoms are fixed models representing specific
phases of the cardiac cycle, typically end-systole and end-diastole.
They offer greater anatomical accuracy but lack the ability to assess
the effect of motion. Dynamic cardiac phantoms replicate heart
motion at varying volumes and heart rates to simulate the beating
heart. Incorporating thorax phantoms to mimic soft tissue
attenuation further enhances these studies. Cardiac phantoms are
widely used in radiology to compare imaging modalities such as
CMR, computed tomography (CT), and 3DE [10, 11].
Advancements in 3D printing have facilitated the development of
anthropomorphic cardiac phantoms that closely resemble human
anatomy. For example, Kiss et al. [12] created two phantoms from
patient-derived PET data: one depicting healthy myocardium and
another with a perfusion defect. These designs are cost-effective,
quick to produce, and customizable, including features such as 'hot'
backgrounds and liver inserts. Similarly, Verrecchia-Ramos et al.
[9] built static phantom pairs from CMR scans at end-diastole and
end-systole to validate cardiac volumes and LVEF across multiple
imaging modalities. Despite their anatomical precision, static
models limit assessments of cardiac volumes and LVEF by failing to
include effects of cardiac motion.
Dynamic cardiac phantoms such as the Amsterdam-gated cardiac
phantom (AGATE) support validation of gated MPI SPECT. AGATE
allows variable LV volumes with realistic ejection fractions but is
specifically designed for MPI, rendering it unsuitable for gated
blood pool imaging [13]. AGATE has been applied in comparisons
of CZT cameras under dual-isotope conditions [7], evaluation of
LVEF from CZT cameras against conventional gamma cameras [8],
and multicenter studies examining variations in LV function [14].
De Bondt et al. [15, 16] designed dynamic cardiac phantoms
explicitly for gated blood pool imaging, featuring biventricular and
four-chamber models with valves to evaluate LV and right
ventricular (RV) function, though they do not support MPI.
Currently, no phantom enables direct comparison between MPI and
blood-pool imaging for cardiac volumes and LVEF. Our custom-built
phantom fills this void. In this study, we showcase its utility by
comparing volumes and LVEF from CZT-ERNA and gated PET-MPI,
using CT as the reference standard.
Methods
Gated heart phantom
The left ventricle is simulated using two silicone balloons. The outer
lumen represents the myocardium, while the inner lumen simulates
the cavity of the left ventricle. Fluid is continually pumped in and
out using a pneumatic system driven by pressurized air, supplied
through compressed air outlets commonly found in hospitals.
The pneumatic pump consists of a piston housed within a
cylindrical chamber. Pressurized air is alternately supplied to the
top and bottom of the chamber of the pump, driving piston
movement. Its velocity is regulated by two adjustable outlet valves
positioned at either end of the chamber, emulating a cardiac cycle,
characterized by a rapid systolic phase and a slower diastolic phase.
Two reed switches are mounted to the pump housing and are
magnetically activated as the piston approaches them. The switches
control an electromagnetic solenoid valve, switching air supply
between the top or bottom of the pump. The spatial distance
between the reed switches defines the piston stroke length, thereby
determining the simulated cardiac stroke volume.
Piston displacement is monitored by a microswitch, which
generates a standardized 5 V trigger signal, simulating an
electrocardiogram (ECG) trigger. This trigger signal is transmitted
to the imaging system to synchronize data acquisition with the
mechanical cycle.
The air pump piston operates a modified 100 mL plastic syringe
(Pic Solution). The syringe has ports specifically for filling the inner
lumen of the system, while the flange has ports for filling the outer
lumen. The syringe is connected via a rubber tube to the flange, on
which the balloons are attached with O-rings. Activation of the
system moves fluid back and forth from the innermost balloon to
simulate the beating heart. The complete mobile phantom system is
arranged on a rolling table (Fig. 1).

Thorax phantom
A thorax phantom mimicking the attenuation of human tissue for
both 140 kV and CT energies was produced using the filament
deposition modeling (FDM) print method, using a Prusa MK3s 3D
printer and PBT + (Polybutylene Terephthalate) filament. The
filament type was selected for having similar photon attenuation
properties as human tissue.
The phantom consists of two types of tissue, lung tissue and ribcage
tissue, the latter representing both bone and soft tissue. The shape
of the tissue types was segmented on a single lung CT slice and
extrapolated in a straight line along the height of the phantom.
A cavity for the dynamic heart phantom was then cut from the
resulting phantom, allowing for uniform positioning of the heart
within the thorax phantom, with a locking plug to fix the dynamic
heart phantom in place.
To achieve similar attenuation characteristics, sample CT scans
were used and an average HU value for lung (-642 HU) and ribcage
tissue (10 HU) was calculated.
