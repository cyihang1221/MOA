</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ab005"><h2>Graphical abstract</h2>
<p><strong>A proposed model for the targeted regulation of sterol biosynthesis in <em>Neurospora crassa</em>.</strong> Perturbations in different steps of the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway lead to the accumulation of specific sterol intermediates and activate distinct transcription factors to regulate the corresponding ergosterol biosynthesis genes, thereby maintaining sterol homeostasis. The accumulation of specific sterol intermediates is proposed to trigger the activation of the responses.</p>

<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h3>
<p>The synthesis and regulation of ergosterol are vital for fungal growth and stress adaptation. While ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation is a recognized mechanism controlling sterol biosynthesis in fungi, prior research suggests the presence of additional regulatory mechanisms. However, the specifics of the alternative regulatory mechanisms have not been systematically investigated.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objectives</h3>
<p>We proposed that a regulatory network is likely to discern disturbances in sterol biosynthesis and trigger responses accordingly. This study aimed to validate the hypothesis and investigate the regulatory mechanisms.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>Quantitative Real-time PCR and HPLC-MS/MS were used to explore and compare the regulation of sterol biosynthesis in different fungi. Key transcription factors involved in the alternative regulatory mechanism in <em>Neurospora crassa</em> were identified by phenotypic profiling of a transcription factor mutant library. ChIP-qPCR, fluorescence confocal imaging, RNA sequencing, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were used to reveal the mechanism of each transcription factor.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>Unlike the canonical ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation in fungi like <em>C. neoformans</em>, our study demonstrated that the inhibitions of ergosterol biosynthesis at specific steps triggered distinct transcriptional responses of <em>erg</em> genes in fungi, including <em>N. crassa</em> and <em>Aspergillus fumigatus</em>. In <em>N. crassa</em>, the responses were orchestrated by different transcription factors. Specifically, the inhibition of ERG24 and ERG2 activated transcription factors SAH-2 and AtrR, resulting in the upregulation of <em>erg24</em>, <em>erg2</em>, <em>erg25,</em> and <em>erg3</em>. Furthermore, the inhibition of ERG11/CYP51 activated transcription factor NcSR, leading to the upregulation of <em>erg11</em> and <em>erg6</em>. Phenotypic profiles of mutants of various <em>N. crassa erg</em> genes and the aforementioned transcription factors implied that the targeted regulation of ergosterol biosynthesis could fortify fungal viability within complex habitats.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p>Our study reveals a novel regulatory mechanism in fungi: targeted upregulation of specific sterol biosynthesis genes in response to given perturbations in ergosterol biosynthesis, exhibiting a higher degree of precision and sophistication in sterol biosynthesis regulation.</p></section></section><section id="s0005"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0030">Sterols are crucial lipid components of eukaryotic cells, essential for maintaining membrane dynamics and ensuring membrane functions. Deficiencies in sterols can result in impaired membrane fluidity and permeability, the destabilization of membrane microdomains, and incorrect distribution of membrane proteins. These disruptions can affect a range of biological processes, including the cell cycle, cell proliferation, cell–cell communication, mating, vesicle trafficking, and stress responses <a href="#b0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0005">[1]</a>, <a href="#b0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0010">[2]</a>, <a href="#b0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0015">[3]</a>, <a href="#b0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0020">[4]</a>. Ergosterol is the predominant sterol in fungi, analogous to cholesterol in mammals. Its biosynthetic pathway represents a pivotal target for widely used antifungal agents in both clinical and agricultural settings <a href="#b0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0025">[5]</a>.</p>
<p id="p0035">To support normal cellular activities and stress adaptation, cells must maintain sterol homeostasis. A critical mechanism is the transcriptional regulation of sterol biosynthesis genes. In fungi, when ergosterol biosynthesis is inhibited by hypoxia or sterol biosynthesis inhibitors like azoles, key sterol biosynthesis genes such as <em>erg11</em>(<em>CYP51</em>) are transcriptionally upregulated to attenuate the resulting toxicity <a href="#b0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0030">[6]</a>, <a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">[7]</a>. Similar phenomena are observed in mammals <a href="#b0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0040">[8]</a>. Current knowledge attributes these transcriptional responses primarily to several key transcription factors, including SREBP (<span class="text-underline">s</span>terol <span class="text-underline">r</span>egulatory <span class="text-underline">e</span>lement <span class="text-underline">b</span>inding <span class="text-underline">p</span>rotein), Upc2p (<span class="text-underline">s</span>terol <span class="text-underline">u</span>ptake <span class="text-underline">c</span>ontrol protein 2), and FgSR. SREBP is a major regulator of sterol biosynthesis, conserved from mammals to fungi <a href="#b0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0045">[9]</a>. Upc2p serves as a functional homolog of SREBP in <em>Saccharomyces</em> fungi <a href="#b0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0050">[10]</a>, and FgSR is a recently identified transcription factor in <em>Fusarium</em>
<a href="#b0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0055">[11]</a>. Additional transcription factors that regulate sterol biosynthesis have also been reported, such as Ndt80 and Adr in yeast <a href="#b0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0060">[12]</a>, <a href="#b0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0065">[13]</a>, and AtrR and SltA in <em>A. fumigatus</em>
<a href="#b0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0070">[14]</a>, <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>, indicating a complex regulatory network controlling sterol biosynthesis.</p>
<p id="p0040">Ergosterol and cholesterol, the end products of sterol biosynthesis, contribute the most in activating these transcription factors and triggering the expression of sterol biosynthesis genes. For SREBP, the presence of ergosterol or cholesterol anchors it to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane. Upon ergosterol depletion, SREBP detaches from the ER membrane and is cleaved at the Golgi apparatus to release the N-terminal transcription factor domain, which is transferred into the nucleus to activate sterol biosynthesis genes <a href="#b0080" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0080">[16]</a>, <a href="#b0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0085">[17]</a>. Similarly, Upc2p is sequestered in the cytosol by ergosterol and is released upon ergosterol depletion <a href="#b0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0090">[18]</a>, <a href="#b0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0095">[19]</a>. Although not fully understood, FgSR is also believed to be activated by insufficient ergosterol levels <a href="#b0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0055">[11]</a>. Obviously, these examples illustrate a typical end-product-mediated feedback regulation mechanism. Regardless of which step in ergosterol biosynthesis is inhibited, the pathway employs a consistent transcriptional response strategy to address ergosterol depletion.</p>
<p id="p0045">Despite extensive research on ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation, the mechanisms governing the regulation of <em>erg</em> genes across the majority of fungal species remain largely unexplored. Unexpectedly, a previous study in <em>N. crassa</em> revealed that conditions leading to ergosterol depletion do not consistently trigger the upregulation of <em>erg</em> genes <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>. Several <em>erg</em> genes exhibited varied transcriptional responses to different kinds of ergosterol biosynthesis inhibitors in <em>N. crassa</em> and <em>Trichophyton rubrum</em>
<a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>, <a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">[21]</a>, <a href="#b0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0110">[22]</a>. These suggest the presence of additional regulatory mechanisms distinct from ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation. In both fungi and mammals, the biosynthesis of sterols is a complex process involving multiple enzymes. Disruption of a single step in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway leads to the depletion of the final product, ergosterol, and the accumulation of one or more sterol intermediates with differing chemical bonds <a href="#b0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0015">[3]</a>. These intermediates exhibit specific functions within the membrane, rather than indiscriminately disturbing membrane functions, thereby eliciting distinct phenotypes in defined cellular processes <a href="#b0115" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0115">[23]</a>, <a href="#b0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0120">[24]</a>, <a href="#b0125" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0125">[25]</a>. Furthermore, mutants of certain ergosterol biosynthesis genes (<em>erg</em>) are viable, and the severity of growth defects caused by the deletion of different <em>erg</em> genes varies among fungi <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>, <a href="#b0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0130">[26]</a>, <a href="#b0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0135">[27]</a>. Notably, under specific stress conditions such as heat and high glucose, some <em>erg</em> mutants exhibit superior growth compared to the wild-type strain <a href="#b0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0135">[27]</a>, <a href="#b0140" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0140">[28]</a>. These results imply that the cells need to distinguish perturbations at different steps of sterol biosynthesis and mount diverse responses. Therefore, we hypothesize that a regulatory mechanism capable of discerning perturbations in sterol biosynthesis may be integral to governing this process in fungi like <em>N. crassa</em>. However, the existence of this mechanism, and its underlying principles, calls for further detailed investigation.</p>
<p id="p0050">In this study, following a comprehensive investigation, we corroborated that inhibition of ergosterol biosynthesis at discrete steps triggered distinct transcriptional responses of <em>erg</em> genes in not only <em>N. crassa</em> but also <em>A. fumigatus</em>. This underscores the existence of targeted regulation of <em>erg</em> genes in response to specific perturbations in ergosterol biosynthesis. The results that the responses of <em>erg</em> genes are separately activated by different transcription factors not only further support the existence of the mechanism but also provide insights into the underlying regulatory networks. This finely tuned regulatory mechanism allows fungi to adapt effectively to complex environments, enhancing their survival strategies. In brief, our study illuminates a sophisticated regulatory framework for fungal sterol biosynthesis that deviates from the canonical ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation, shedding new light on the maintenance of sterol homeostasis.</p></section><section id="s0010"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="s0015"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Strains and growth conditions</h3>
<p id="p0055">The strains used in this study and their sources are listed in <a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">File S1</a>. Vogel’s medium (supplemented with 2% sucrose for slants, and with 2% glucose for plates and liquid culture) was used to culture <em>Neurospora crassa</em> strains and solidified with 1.5% agar as required. Sorbose plates (Vogel’s medium supplemented with 2% sorbose, 0.05% fructose, and 0.05% glucose) were employed for electroporation transformation. All cultures were grown at 28°C. <em>Aspergillus fumigatus</em> strains were cultured on the complete medium (CM) at 37°C, solidified with 1.5% agar <a href="#b0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0145">[29]</a>. 5 mM uridine and uracil were added when <em>A. fumigatus</em> CEA17 or the revertant strain was grown. <em>Cryptococcus neoformans</em> H99 was cultured at 30°C on yeast extract-peptone-dextrose (YPD) medium, solidified with 1.5% agar <a href="#b0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0150">[30]</a>.</p></section><section id="s0020"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Total RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR</h3>
<p id="p0060">For <em>N. crassa</em>, the samples for RNA extraction were performed as previously described <a href="#b0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0155">[31]</a>. Sterol biosynthesis inhibitors, including ketoconazole (Sigma-Aldrich, United States), terbinafine (J&amp;K, China), and amorolfine (TCI, Japan), were added at final concentrations of 1.5, 2.5, and 0.375 μg/mL, respectively. Subsequently, the obtained mycelium was harvested and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen.</p>
<p id="p0065">For <em>A. fumigatus</em>, fresh conidia at a final concentration of 1×10<sup>6</sup> were inoculated into 100 mL liquid CM medium and grown at 37°C and 200 rpm for 12 h. Subsequently, the mycelium was treated with sterol biosynthesis inhibitors for 6 h before harvesting. Sterol biosynthesis inhibitors include ketoconazole, terbinafine, and amorolfine, with final concentrations of 0.25, 1.8, and 10 μg/mL, respectively. Due to the impact on sporulation in the <em>A. fumigatus erg5</em><sup>KO</sup> strain, 10 mycelial plugs (approximately 1–2 mm<sup>2</sup>) from the unsporulated edges of colonies grown on plates replaced the conidial suspension. The CEA17<sup>COM</sup> strain was used as a control for the <em>A. fumigatus erg5</em><sup>KO</sup> strain and subjected to the same procedure.</p>
<p id="p0070">For <em>C. neoformans</em>, fresh cells at a final concentration of 1×10<sup>6</sup> were inoculated into 100 mL liquid YPD medium and grown at 30°C and 200 rpm for 18 h, and then treated with sterol biosynthesis inhibitors for 12 h. The cells were harvested by centrifuge and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Sterol biosynthesis inhibitors include ketoconazole, terbinafine, and amorolfine, with final concentrations of 4, 10, and 20 μg/mL, respectively.</p>
<p id="p0075">Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR were performed as previously described <a href="#b0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0155">[31]</a>. Gene-specific primers were designed and listed in <a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">File S1</a>. The expression level of each <em>erg</em> gene was normalized by the expression level of <em>β-tubulin</em> in <em>N. crassa</em>, <em>actin</em> in <em>A. fumigatus</em>, and <em>TEF1</em> in <em>C. neoformans</em>. Error bars represent the standard error of the mean, n = 3.</p></section><section id="s0025"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">RNA-seq and data analysis</h3>
<p id="p0080">The transcriptomic profiles of <em>N. crassa</em> strains were analyzed by high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Sample preparation and RNA extraction were conducted as described above. The total RNA samples underwent RNA quality control, sequencing library construction, single-end RNA-sequencing (BGIseq-500RS platform), and data processing at the Beijing Genomics Institute (Wuhan, China). The reference genomic sequences of <em>N. crassa</em> were obtained from FungiDB. Genes with FPKM values &lt; 5 in all samples were considered low-abundance genes and were removed before further analysis. Differentially expressed genes were identified based on expression changes between two samples &gt; 2 or &lt; 0.5, with a q-value (adjusted <em>P</em>-value) &lt; 0.05. Functional category enrichment analysis and pathway enrichment analysis were performed using DAVID (<a href="https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp</a>).</p>
<p id="p0085">Gene set enrichment analysis was conducted using GSEA 4.3.2. The expression dataset of all detected genes for each mutant strain was imported and analyzed in GSEA with 10,000 permutations to identify enriched pathways. The enriched pathways with normalized enrichment score (NES) greater than 1 and adjusted <em>P</em>-value (adj. <em>P</em>) less than 0.05 were considered significant.</p></section><section id="s0030"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">HPLC-MS/MS analysis of sterol profiles</h3>
<p id="p0090">Samples for sterol extraction were prepared in the same manner as for RNA extraction. Subsequently, the mycelial samples were lyophilized for 24 h, followed by grinding into fine powder for sterol extraction and HPLC-MS/MS analysis, as previously described <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>. An ergosterol standard (J&amp;K, China) was utilized as a control. Posaconazole (Macklin, China) at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL was added as an internal reference during the sterol extraction procedure for subsequent calculation of the relative content of the sterol fraction.</p></section><section id="s0035"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Construction of knockout strains of <em>N. crassa</em>
</h3>
<p id="p0095">Construction of double knockout strains of <em>N. crassa</em> was achieved through sexual crossing. The sexual crossing procedure followed methods described in a previous study <a href="#b0160" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0160">[32]</a>. The Homokaryons of <em>N. crassa</em> were purified from initial heterokaryotic strains using microconidial isolation <a href="#b0165" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0165">[33]</a>.</p>
<p id="p0100">Deletion of <em>ncSR</em> was performed with homologous recombination method, using hygromycin B resistance gene <em>hph</em> as the selective marker. Briefly, primers were used to amplify the promoter of the <em>ncSR</em> gene (510-bp), the coding sequence of gene <em>hph</em> (1450-bp), the terminator of the <em>ncSR</em> gene (619-bp), and the plasmid backbone fragment. These fragments were then assembled into the plasmid pCSN-KO-NcSR using the One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China). The knockout fragment, comprising the <em>ncSR</em> gene promoter, the <em>hph</em> fragment, and the <em>ncSR</em> gene terminator, was amplified with primers T7/3 and purified via gel extraction. The purified knockout fragment was introduced into <em>N. crassa</em> N85 (<em>bd Ku70<sup>RIP</sup></em>) strain via electroporation <a href="#b0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0170">[34]</a>. Transformants were screened by cultivation on Vogel’s slants containing 150 μg/mL hygromycin B (Amresco, United States) and verified by PCR. Subsequently, the obtained deletion mutant was crossed with the <em>N. crassa</em> wild-type strain (FGSC#2225) to eliminate the genetic background of <em>bd</em> and <em>Ku70<sup>RIP</sup></em> mutations. All primers used to construct and verify the <em>ncSR</em> knockout strain were listed in <a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">File S1</a>.</p></section><section id="s0040"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Deletion of <em>erg5</em> in <em>A. fumigatus</em>
</h3>
<p id="p0105">The deletion of <em>erg5</em> (Afu1g03950) in <em>A. fumigatus</em> was achieved by gene replacement via double homologous recombination events using the orotidine-5′-phosphate decarboxylase gene (<em>pyrG</em>) as a selectable marker <a href="#b0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0145">[29]</a>. Briefly, primers were used to amplify several fragments: the promoter of the Af<em>erg5</em> gene (1616-bp), the coding sequence of gene <em>pyrG</em> (3881-bp), the terminator of Af<em>erg5</em> gene (1599-bp), and the plasmid backbone fragment. These fragments were assembled into the plasmid pCSN-KO-AfERG5 using the One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China). The knockout fragment, comprising the Af<em>erg5</em> gene promoter, the <em>pyrG</em> fragment, and the Afe<em>rg5</em> gene terminator, was amplified with T7/3 primers and purified through gel extraction. Subsequently, this knockout fragment was introduced into the <em>A. fumigatus</em> uracil-auxotrophic strain (CEA17) via protoplast transformation. Positive transformants were selected based on their auxotrophic phenotype and verified by PCR. All primers used for construction and validation are listed in <a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">File S1</a>.</p></section><section id="s0045"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Construction of relevant complementation strains in <em>N. crassa</em>
</h3>
<p id="p0110">The plasmid pCB1532, containing the chlorimuron ethyl resistance gene, was utilized to construct plasmids pCOM-sGFP-SAH2, pCOM-AtrR-sGFP, and pCOM-NcSR, intended for complementing <em>sah-2</em><sup>KO</sup>, <em>atrR</em><sup>KO</sup>, and <em>ncSR</em><sup>KO</sup> strains, respectively. Briefly, specific fragments were amplified and purified: the 2,162-bp upstream regulatory region of <em>sah-2</em>, the 717-bp sGFP coding sequence, and a 4,709-bp fragment encompassing the <em>sah-2</em> coding sequence and its downstream region. These fragments were inserted into pCB1532 digested by XbaI and KpnI through the One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China), resulting in plasmid pCOM-sGFP-SAH2. Similarly, a 5,777-bp fragment containing the upstream regulatory region and the coding sequence of <em>atrR</em>, a 747-bp sGFP coding sequence, and a 1,524-bp downstream region of <em>atrR</em> were assembled into pCB1532 using the same cloning method, producing plasmid pCOM-AtrR-sGFP. For pCOM-NcSR, a 5,413-bp fragment containing the <em>ncSR</em> coding sequence flanked by its upstream and downstream regions was amplified and inserted into pCB1532 digested with XpnI and KpnI by a one-step cloning kit.</p>
<p id="p0115">Likewise, pCB1532 was employed in the construction of the plasmid pCOM-Tef1-sGFP-NcSR-TrpC for generating the sGFP-NcSR strain. Briefly, the 923-bp promoter sequence of the <em>tef-1</em>, the 783-bp sGFP coding sequence, the 1,668-bp coding sequence <em>ncSR</em>, and the 774-bp terminator of the <em>trpC</em> were amplified, purified, and inserted into the pCB1532 digested with XbaI and KpnI through a one-step cloning kit.</p>
<p id="p0120">The pCOM-sGFP-SAH2, pCOM-AtrR-sGFP, pCOM-NcSR, and pCOM-Tef1-sGFP-NcSR-TrpC were introduced into their respective knockout strains using the electroporation method, as previously described <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>. Positive transformants were selected on the medium supplemented with 15 μg/mL chlorimuron ethyl (Sigma-Aldrich, United States) and were further confirmed by PCR. All primers used for construction and validation were listed in <a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">File S1</a>.</p></section><section id="s0050"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Phenotypic analysis of mutants</h3>
<p id="p0125">The susceptibility test for <em>N. crassa</em> strains followed established procedures <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>. Briefly, two microliters of the conidial suspension (2×10<sup>6</sup> conidia/mL) were inoculated onto Vogel’s medium plates, with or without inhibitors. The inhibitors used included 2.5 µg/mL terbinafine, 1.5 µg/mL ketoconazole, 0.375 µg/mL amorolfine, 1 µg/mL lovastatin (Sigma-Aldrich, United States), 0.5 µg/mL voriconazole (Selleck, United States), 0.04 µg/mL amphotericin B (Beyotime, China), 1 µg/mL caspofungin (APExBIO, United States), 100 µg/mL polyoxinB (DR.E, Germany), 20 ng/mL myriocin (Enzo, United States), 75 µg/mL rotenone (Sigma-Aldrich, United States), 75 ng/mL oligomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, United States), 2 mM dithiothreitol (Sigma-Aldrich, United States), 0.5 µg/mL menadione (Selleck, United States). All the plates were incubated at 28°C for at least 24 h. Similarly, <em>A. fumigatus</em> conidia (2×10<sup>6</sup> conidia/mL) were inoculated onto CM plates, with or without inhibitors, followed by incubation at 37°C. <em>C. neoformans</em> cells from fresh liquid cultures were washed and diluted to 10<sup>7</sup>, 10<sup>6</sup>, 10<sup>5</sup>, and 10<sup>4</sup> cells/mL. Two microliters of the cell suspension were inoculated onto YPD plates containing or lacking inhibitors and incubated at 30°C. To facilitate a more accurate comparison and microscopic observation of <em>N. crassa</em> growth under inhibitory conditions, an extended incubation period was employed.</p>
<p id="p0130">Minimal inhibitory concentrations (MIC) were determined by microliquid-based dilution according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M38-A2 protocol. A final concentration of 2 × 10<sup>4</sup> conidia/mL spore suspension was incubated with varying concentrations of sterol biosynthesis inhibitor for 24 h at 28 °C in Vogel's liquid medium. The status of spore germination was assessed microscopically, supplemented by 10 % Alamarblue (Invitrogen, United States) for cell viability. MIC values were determined per the standard established by CLSI. The experiments were repeated three times independently.</p>
<p id="p0135">For fluorescent microscopic observation of GFP-tag strains, spore suspensions (2 × 10<sup>6</sup> conidia/mL) were germinated for 5 h at 28 °C, 200 rpm. Subsequently, the germinated spores were treated with 1.5 µg/mL ketoconazole or 0.375 µg/mL amorolfine for 2–3 h, followed by staining with Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, China) for 10 min. After staining, the germlings were washed thrice with liquid Vogel's minimal medium before fluorescent microscopic observation.</p>
<p id="p0140">To evaluate sporulation capacity, a 50 µL spore suspension (2×10<sup>6</sup> conidia/mL) was inoculated into slants containing 2 mL Vogel's medium and cultured in darkness for 9 days. Subsequently, formed spores were washed and counted. Phenotypes of sexual development were analyzed as previously described <a href="#b0160" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0160">[32]</a>.</p></section><section id="s0055"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Drug sensitivity phenotypic profiling of collected <em>N. crassa</em> transcription factor mutants</h3>
<p id="p0145">The drug sensitivity phenotypic profiling of transcription factor mutants to sterol biosynthesis inhibitors was analyzed by referring to the broth dilution method. Briefly, a spore suspension with a final concentration of 2×10<sup>4</sup> conidia/mL was incubated in Vogel's liquid medium containing sterol biosynthesis inhibitors at 28°C for 36 h, followed by an additional 4 h of incubation with 10% Alamarblue. Ketoconazole concentrations used in the screen were 0.75 µg/mL, 1.5 µg/mL, and 3 µg/mL, representing 0.5, 1, and 2 times the MIC, respectively. Similarly, amorolfine concentrations were set at 0.1875 µg/mL, 0.375 µg/mL, and 0.75 µg/mL, and terbinafine concentrations were set at 1.25 µg/mL, 2.5 µg/mL, and 5 µg/mL. Conidia cultured in Vogel's liquid medium without inhibitors were used as a blank control. The experiment was performed in duplicate.</p></section><section id="s0060"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR analysis</h3>
<p id="p0150">ChIP was performed following a previously described protocol with some modifications <a href="#b0175" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0175">[35]</a>. Briefly, Immunoprecipitation was carried out using the rabbit polyclonal anti-GPF antibody (ab290, Abcam, UK; 1:500 dilution) and protein G magnetic beads (P2106, Beyotime, China). Immunoprecipitation using rabbit IgG magnetic beads (P2173, Beyotime, China) was used as a control. After washing with low salt buffer, high salt buffer, LiCl buffer, and TE buffer, DNA was eluted from the magnetic beads and then precipitated by glycogen after de-crosslinking, RNase A treatment, and protease K digestion. The DNA solution was subsequently analyzed using quantitative PCR, employing primers (<a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">File S1</a>) specifically designed for the targeted regions within the promoters. The fragment in coding sequences of the gene served as the control region for the analysis. ChIP-qPCR was performed three times.</p></section></section><section id="s0065"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="s0070"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sterol biosynthesis genes respond differently to ergosterol biosynthesis inhibitors in <em>Neurospora crassa</em>
</h3>
<p id="p0155">To test our hypothesis, we conducted further investigations informed by our previous results in <em>N. crassa</em> that several <em>erg</em> genes exhibited varied transcriptional responses to different kinds of ergosterol biosynthesis inhibitors <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>. We first analyzed genome-wide gene expression profiles of <em>N. crassa</em> treated with three distinct sterol biosynthesis inhibitors (SBIs): terbinafine (TERB) targeting squalene epoxidase ERG1, ketoconazole (KTC) targeting sterol C14 demethylase ERG11, and amorolfine (AMO) targeting sterol C14 reductase ERG24/sterol C8 isomerase ERG2. Each SBI inhibited the hyphal extension of the wild-type <em>N. crassa</em> strain at the minimum inhibitory concentration (<a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S1</a>A). Sterol profiling revealed that each SBI reduced ergosterol content and led to the accumulation of specific sterol intermediates (<a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S1</a>B).</p>
<p id="p0160">RNA-seq analysis revealed diverse genome-wide transcriptional changes induced by these inhibitors (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>A). Unexpectedly, despite the unique effects of each SBI, a substantial number of genes (893 genes) were commonly affected by all three inhibitors (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>B). GO enrichment analysis suggests that these genes likely correspond to general stress responses, possibly related with impaired membrane functions due to abnormal sterol biosynthesis (<a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">File S2</a>). Except that, a series of genes were found to be specifically affected by each SBI (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>B). Sterol biosynthesis genes, which are required for adaptation to sterol biosynthesis inhibition, were enriched among genes specifically upregulated by ketoconazole or amorolfine, as revealed by pathway enrichment analysis (<a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S2</a> and <a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">File S2</a>), indicating differential responses of <em>erg</em> genes to distinct inhibitors. KTC treatment dramatically induced the expression of <em>erg11</em>, <em>erg6,</em> and <em>erg24</em>, while AMO treatment significantly induced <em>erg24</em>, <em>erg2</em>, <em>erg25,</em> and <em>erg3</em> (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>C). In contrast, TERB treatment did not induce these <em>erg</em> genes. These results were further confirmed by qRT-PCR (<a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S1</a>C). For comparison with responses typically seen in ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation, we also analyzed the responses of <em>erg</em> genes to the three SBIs in <em>Cryptococcus neoformans</em>. As with <em>N. crassa</em>, the growth of wild-type <em>C. neoformans</em> strain was inhibited by SBIs, resulting in reduced ergosterol content and the accumulation of sterol intermediates (<a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S1</a>D and E). However, not like in <em>N. crassa</em>, treatments with different SBIs induced consistent transcriptional responses of <em>erg</em> genes (<a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S1</a>F), representing the canonical ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation.</p>

<p id="p0170">We previously demonstrated that the knockdown of <em>erg11</em> dramatically induced <em>erg6</em> and <em>erg24</em>, resembling the effect of KTC treatment <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>. To further confirm whether the transcriptional responses of the aforementioned <em>erg</em> genes are directly associated with the inhibition of ergosterol biosynthesis, we investigated the expression of <em>erg</em> genes in the deletion mutants of <em>erg24</em> and <em>erg2</em>, which encode the targets of AMO. Similar to AMO treatment, deletion of <em>erg24</em> (or <em>erg2</em>) significantly upregulated <em>erg2</em> (or <em>erg24</em>), <em>erg25</em>, <em>erg3A</em>, and <em>erg3B</em>, but not <em>erg6</em>, while <em>erg11</em> was only slightly induced in <em>erg24</em> or <em>erg2</em> mutants (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>E). Additional examination was conducted in the deletion mutants of <em>erg3</em> (where both <em>erg3A</em> and <em>erg3B</em> were deleted), <em>erg4</em>, and <em>erg5</em> (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>E). Only <em>erg11</em> and <em>erg1</em> showed a modest upregulation, reaching a maximum of approximately 3 folds in <em>erg5</em> mutant or <em>erg4</em> mutant. None of the tested sterol biosynthesis genes exhibited differential expression in the <em>erg3</em> mutant compared to the wild-type strain. Overall, disturbing various steps in ergosterol biosynthesis in <em>N. crassa</em>, either by chemical inhibitors or through <em>erg</em> gene deletions, can elicit distinct transcriptional responses of <em>erg</em> genes.</p></section><section id="s0080"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">The transcriptional regulation of <em>erg</em> genes in <em>N. crassa</em> involves distinct transcription factors</h3>
<p id="p0175">Different transcriptional responses to diverse SBIs hint that distinct key transcription factors underlay the regulation of ergosterol biosynthesis (<a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S4</a>A). Based on this presumption, we screened a collection of 258 <em>N. crassa</em> transcription factor deletion mutants to identify key transcription factors for drug sensitivity against the three SBIs used in our study, using the broth dilution method as outlined in the schematic diagram (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>B). As expected, distinct transcription factor knockout mutants indeed exhibited altered drug sensitivity to different sterol biosynthesis inhibitors (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>A and <a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">File S3</a>). Among them, <em>sah-2</em> (NCU04731), <em>atrR</em> (NCU01478), and <em>ncSR</em> (NCU08594) garnered attention due to their most prominent mutant phenotypes. More interestingly, SAH-2 is the SREBP homolog in <em>N. crassa</em>, AtrR is the homolog of <em>A. fumigatus</em> AtrR, and NcSR is the homolog of FgSR. Deletion of <em>sah-2</em> or <em>atrR</em> resulted in a hypersensitive phenotype to AMO, rendering the strain nonviable upon AMO treatment with no hyphal growth (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>C). The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for each strain to AMO was 4–8 times or 5–10 times lower than that of the WT, respectively (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>D). However, their deletion did not alter the sensitivity to KTC and TERB. Consistently, the knockout of <em>sah-2</em> or <em>atrR</em> significantly affected the responses of <em>erg24</em> and <em>erg2</em> to AMO, as well as KTC, but did not impact the response of <em>erg11</em> (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>E and F). In contrast, the deletion of <em>ncSR</em> resulted in a hypersensitive phenotype to KTC but not to AMO and TERB (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>C). The MIC for <em>ncSR</em> deletion mutant to KTC was 4–10 times lower than that of WT (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>D). Gene expression analysis showed that the deletion of <em>ncSR</em> abolished the response of <em>erg11</em> to KTC without affecting the expression of <em>erg24</em> and <em>erg2</em> (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>G).</p>

As revealed by ChIP-qPCR, AtrR exhibited binding affinity to the promoters of <em>erg24</em> and <em>erg2</em>, with enhanced binding upon AMO treatment (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>B). Similar to SAH-2, a decrease in basal expression of <em>erg24</em> was observed in <em>atrR</em><sup>KO</sup> strain, consistent with the capacity of AtrR to bind to the <em>erg24</em> promoter under normal conditions (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>F). Unlike SAH-2, AtrR predominantly localized in the cytosol under normal conditions and translocated to the nucleus upon AMO treatment (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>D), underscoring its role as a putative primary transcription factor responsive to AMO.</p>
<p id="p0205">However, the expression of <em>erg24</em> remained upregulated following AMO treatment in both <em>sah-2</em><sup>KO</sup> and <em>atrR</em><sup>KO</sup> strains (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>F). Given a coordinated regulation of SREBP and AtrR in gene expression in <em>A. fumigatus</em>
<a href="#b0185" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0185">[37]</a>, we postulated that similar coordination occurs in the regulation of <em>erg24</em> expression in <em>N. crassa</em>. Consequently, we constructed the double mutant of <em>sah-2</em> and <em>atrR</em>. Plate drug sensitivity assay revealed that the double mutant exhibited increased sensitivity to AMO compared to the single deletion mutants (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>E). Analysis of gene expression demonstrated a complete loss of <em>erg24</em> response to AMO in the double mutant (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>F). These results suggest a collaborative role of AtrR and SAH-2 in the transcriptional regulation of <em>erg24</em> in <em>N. crassa</em>.</p>
<p id="p0210">To further understand the regulatory relationship of these two transcription factors, we conducted a transcriptome analysis of <em>atrR</em><sup>KO</sup> and <em>sah-2</em><sup>KO</sup> strains. The GSEA analysis showed that while distinct biological processes were enriched in the <em>atrR<sup>KO</sup></em> and <em>sah-2<sup>KO</sup></em> strains, ‘Ergosterol biosynthesis’ emerged as a shared pathway in both strains under normal and AMO-treated conditions (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>A, B and <a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S8</a>). This further indicates that the two transcription factors possess different functions but collaborate to regulate the response of <em>erg</em> genes. Focusing on ergosterol biosynthesis genes, we found that AtrR and SAH-2 are indispensable for the transcriptional responses of not only <em>erg24</em> and <em>erg2</em> but also <em>erg25</em>, <em>erg3A,</em> and <em>erg3B</em> (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>C). Further, ChIP-qPCR experiments showed a clear enrichment of both SAH-2 and AtrR in the promoter regions of <em>erg25</em>, <em>erg3A</em>, and <em>erg3B</em> (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>D and E). Interestingly, AtrR was more enriched in promoter regions of these <em>erg</em> genes under AMO treatment, suggesting a more dominant role for AtrR in the regulation of induced expression. To gain a more profound understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of the two transcription factors, we utilized the MEME program to predict binding motifs in the promoter regions of <em>erg24</em>, <em>erg2</em>, <em>erg25</em>, <em>erg3A</em>, and <em>erg3B</em>, which were significantly enriched by SAH-2 and AtrR in ChIP-qPCR experiments. We predicted two potential motifs that resemble the reported binding sequences of AtrR <a href="#b0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0070">[14]</a> and SREBP <a href="#b0190" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0190">[38]</a> in <em>A. fumigatus</em>, respectively (<a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S7</a>B), indicating that AtrR and SAH-2 modulate gene expression together via preserved binding motifs.</p>

<p id="p0215">Our results suggest that the inhibition of different steps in sterol biosynthesis triggers distinct stress responses, which are likely linked to the accumulated sterol intermediates. To comprehensively understand the physiological functions of different sterols and provide further insights into the sophisticated regulation, we characterized the phenotypes of available <em>erg</em> mutants, including <em>erg24</em>, <em>erg2</em>, <em>erg3</em>, <em>erg4,</em> and <em>erg5</em> (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a> and <a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S9</a>), in which ergosterol is absent and specific sterol intermediates accumulated <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>, <a href="#b0125" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0125">[25]</a>, <a href="#b0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0195">[39]</a>. All analyzed mutants exhibited increased resistance to ergosterol-binding agent Amphotericin B and general cell membrane stress-inducing agent SDS, and increased sensitivity to sterol biosynthesis inhibitor azoles and sphingolipid biosynthesis inhibitor myriocin, confirming the absence of ergosterol and membrane perturbations (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>B). Additionally, the deletion of these genes resulted in hypersensitivity to the ATP synthetase inhibitor oligomycin and the oxidative stress inducer H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>B). Except that, the deletion of different <em>erg</em> genes led to varied growth, development, and stress resistance phenotypes. For instance, <em>erg24</em>, <em>erg3,</em> and <em>erg5</em> mutants were hypersensitive to AMO, while <em>erg2</em> and <em>erg4</em> mutants displayed resistance (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>B). The production of conidia was reduced in <em>erg2</em> and <em>erg3</em> mutants but increased in <em>erg5</em> mutant (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>C). Additional phenotypic differences could be found under other conditions, including exposure to cell wall biosynthesis inhibitors, low oxygen stress, and osmotic stress (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>B). These observations imply that different sterols are necessary for growth under specific environmental conditions, and the targeted regulation of ergosterol biosynthesis may potentiate fungal survival across diverse environmental challenges. Consistent with this notion, we discovered that NcSR was exclusively essential for resistance to inhibition of sterol C-14 demethylase but not for other stresses tested, while SAH-2 and AtrR were only required for resistance to the hypoxia mimicking agent CoCl<sub>2</sub> (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>D and E). In line with this, mutants of <em>erg24</em> and <em>erg2</em>, whose expressions are regulated by SAH-2 and AtrR, exhibited hypersensitivity to CoCl<sub>2</sub> (<a href="#f0030" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>B).</p>

<p id="p0220">Given that our findings in <em>N. crassa</em> unveil a previously uncharted mechanism of sterol biosynthesis regulation, it is important to extend our investigation to other fungi. Since the most notable and readily accessible characteristic is the differential response of <em>erg</em> genes to various inhibitors in sterol biosynthesis, we examined the responses of <em>erg</em> genes to diverse SBIs in <em>A. fumigatus</em>. In <em>A. fumigatus</em>, treatment with SBIs at sub-inhibitory concentrations resulting in significant growth inhibition on plates led to a reduction in ergosterol and the accumulation of specific sterol intermediates, resembling the case in <em>N. crassa</em> (<a href="#f0035" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a>A, B and <a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S10</a>). As expected, the transcriptional response of <em>erg</em> genes differed across treatments. All three SBIs induced the expression of <em>erg24A</em>, with varying levels of induction, the highest being observed with AMO treatment. Except for <em>erg24A</em>, TERB treatment did not induce the expression of tested genes. In contrast, KTC treatment induced the expression of <em>erg1</em>, <em>erg7B</em>, <em>erg7C</em>, <em>erg11A</em>, <em>erg11B</em>, <em>erg6</em>, and <em>erg3B</em> by at least 2-fold, and AMO induced only <em>erg7B</em>, <em>erg2</em>, and <em>erg4A</em>, indicating targeted responses (<a href="#f0035" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a>B). As a complementary assay, we examined the expression of <em>erg</em> genes in an <em>A. fumigatus erg5</em> knockout mutant, which is unable to synthesize ergosterol normally and accumulate sterol intermediates ergosta-5,7-dienol and ergosta-5,7,24(28)-trienol (<a href="#f0035" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a>C and D). By qRT-PCR, no significant upregulation in examined <em>erg</em> genes like <em>erg11</em>, <em>erg24</em>, and <em>erg2</em> was observed in the <em>erg5</em><sup>KO</sup> strain (<a href="#f0035" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a>E), implying that the upregulation of certain <em>erg</em> genes requires inhibitions at specific sterol biosynthesis steps. Thus, the targeted regulation of ergosterol biosynthesis is also present in other fungi like <em>A. fumigatus</em>.</p>

<p id="p0225">Understanding how sterol homeostasis is achieved through the transcriptional regulation of sterol biosynthesis genes holds broad implications for cellular physiology, fungal pathogen control, and evolutionary biology. Prior studies in fungi and mammals have firmly established the well-documented end-product feedback regulation of sterol biosynthesis, in which the upregulation of <em>erg</em> genes is triggered by a key transcription factor upon ergosterol/cholesterol depletion <a href="#b0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0045">[9]</a>, <a href="#b0200" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0200">[40]</a>. In yeast, for example, conditions, such as hypoxia, sterol biosynthesis inhibitors, and <em>erg</em> gene disruptions, can activate Upc2p, inducing the expression of <em>erg11</em>, <em>erg2</em>, <em>erg25</em>, <em>erg5,</em> and others <a href="#b0205" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0205">[41]</a>, <a href="#b0210" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0210">[42]</a>, <a href="#b0215" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0215">[43]</a>. While it has been believed that ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation of sterol biosynthesis is conserved across fungi, our study reveals that <em>erg</em> genes can be separately regulated by multiple transcription factors in response to perturbations at different steps of sterol biosynthesis (<a href="#f0040" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a>). Given that target overexpression is a major mechanism for antifungal resistance <a href="#b0220" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0220">[44]</a>, our results indicate that fungi can selectively induce the expression of required <em>erg</em> genes rather than all of them, hinting at a more intelligent regulation strategy. Interestingly, the genetic disruption of <em>erg3</em>, <em>erg4,</em> or <em>erg5</em> in the late steps of ergosterol biosynthesis, which had a slight or even negligible impact on growth, exhibited a minimal effect on the expression of examined <em>erg</em> genes (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>E, 6A). This suggests that fungi only need to address defects that pose a significant threat to their survival by modulating sterol biosynthesis. More importantly, differential responses of <em>erg</em> genes to different SBIs are also observed in <em>A. fumigatus</em> in our study, as well as <em>T. rubrum</em> in previous studies <a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">[21]</a>, <a href="#b0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0110">[22]</a>, confirming the widespread nature of this sophisticated regulatory mechanism.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="f0040"><h3 class="obj_head">Fig. 8.</h3>

<figcaption><p><strong>A proposed model for the targeted regulation of sterol biosynthesis in <em>Neurospora crassa</em>.</strong> Perturbations at different steps of the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway activate distinct transcription factors to regulate corresponding ergosterol biosynthesis genes for maintaining sterol homeostasis. In <em>N. crassa</em>, inhibition of sterol C14 demethylase ERG11 by KTC activates NcSR, leading to the upregulation of <em>erg11</em> and <em>erg6</em>. On the other hand, inhibition of sterol C14 reductase ERG24 and sterol C8 isomerase ERG2 by AMO activate SAH-2 and AtrR, resulting in the upregulation of <em>erg24</em>, <em>erg2</em>, <em>erg25</em>, and <em>erg3</em>. The accumulation of specific sterol intermediates is proposed to trigger the activation of the responses. Draw by FigDraw.</p></figcaption></figure><p id="p0230">It is evident from our aggregated data that finely tuned regulatory mechanisms according to inhibited steps in sterol biosynthesis are present in particular fungal strains. Although the role of ergosterol depletion has not been definitively ruled out, the observed responses could be predominantly attributed to the accumulation of specific sterol intermediates. Different SBI treatments resulted in the accumulation of distinct sterol intermediates along with ergosterol depletion in fungi (<a href="#f0035" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a> and <a href="#s0130" class="usa-link">Fig. S1</a>). In line with this, <em>N. crassa</em> and <em>A. fumigatus</em> exhibited variable transcriptional responses of <em>erg</em> genes to different SBI treatments. Typical ergosterol feedback regulation in fungi like <em>C. neoformans</em> yields a uniform transcriptional response of <em>erg</em> genes. Interestingly, recent studies on <em>A. fumigatus</em> also suggest that the accumulation of particular intermediates like eburicol is involved in the toxicity of azoles and the activation of a specific protein Hmg1 <a href="#b0225" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0225">[45]</a>, <a href="#b0230" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0230">[46]</a>, <a href="#b0235" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0235">[47]</a>. Sterol intermediates are believed to activate cellular responses <a href="#b0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0120">[24]</a>, <a href="#b0240" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0240">[48]</a>, <a href="#b0245" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0245">[49]</a>. In mammals and yeast, the accumulation of sterol intermediate lanosterol promotes the degradation of HMG CoA reductase, an enzyme upstream in the sterol biosynthesis pathway <a href="#b0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0045">[9]</a>, suggesting that sterol intermediates can regulate sterol biosynthesis. However, it remains largely unknown which sterol intermediates are responsible for triggering the responses observed in our study. KTC treatment results in the accumulation of 14-methyl sterols with defective chemical bonds at C14, C7, C24, and C22, while AMO treatment leads to defects at C14 and C7. The shared C14 and C7 defects may explain why KTC also induced the upregulation of <em>erg24</em>, albeit to a lesser extent than the AMO-induced level (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>C and D). The relevance of the C7 double bond to the AMO response seems more pronounced. Since the responses of <em>erg</em> genes are similar in <em>erg24</em><sup>KO</sup> and <em>erg2</em><sup>KO</sup> strains (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>E), the deletion of <em>erg24</em> leads to defects in both C14 and C7, while <em>erg2</em> deletion only affects C7. The roles of other chemical bonds in sterols remain unclear due to the complexity of the sterol biosynthetic pathway. Why do different sterol intermediates activate distinct transcriptional responses? We hypothesize that sterol intermediates may be specifically detected by the identified transcription factors like NcSR and AtrR. In mammals, steroid hormones are detected by a class of transcription factors known as nuclear receptors, such as estrogen receptors <a href="#b0250" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0250">[50]</a>. In yeast and <em>Candida</em> species, the transcription factor Upc2p, which is essential for the regulation of sterol biosynthesis, interacts with ergosterol <a href="#b0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0090">[18]</a>, <a href="#b0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0095">[19]</a>. Interestingly, according to protein structure search by Foldseek <a href="#b0255" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0255">[51]</a>, the C-terminal of NcSR exhibits a high degree of structural similarity to the ergosterol-binding region of Upc2p, and AtrR also shares a certain degree of structural similarity with the ligand binding region of Upc2p and estrogen receptor, thus providing a critical support for the hypothesis. Nevertheless, this hypothesis, along with its underlying mechanisms, necessitates additional exploration and validation.</p>
<p id="p0235">Our findings reemphasize the rewiring in sterol biosynthesis regulation. The regulatory mechanism of sterol biosynthesis has been demonstrated to vary across the phylogenetic tree of fungi. Typically, i) Upc2p replaces SREBP to regulate <em>erg</em> genes in Saccharomycotina <a href="#b0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0050">[10]</a>; ii) in <em>Fusarium</em>, a novel transcription factor FgSR, but not SREBP, functions to activate the expression of <em>erg</em> genes <a href="#b0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0055">[11]</a>. Even within the SREBP pathway, differences in the proteinases responsible for SREBP cleavage and the SCAP protein that binds ergosterol are observed among different fungal species <a href="#b0260" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0260">[52]</a>. Despite these variations, ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation remains a fundamental mechanism. Our study reveals an extensive alteration with a shift from ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation in species like fission yeast, <em>C. neoformans</em>, budding yeast, and <em>Candida</em> to targeted regulation based on disruptions at specific steps in fungi such as <em>N. crassa</em> and <em>A. fumigatus</em>
<a href="#b0180" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0180">[36]</a>, <a href="#b0200" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0200">[40]</a>, <a href="#b0265" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0265">[53]</a>. The regulatory mechanisms in other fungi remain largely unexplored, but our results give a glimpse into the evolving paradigm of sterol biosynthesis regulation among fungi. As supposed by our results, this kind of regulation could potentiate the survival of fungi in complex environments. However, the situation within certain fungi is complex, characterized by the presence of multiple copies of <em>erg</em> genes and the differential tolerance to the accumulated specific sterol intermediates <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>, <a href="#b0225" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0225">[45]</a>, <a href="#b0270" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0270">[54]</a>. The underlying reasons and mechanisms by which the rewiring of sterol biosynthesis regulation confers benefits to fungi warrant further exploration.</p>
<p id="p0240">Remarkably, as our understanding of the regulatory framework of sterol biosynthesis deepens, transcription factors like SREBP, AtrR, and FgSR, which are initially identified in other fungi <a href="#b0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0055">[11]</a>, <a href="#b0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0070">[14]</a>, <a href="#b0275" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0275">[55]</a>, continue to be the prominent players in the emerging model, highlighting their significance. However, their subcellular localization, responses to sterol biosynthesis inhibitors, and the genes they regulate have changed across fungi, indicating functional rewiring of these regulators. For instance, AtrR and SREBP (SAH-2) directly bind to the promoters of <em>erg24</em> and <em>erg2</em> to control their expression in <em>N. crassa</em> (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>). However, in <em>A. fumigatus</em>, there are no recognized binding sites for AtrR and SREBP (SrbA) on the <em>erg24</em> and <em>erg2</em> promoters. Instead, they directly regulate the expression of other <em>erg</em> genes, such as <em>erg11</em> and <em>erg1</em><a href="#b0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0070">[14]</a>, <a href="#b0190" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0190">[38]</a>. These findings indicate that, although similar differential responses are observed in <em>A. fumigatus</em> and <em>N. crassa</em>, the transcription factors acting for a given perturbation may differ. It is plausible as the rewiring of transcription factors frequently occurs in species <a href="#b0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0050">[10]</a>, <a href="#b0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0065">[13]</a>, <a href="#b0280" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0280">[56]</a>. However, this greatly complicates our understanding of how transcription factors are associated with various perturbations, which is largely unknown. Anyway, our results may give an example of phenotypic innovations through rewiring in sterol biosynthesis regulation. Phenotypic innovations enhance the adaptability of fungi to changing environments <a href="#b0285" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0285">[57]</a>. Thus, while the underlying mechanisms are not yet fully elucidated, our findings suggest implications for environmental adaptation via the modulation of sterol biosynthesis regulation.</p></section><section id="s0110"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0245">Our study discloses a nuanced transcriptional regulation mechanism for sterol biosynthesis, extending beyond the already-known ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation. This mechanism arises from the functional rewiring of several conserved transcription factors and enables fungi to address perturbations in sterol biosynthesis with increased precision and potentially greater efficiency, thereby enhancing fungal resilience in complex environments.</p></section><section id="s0115"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CRediT authorship contribution statement</h2>
