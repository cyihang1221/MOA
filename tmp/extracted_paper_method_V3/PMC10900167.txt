</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abs0010"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="sec1_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Background</h3>
<p>Folic acid (FA) is the oxidized form of folate found in supplements and FA-fortified foods. Most FA is reduced by dihydrofolate reductase to 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (5mTHF); the latter is the form of folate naturally found in foods. Ingestion of FA increases the plasma levels of both 5mTHF and unmetabolized FA (UMFA). Limited information is available on the downstream metabolic effects of FA supplementation, including potential effects associated with UMFA.</p></section><section id="sec2_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objective</h3>
<p>We aimed to assess the metabolic effects of FA-supplementation, and the associations of plasma 5mTHF and UMFA with the metabolome in FA-naïve Bangladeshi adults.</p></section><section id="sec3_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>Sixty participants were selected from the Folic Acid and Creatine Trial; half received 800 μg FA/day for 12 weeks and half placebo. Plasma metabolome profiles were measured by high-resolution mass spectrometry, including 170 identified metabolites and 26,541 metabolic features. Penalized regression methods were used to assess the associations of targeted metabolites with FA-supplementation, plasma 5mTHF, and plasma UMFA. Pathway analyses were conducted using Mummichog.</p></section><section id="sec4_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>In penalized models of identified metabolites, FA-supplementation was associated with higher choline. Changes in 5mTHF concentrations were positively associated with metabolites involved in amino acid metabolism (5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid, acetylmethionine, creatinine, guanidinoacetate, hydroxyproline/n-acetylalanine) and 2 fatty acids (docosahexaenoic acid and linoleic acid). Changes in 5mTHF concentrations were negatively associated with acetylglutamate, acetyllysine, carnitine, propionyl carnitine, cinnamic acid, homogentisate, arachidonic acid, and nicotine. UMFA concentrations were associated with lower levels of arachidonic acid. Together, metabolites selected across all models were related to lipids, aromatic amino acid metabolism, and the urea cycle. Analyses of nontargeted metabolic features identified additional pathways associated with FA supplementation.</p></section><section id="sec5_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p>In addition to the recapitulation of several expected metabolic changes associated with 5mTHF, we observed additional metabolites/pathways associated with FA-supplementation and UMFA. Further studies are needed to confirm these associations and assess their potential implications for human health.</p></section><section id="sec6_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Trial registration number</h3>
<p>This trial was registered at <a href="https://clinicaltrials.gov" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://clinicaltrials.gov</a> as <a href="https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT01050556" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NCT01050556</a>.</p></section><section id="kwrds0010" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> folic acid, folate, unmetabolized folic acid, nutrition, metabolomics, arachidonic acid</p></section></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0010">Folate is an essential nutrient that is naturally present in foods in its methylated and reduced form, 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (5mTHF). The main dietary sources of 5mTHF are dark green leafy vegetables as well as seeds, fruits, and organ meats [<a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1">1</a>,<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">2</a>]. 5mTHF is involved in the synthesis and catabolism of molecules essential to human growth and development, including amino acids and nucleotides, largely through its central role in one-carbon metabolism (OCM) [<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">2</a>,<a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">3</a>]. Folic acid (FA), or pteroylglutamic acid, is the oxidized form of folate. It is more stable and resistant to heat-induced degradation and is the most common chemical form present in supplements and fortified foods such as grains and cereals [<a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4">4</a>,<a href="#bib5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib5">5</a>]. FA undergoes sequential enzymatic reduction by dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) to form dihydro- and tetrahydrofolate, ultimately generating 5mTHF, the most abundant biologically active form of folate [<a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">3</a>,<a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4">4</a>]. The reduction of FA occurs in the intestinal epithelial cells and in the liver [<a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6">6</a>,<a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7">7</a>]. Recent studies have shown that DHFR activity can be saturated with an intake of FA above 200 μg/meal, leading to circulating unmetabolized folic acid (UMFA) [<a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7">[7]</a>, <a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8">[8]</a>, <a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9">[9]</a>].</p>
<p id="p0015">OCM is a folate-dependent metabolic pathway responsible for the synthesis of the universal methyl donor, S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) [<a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">3</a>,<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">10</a>,<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>]. Although this pathway is tightly regulated, more than 200 methyltransferase enzymes utilize SAM as a methyl donor [<a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12">12</a>] and could potentially be influenced by FA supplementation through the enhanced production of SAM, ultimately leading to changes in their substrates, methylated products, or downstream metabolic pathways. These enzymes are involved in a multitude of functions, including the synthesis of creatine, epinephrine, and phosphatidylcholine; the methylation of DNA, RNA, and proteins; and the detoxification of harmful chemicals [<a href="#bib13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib13">13</a>,<a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">14</a>].</p>
<p id="p0020">In the United States, primary sources of higher FA intake include vitamin supplements, ready to eat cereals, and fortified beverages; these include products that are not part of the mandatory fortification strategy of enriched grain products. Collectively, this can result in high FA intake in the general population, and it is not uncommon for individuals to exceed the upper limit of 1000 μg/d [<a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8">8</a>,<a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">[15]</a>, <a href="#bib16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib16">[16]</a>, <a href="#bib17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib17">[17]</a>]. DHFR saturation can be achieved after the consumption of FA supplements or fortified foods and is further exacerbated by combined food FA fortification and FA-containing supplement use [<a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18">18</a>]. As a consequence, blood UMFA is detectable in &gt;95% of the United States population [<a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18">[18]</a>, <a href="#bib19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib19">[19]</a>, <a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20">[20]</a>, <a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21">[21]</a>], and previous cohort studies have determined that 75% of adults consuming fortified foods have concentrations &gt;1.32 nmol/L of UMFA in blood [<a href="#bib22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib22">22</a>]. Higher concentrations have been detected among adults over 60 years [<a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20">20</a>] and children under 11 years [<a href="#bib23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib23">23</a>]. Although adequate folate intake is critically essential for human health and the prevention of neural tube defects (NTDs), limited evidence in animal [<a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24">[24]</a>, <a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25">[25]</a>, <a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26">[26]</a>] and population studies have associated elevated FA intake with increased risk for cancer [<a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27">27</a>,<a href="#bib28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib28">28</a>], cardiometabolic diseases [<a href="#bib17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib17">17</a>,<a href="#bib29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib29">29</a>,<a href="#bib30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib30">30</a>], and neurocognitive endpoints [<a href="#bib31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib31">[31]</a>, <a href="#bib32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib32">[32]</a>, <a href="#bib33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib33">[33]</a>, <a href="#bib34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib34">[34]</a>]. This suggestive evidence raises questions about potential unanticipated metabolomic effects associated with moderate-to-high FA intake and UMFA [<a href="#bib29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib29">29</a>,<a href="#bib35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib35">35</a>,<a href="#bib36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib36">36</a>]. However, the current evidence is incomplete as dose–response data are lacking and the biological pathways and potential metabolomic effects of FA supplementation remain unknown [<a href="#bib32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib32">[32]</a>, <a href="#bib33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib33">[33]</a>, <a href="#bib34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib34">[34]</a>,<a href="#bib37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib37">[37]</a>, <a href="#bib38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib38">[38]</a>, <a href="#bib39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib39">[39]</a>].</p>
<p id="p0025">The Folic Acid and Creatine Trial (FACT) is a randomized controlled trial (RCT) of FA supplementation in Bangladeshi adults chronically exposed to arsenic-contaminated drinking water. We originally hypothesized that FA supplementation would increase the methylation of arsenic, a process that facilitates arsenic elimination in urine. The results confirmed these beneficial effects of FA supplementation on enhancing arsenic methylation and lowering of blood arsenic [<a href="#bib40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib40">40</a>,<a href="#bib41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib41">41</a>]. Given that arsenic is one of many substrates that are methylated through OCM, we here wished to leverage the biorepository from this RCT to determine what other metabolites and/or metabolic pathways might be influenced by FA supplementation.</p>
<p id="p0030">The metabolome is defined as the set of small-molecule metabolites that are intermediates and products of metabolism. Metabolomic profiling can be used as a direct measure of the functional output of genetic disposition, environment, diet, and health [<a href="#bib42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib42">[42]</a>, <a href="#bib43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib43">[43]</a>, <a href="#bib44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib44">[44]</a>]. By connecting exposures to internal dose and biological response [<a href="#bib45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib45">45</a>,<a href="#bib46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib46">46</a>], metabolomic profiles can ultimately provide a systems biology approach to identify key regulatory metabolites and pathways influenced by an intervention such as FA supplementation [<a href="#bib42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib42">42</a>,<a href="#bib45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib45">45</a>,<a href="#bib46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib46">46</a>]. To the best of our knowledge, no previous metabolomics studies have examined the effects of FA supplementation or of UMFA in humans. Conducting these studies in countries with FA-fortified foods is complicated because the presence of UMFA in plasma is nearly ubiquitous.</p>
<p id="p0035">In this study, we utilized plasma samples from a subset of participants enrolled in the FACT study. A unique feature of this RCT is that study participants were previously naïve to FA exposure as there is no FA-food fortification in Bangladesh and vitamin supplement use is uncommon. To provide new insight into the potential effects of FA on the metabolome, we leverage nontargeted, high-resolution mass spectrometry-based metabolomics to investigate <em>1</em>) the effects of FA supplementation on the metabolome and <em>2</em>) the associations of plasma 5mTHF and UMFA concentrations with the metabolome.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>
<section id="sec2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study population and study design</h3>
<p id="p0040">The study population is a random sample of 60 participants from the FACT study, an RCT that recruited 610 adults 20–75 years of age from Araihazar, Bangladesh, starting in 2010. This population is chronically exposed to arsenic-contaminated drinking water, and this clinical trial was initially designed to determine if FA supplementation could increase arsenic methylation and thereby lower blood arsenic concentrations [<a href="#bib41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib41">41</a>,<a href="#bib47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib47">47</a>].</p>
<p id="p0045">Exclusion criteria for the FACT study included pregnant females, health conditions such as renal disease or gastrointestinal disease, and previous use of nutritional supplements (uncommon in this region). Eligible participants were previously randomly assigned, conditioned on biological sex, to 1 of 5 treatment groups: placebo, 400 (roughly equivalent to the recommended dietary allowance in the United States) [<a href="#bib48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib48">48</a>], or 800 μg FA/day, 3 g/day creatine, or 3 g/day creatine + 400 μg/day FA. The median compliance with treatment assignment as assessed through pill counts was 99.5% [<a href="#bib47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib47">47</a>].</p>
<p id="p0050">For this study, we selected a total of 60 individuals: 30 individuals who received 800 μg/day FA treatment for 12 weeks and 30 sex-matched controls who received placebo for 12 weeks. We excluded participants who smoked tobacco or chewed betel nut.</p>
<p id="p0055">All participants provided informed consent and ethical approval was obtained by the Institutional Review Board of Columbia University Medical Center and the Bangladesh Medical Research Council. The study protocol has been previously described in detail [<a href="#bib49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib49">49</a>]. A flowchart of the study participants is presented in <a href="#appsec1" class="usa-link">Supplemental Figure 1</a>.</p></section><section id="sec2.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Plasma metabolomic profiling</h3>
<section id="sec2.2.1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Collection of biological samples and detection of 5mTHF and UMFA.</h4>
<p id="p0060">Venous blood samples were collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid vacutainers at baseline and at 12 weeks. Further details of blood collection have been previously described[<a href="#bib47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib47">47</a>]. Plasma 5mTHF and UMFA were determined by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), and red blood cell folate was measured by a radioprotein binding assay as previously described [<a href="#bib50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib50">[50]</a>, <a href="#bib51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib51">[51]</a>, <a href="#bib52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib52">[52]</a>].</p></section><section id="sec2.2.2"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Nontargeted metabolomic profiling.</h4>
<p id="p0065">Plasma samples from baseline and week 12 were analyzed using established methods for nontargeted metabolomics [<a href="#bib53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib53">53</a>]. Briefly, plasma samples previously stored at −80°C without a freeze–thaw cycle, were treated with 2 volumes of acetonitrile containing 14 stable isotope standards, and then transferred to a low-volume vial at 4°C before analysis [<a href="#bib54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib54">54</a>]. Samples were analyzed in triplicate by liquid chromatography-Fourier transform mass spectrometry (Thermo Scientific Ultimate 3000 liquid chromatography system interfaced to a Fusion Tribrid Orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometer) [<a href="#bib54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib54">54</a>,<a href="#bib55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib55">55</a>] using dual-column chromatography with C<sub>18</sub> run in negative electrospray ionization (ESI) mode (for the retention of nonpolar compounds) and hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC), run in positive ESI (for the retention of polar compounds). All samples were run 6 times, with 3 analytical replicates for each separation method (C<sub>18</sub> and HILIC). The median coefficient of variation (CV) for technical replicates was calculated for each detectable feature, and those with CV &gt;30% were excluded. After data processing, our dataset included information on mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), retention time (seconds), intensity, CV, and related descriptive characteristics, including minimal information standards for metabolomics data using apLCMS [<a href="#bib56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib56">[56]</a>, <a href="#bib57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib57">[57]</a>, <a href="#bib58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib58">[58]</a>] and xMSanalyzer [<a href="#bib59" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib59">59</a>]. In total, nontargeted metabolomics analysis included 12,413 HILIC+ and 14,128 C<sub>18</sub>- features, of which 99 (HILIC+) and 73 (C<sub>18</sub>-) had confirmed chemical metabolite identities via internal libraries generated from commercial standards (that is, “identified” metabolites).</p></section><section id="sec2.2.3"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolite data processing.</h4>
<p id="p0070">Raw spectral data were processed following established filtering and normalization criteria [<a href="#bib60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib60">60</a>]. Daily batches were normalized using ComBat [<a href="#bib61" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib61">61</a>]. Two outliers were identified during quantile normalization and again via visualization of exploratory principal component analysis and were subsequently removed. Features that were not detected in at least 50% of the overall samples (2000 features, 7%) were removed and for the remaining features, values below detection were imputed with the lowest observed value divided by square root of 2. For identified metabolites, 2 C<sub>18</sub> metabolites were dropped because of the low detection rate, resulting in 98 HILIC+ and 72 C<sub>18</sub>- metabolites available for analysis. For the statistical analysis, plasma metabolite levels were quantile normalized and log<sub>2</sub>-transformed.</p></section></section><section id="sec2.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h3>
<section id="sec2.3.1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Association with targeted plasma metabolome.</h4>
<p id="p0075">As our primary analysis, we used linear and logistic regression with Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) penalty [<a href="#bib62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib62">62</a>] to identify the targeted metabolites (<em>n =</em> 170) that are most predictive of FA treatment, 5mTHF, or UMFA. We modeled FA treatment as a binary outcome as this formulation allows the identification of metabolites associated with treatment assignment. The difference of plasma 5mTHF between baseline and week 12 was modeled as a continuous outcome and linear regression models were used. The models for UMFA used only week 12 concentrations as a continuous variable because the study population were FA-naïve and UMFA levels at baseline were expected to be negligible and only detectable at week 12 [<a href="#bib63" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib63">63</a>,<a href="#bib64" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib64">64</a>]. We use this modeling framework to identify the collections of variables that are most strongly associated with FA supplementation and with the levels of plasma 5mTHF and plasma UMFA over the study period. The tuning parameters controlling the degree of penalization (that is, λ) across all 3 endpoint models were selected using leave-one-out cross-validation. For predictors, the difference of <em>n =</em> 170 identified plasma metabolite levels between baseline and week 12 for each metabolite was included. All models were adjusted for sex, age, and BMI by including them as unpenalized covariates in the models, because these characteristics may have an impact on plasma 5mTHF levels, UMFA levels, and the metabolome. The models for 5mTHF were further adjusted by the treatment group. Sensitivity analyses were run using elastic-net models, again using cross-validation to choose tuning parameters [<a href="#bib65" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib65">65</a>].</p>
<p id="p0080">As a secondary approach, to quantify the magnitude of the association for the metabolites identified by LASSO, we fitted unpenalized regression models. For the treatment group and plasma 5mTHF, we used generalized estimating equation (GEE) models (which account for the repeated measurements for each individual) and linear regression models for UMFA using only week 12 data. In these models, each person-visit was treated as an observation and each metabolite identified by LASSO was treated as the outcome variable. For interpretation, we calculated the change in the concentration of metabolites per a 10 nm/L increase in the concentration of plasma 5mTHF (∼1 SD) and per 1 nM/L increase in the concentration of UMFA. The model output was then exponentiated to obtain geometric mean ratios (GMR) of metabolite levels. All models were adjusted for sex, age, and BMI. These models were only intended to refine the understanding of the potential directions of association and only run for a limited number of metabolites.</p>
<p id="p0085">We extracted the KEGG IDs for the metabolites identified by the LASSO analyses and visualized them on the KEGG human metabolic map using iPATH [<a href="#bib66" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib66">66</a>].</p></section><section id="sec2.3.2"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Association with nontargeted plasma metabolome and pathway analysis.</h4>
<p id="p0090">In separate models, we fitted unpenalized linear models where FA supplementation, 5mTHF, UMFA levels were predictor variables, and nontargeted metabolites levels (as continuous, log<sub>2</sub>-transformed) were outcome variables. For FA supplementation and 5mTHF, we included both week 0 and week 12 data using GEE models. All models were adjusted for sex, age, and BMI. The outputs from these models were evaluated using Mummichog [<a href="#bib67" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib67">67</a>], a validated annotation-free pipeline specifically designed to analyze nontargeted high-resolution metabolomics data. For each model, we ran the Mummichog algorithm on the MetaboAnalyst platform [<a href="#bib68" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib68">68</a>] specifying mixed mode (both positive and negative ion modes), 5 parts per million mass tolerance, and included retention time, <em>P</em> values, and <em>z</em>-scores from the models.</p>
<p id="p0095">For the statistical analysis, we used R software (version 4.3.0), and for the LASSO analysis, we used “glmnet” and “caret” R packages [<a href="#bib65" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib65">65</a>,<a href="#bib69" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib69">69</a>].</p></section></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="sec3.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Descriptive features and 5mTHF and UMFA distribution</h3>
<p id="p0100">The mean age of the study sample was 34.4 years, and 50% were males. The baseline sociodemographic characteristics and nutritional status variables were similar between the placebo and the treatment groups (<a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). At baseline, there were no differences between placebo and treatment groups with respect to plasma 5mTHF (7.66 nmol/L ± 3.61 compared with 7.58 nnmol/L ± 2.88) or UMFA (2.19 nmol/L ± 0.81 compared with 2.17 nmol/L ± 0.71). After 12 weeks, the supplementation group had notably higher levels of 5mTHF (35.3 nmol/L ± 15.0 compared with 8.39 nmol/L ± 4.22) and UMFA (4.85 nmol/L ± 10.61 compared with 1.93 nmol/L ± 0.62). The correlation between 5mTHF and UMFA at week 12 was weak in the FA treatment group (Spearman <em>r</em> = 0.26, <em>P</em> value = 0.17).</p>

<p id="p0105">In LASSO regression models including 170 plasma metabolites with confirmed identity, 16 unique metabolites were selected. Plasma 5mTHF levels were associated with the largest number of metabolites (<em>n =</em> 15), followed by FA supplementation (<em>n =</em> 1) and plasma UMFA (<em>n =</em> 1). The identified associations include one overlap of arachidonic acid between 5mTHF and UMFA (<a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). In the LASSO model for FA supplementation compared with placebo, choline was selected as positively associated with FA supplementation. In GEE models, FA supplementation was associated with 32% higher plasma choline levels (95% CI: 11%, 56%) as compared with those with placebo. In LASSO regression models for 5mTHF concentrations, 15 metabolites were selected (<a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). Seven metabolites were positively associated with higher plasma 5mTHF concentrations, including 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (HIAA), acetylmethionine, creatinine, guanidinoacetate (GAA), hydroxyproline/N-acetylalanine, docosahexanoic acid, and linoleic acid. Eight metabolites were negatively associated with plasma 5mTHF concentrations; these included acetylglutamate, acetyllysine, carnitine, propionyl carnitine, arachidonic acid, cinnamic acid, homogestisate, and nicotine. Lastly, plasma concentrations of UMFA were negatively associated with arachidonic acid (<a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). In GEE models, each nmol/L increase in UMFA was associated with 20% lower plasma arachidonic acid levels (95% CI: 3%, 33%).</p>

<p id="p0125">Folate is an essential nutrient with roles in numerous biological pathways largely because of its central role in OCM. Folate deficiency is a major risk factor for NTDs. In 1998, the United States implemented mandatory fortification of grains with FA, leading to the successful reduction of risk for NTDs. Since then, more than 80 countries have followed suit. In addition, FA supplement use is widespread. Consequently, increasing levels of FA intake through combined food fortification and supplement use has led to a growing acknowledgment that there are “knowledge gaps” related to potential unanticipated effects of moderate-to-high FA intake and/or increased UMFA; these are detailed in the proceedings of an National Institutes of Health workshop [<a href="#bib29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib29">29</a>]. To begin to address this issue, we leveraged banked samples from an RCT of FA supplementation in a population naïve to FA fortification and applied HRM techniques to identify the metabolomic impact of FA supplementation and of plasma concentrations of 5mTHF and UMFA. This work builds on our previous findings on metabolites more directly related to the FACT study’s primary and secondary outcomes, including choline, betaine, dimethylglycine (described by Hall et al. [<a href="#bib70" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib70">70</a>]) and GAA (described by Peters et al. [<a href="#bib71" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib71">71</a>]). This new expanded approach suggests that FA supplementation induces numerous metabolomic changes using both identified metabolites and nontargeted metabolome profiling.</p>
<p id="p0130">Consistent with prior knowledge, our analyses identified FA treatment and changes in 5mTHF levels to be associated with changes in several metabolites directly or indirectly involved in OCM. For example, FA treatment was significantly associated with higher levels of choline, consistent with observations by Hall et al. [<a href="#bib70" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib70">70</a>], in which FA was also found to increase betaine (i.e., trimethylglycine) as choline can be oxidized to form betaine. In the liver and kidney, betaine and 5mTHF can serve as alternative methyl donors for the remethylation of homocysteine to contribute to the synthesis of SAM [<a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">3</a>,<a href="#bib72" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib72">72</a>,<a href="#bib73" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib73">73</a>]. The most likely reason for increased choline with FA treatment is that the methylation via 5mTHF can spare methyl groups from betaine, thereby increasing the precursor, choline. Previous studies have identified effects of folate supplementation on increased levels of plasma betaine, demonstrating the interaction of folate and choline on OCM [<a href="#bib74" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib74">74</a>]. Downstream effects of increased choline may be multifactorial and beneficial, as choline has many important roles including lipoprotein metabolism, the maintenance of cell membranes [<a href="#bib75" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib75">75</a>], and the synthesis of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine, and other signaling molecules [<a href="#bib76" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib76">76</a>].</p>
<p id="p0135">Changes in plasma 5mTHF were positively associated with GAA and creatinine (a degradation product of creatine). GAA and creatine were previously evaluated in the FACT study by Peters et al. [<a href="#bib71" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib71">71</a>], in which FA supplementation (assessed in the 400 μg/d group only) also increased GAA but not creatine + creatinine. Given that the methylation of GAA to form creatine is a major consumer of SAM [<a href="#bib77" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib77">77</a>], it is reasonable to predict that higher 5mTHF might influence creatine synthesis. Creatine plays critical roles in many aspects of energy metabolism, as creatine phosphate is important in regenerating ATP from ADP [<a href="#bib78" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib78">78</a>]. These observations suggest that, in this population of Bangladeshi adults, folate nutritional status may influence creatine metabolism, which could also have pleiotropic downstream effects.</p>
<p id="p0140">Another OCM-related targeted metabolite found to be increased in association with 5mTHF was acetylmethionine, a derivative of the essential amino acid methionine. Methionine is the product of the remethylation of homocysteine by 5mTHF and/or betaine, and it is the immediate precursor to SAM [<a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">3</a>]. In addition, it is an important proteinogenic amino acid that could also be associated with changes in other amino acids, including amino acids captured by LASSO-penalized regression to be associated with 5mTHF levels such as acetylglutamate, acetylalanine, and acetyllysine.</p>
<p id="p0145">Some metabolites altered in association with 5mTHF were less predictable, but in retrospect are metabolically linked to SAM and thus, are proximal to OCM. For example, 5mTHF was found to be associated with increased 5-HIAA, an intermediate in serotonin metabolism, a pathway that requires SAM as a cofactor for N-acetylserotonin O-methyltransferase [<a href="#bib79" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib79">79</a>]. This connection is also supported by previous studies that found lower levels of 5-HIAA in folate and B<sub>12</sub>-deficient adults [<a href="#bib80" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib80">[80]</a>, <a href="#bib81" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib81">[81]</a>, <a href="#bib82" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib82">[82]</a>]. Studies have also linked folate deficiency to depression because of the alterations in serotonin levels [<a href="#bib83" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib83">83</a>].</p>
<p id="p0150">UMFA is a metabolite that can be detected in the bloodstream after the consumption of FA-fortified foods and FA supplements [<a href="#bib19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib19">19</a>,<a href="#bib84" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib84">84</a>]. Although evidence is limited, prior observational studies have identified metabolic changes associated with FA supplementation that differ from those associated with the consumption of natural folates in food. For example, a study of females who received 500 μg of FA per day for 12 weeks found higher plasma levels of glycine, betaine, choline, histidine, formate, and threonine as compared with females consuming a diet high in 5mTHF [<a href="#bib85" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib85">85</a>]. In laboratory and animal models, UMFA can bind folate-dependent enzymes such as DHFR and methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase, as well as folate transporters, and may interfere with their function [<a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24">24</a>,<a href="#bib86" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib86">86</a>].</p>
<p id="p0155">In addition to the OCM-related changes associated with FA treatment and 5mTHF, we also found novel metabolic changes associated with UMFA. Using confirmed metabolites, we identified that plasma levels of both 5mTHF and UMFA were negatively associated with arachidonic acid levels. Arachidonic acid is an omega-6 polyunsaturated long-chain fatty acid [<a href="#bib87" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib87">87</a>]. Together with choline, arachidonic acid is an essential component of the phospholipid layer of cell membranes, is the main metabolic precursor of eicosanoids that are involved in cellular proliferation and apoptosis, and is involved in the modulation of the inflammatory response, with a key role in the activation of natural killer cells [<a href="#bib88" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib88">[88]</a>, <a href="#bib89" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib89">[89]</a>, <a href="#bib90" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib90">[90]</a>]. Previous studies have identified an association between higher UMFA levels and reduced natural killer cell numbers as well as their cytotoxic activity [<a href="#bib84" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib84">84</a>,<a href="#bib91" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib91">91</a>,<a href="#bib92" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib92">92</a>]. Although the mechanisms underlying this somewhat controversial association remain unclear, our findings suggest that arachidonic acid could provide a metabolic link between UMFA and immune function. Interestingly, 2 metabolomics studies in rodents found lower levels of serum arachidonic acid in the offspring of dams supplemented with FA [<a href="#bib93" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib93">93</a>,<a href="#bib94" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib94">94</a>]. However, the mechanisms by which UMFA may influence arachidonic acid are unclear and will require further study.</p>
<p id="p0160">Our nontargeted analyses identified 2 pathways specifically associated with UMFA: tyrosine metabolism and ubiquinone biosynthesis. Tyrosine is a nonessential proteinogenic amino acid, and a precursor of several metabolites including catecholamines (for example, adrenaline, noradrenaline, and dopamine) involved in neuronal transmission. Tyrosine is also important in the synthesis of thyroxine and melanin; the latter is a determinant of skin pigmentation [<a href="#bib95" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib95">95</a>]. Ubiquinone, or coenzyme Q10, is an essential molecule required for the synthesis of ATP, and thus, plays a key role cellular energy metabolism [<a href="#bib96" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib96">96</a>]. Metabolic pathways of both tyrosine and ubiquinone utilize SAM as a cofactor [<a href="#bib95" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib95">95</a>]. However, whether the association between UMFA and these pathways occurs through OCM remains unclear.</p>
<section id="sec4.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Limitations and strengths</h3>
<p id="p0165">Although we were able to detect meaningful metabolic differences across treatment groups, the sample size of 60 individuals could have reduced our statistical power. We used penalized regression strategies to increase the model’s flexibility given that the number of metabolites was larger than the sample size. Despite the limited sample size, our results were internally consistent across targeted and nontargeted approaches, as well as in sensitivity analyses using different statistical approaches, which support the internal validity of the results. In addition to the use of penalized regression, we ran unpenalized regression models for a limited number of metabolites as a second-stage analysis. In these analyses, we did not explicitly account for postselection inference nor the uncertainty that can emerge from including the metabolites selected by LASSO, which can limit the interpretability of the estimates. However, these analyses were only intended to understand the direction of the associations and were not interpreted in the context of statistical significance.</p>
<p id="p0170">We were not able to assess any changes in the metabolome that may have specifically resulted from the correction of a state of baseline folate deficiency as the number of folate-deficient individuals included in this study was small (<em>N</em> = 9). Future studies should assess the potential metabolomic effects of FA supplementation in folate-deficient compared with sufficient populations. In addition, because all individuals included in the supplementation group received the same dosage of FA, a dose–response assessment was not possible. Our data showed some detectable UMFA values at baseline, suggesting that there was background noise from the assay and that the absolute levels of UMFA from the study should not be interpreted in either the treatment or placebo group. However, there was a clear increase in UMFA levels with FA treatment compared with placebo, showing that the relative exposure levels (that is, rank-order) was maintained. Inter-laboratory assay comparisons have shown variability in the concentrations of UMFA by LC-MS/MS, indicating that the absolute concentrations of UMFA may not be entirely comparable across laboratories [<a href="#bib97" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib97">97</a>]. This issue should be addressed in future studies to improve comparability across populations. Lastly, the study results may not be generalizable to other populations, given that metabolic differences across populations can occur because of several factors such as exposure dose, genetic differences, dietary preferences, and use of vitamin supplements.</p></section></section><section id="sec5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0175">Very few studies have investigated the effects of FA supplementation on the metabolome despite previous evidence suggesting unanticipated metabolic effects of high FA intake and UMFA. Addressing this research gap, our study was able to identify novel metabolites and pathways associated with FA supplementation and plasma concentrations of 5mTHF and UMFA. FA supplementation and 5mTHF levels were consistently associated with metabolites and pathways directly or indirectly connected to OCM. Novel associations between UMFA and lower levels of arachidonic acid, a long-chain fatty acid involved in cell membrane structure and the modulation of immune response, as well as tyrosine and ubiquinone metabolism, were identified. Our findings can help elucidate the metabolic effects of FA supplementation on the metabolome, suggesting the existence of both anticipated OCM-related and novel metabolic effects. Future studies are needed to confirm these findings, to better understand and characterize these associations across populations, and to evaluate their potential health implications.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Author contributions</h2>
