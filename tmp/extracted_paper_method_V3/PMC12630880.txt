</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="Par1">The speed of sound is of particular importance in determining the physical and chemical properties of gas mixtures. Given the importance of this parameter in hydrogen transport and the high speed and accuracy of machine learning methods compared to traditional methods such as equations of state, this study estimates the speed of sound in hydrogen/cushion gas mixtures. For this purpose, 665 data sets of sound speed in hydrogen/cushion gas mixtures were collected from previous studies, and then 4 machine learning methods were used, including Linear Regression (LR), Extra Trees Regressor (ETR), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN). The hyperparameters of these models were optimized using the Bayesian algorithm, and fivefold cross-validation was used to prevent overfitting. The ETR method had the best performance with R<sup>2</sup> = 0.9996 and RMSE = 6.2775 m/s, followed by the KNN method with R<sup>2</sup> = 0.9996 and RMSE = 7.0540 m/s. Also, the weakest performance belonged to the Linear method with R<sup>2</sup> = 0.8104. Also, the ETR method was able to estimate 64.81% of the data with an error of less than 0.0001%. Also, sensitivity analysis was performed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method, and based on its results, the hydrogen mole fraction had the greatest effect on the speed of sound, and at low values, this effect was inverse, and at high values, this effect was direct. After the hydrogen mole fraction, pressure was the second most effective parameter, which showed a behavior similar to the hydrogen mole fraction (inverse relationship at low values and direct at high values). Also, the methane mole fraction had the least effect on the speed of sound in the gas mixture. The developed machine learning models not only demonstrate outstanding predictive performance for sound speed in hydrogen-based mixtures but also offer valuable insight into the acoustic behavior of multicomponent gas systems. These findings can support the design and control of hydrogen energy infrastructures, where real-time monitoring and compositional analysis are critical.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Speed of sound, Machine learning (ML), Gas mixtures, Hydrogen transport, Acoustic properties</p></section><section id="kwd-group2" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Subject terms:</strong> Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par25">The investigation and determination of the speed of sound in gases and gas mixtures have long been recognized as essential tools for analyzing their physical and chemical properties<sup><a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>–<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a></sup>. In gas mixtures, the sound speed provides valuable information about the composition percentages of constituent gases, which is crucial for both industrial applications and research purposes<sup><a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>–<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a></sup>. To address this need, various commercial sensors have been developed to determine the speed of sound by analyzing the resonance frequency of an acoustic resonator containing the gas sample<sup><a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a></sup>. These sensors employ acoustic excitation and analyze the response using techniques such as the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) or frequency sweeping methods<sup><a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>–<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a></sup>. Although signal processing plays a crucial role in these approaches, rapid changes in gas composition can introduce delays that limit their practical applicability.</p>
<p id="Par26">The growing adoption of hydrogen (H<sub>2</sub>) as a clean and safe energy carrier has further highlighted the need for accurate, rapid, and reliable methods of estimating the thermodynamic properties of H<sub>2</sub>-based gas mixtures<sup><a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>–<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a></sup>. Among these properties, the speed of sound plays a pivotal role in controlling transport processes, ensuring operational safety, and designing H<sub>2</sub>-based energy systems<sup><a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>,<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a></sup>. However, traditional methods of measuring sound speed often rely on complex experimental setups or thermodynamic models, which can be time-consuming, costly, and sensitive to measurement errors.</p>
<p id="Par27">With recent advancements in data-driven technologies, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful alternative for modeling complex physical phenomena. ML algorithms can predict the sound speed with high accuracy by learning patterns from existing datasets, thereby reducing reliance on exhaustive laboratory experiments and intricate theoretical calculations. Particularly in H<sub>2</sub>-containing gas mixtures, ML provides a promising approach to address the nonlinear and multivariable nature of gas properties, including variations in temperature, pressure, and composition.</p>
<p id="Par28">Moreover, the integration of H<sub>2</sub> into natural gas networks has been proposed as a practical solution for transporting and storing energy produced from renewable sources such as wind, solar, and hydroelectric power. H<sub>2</sub> can be produced through processes like gasification and steam reforming, often combined with carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) methods<sup><a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a></sup>. In this context, analyzing and modeling the thermodynamic properties of H<sub>2</sub>-containing gas mixtures, such as those containing nitrogen (N<sub>2</sub>) and carbon dioxide (CO<sub>2</sub>), is of significant importance<sup><a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a></sup>. The speed of sound in these mixtures greatly influences gas flow behavior, combustion processes, and energy system design and optimization.</p>
<p id="Par29">The application of ML techniques to predict the speed of sound offers substantial improvements in modeling accuracy compared to traditional methods. By utilizing experimental data and sophisticated ML algorithms, it becomes possible to simulate the complex and nonlinear characteristics of H<sub>2</sub>-based gas mixtures more accurately. This enhanced predictive capability can contribute to the optimization of industrial processes, the better design of gas storage and transportation systems, the determination of flow characteristics, and the improvement of combustion efficiency<sup><a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par30">In terms of theoretical frameworks, one of the commonly used equations of state (EoS) is EoS-CG 2019, developed to estimate the thermodynamic properties of gas mixtures in CCUS processes<sup><a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>,<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a></sup>. This equation incorporates gases such as CO<sub>2</sub>, N<sub>2</sub>, oxygen, and H<sub>2</sub>, enabling the simulation of gas properties under varying pressure and temperature conditions. Nonetheless, accurate prediction using such equations requires extensive and precise experimental data, which are particularly scarce for H<sub>2</sub>-N<sub>2</sub> binary systems<sup><a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>–<a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a></sup>.</p>
<p id="Par31">By leveraging experimental data and advanced ML modeling techniques, ML can significantly enhance the accuracy of equations of state and the analysis of gas properties. Specifically, in H<sub>2</sub>-N<sub>2</sub> binary systems where experimental data are limited, ML approaches can facilitate more accurate simulations and provide optimal predictions of the speed of sound. These advancements are not only beneficial for industrial process optimization but also crucial for the design of H<sub>2</sub> storage systems and the management of renewable energy resources.</p>

<p id="Par33">This research provided a novel ML framework for predicting the sound speed of mixtures of H<sub>2</sub>. <p id="Par41">In this study, a set of ML algorithms was employed to predict the speed of sound in gas mixtures. Linear regression (LR) was included as a baseline to provide a simple reference model. To capture more complex relationships, we considered ensemble tree-based methods (ETR and XGBoost), a kernel-based method (SVR), and an instance-based method (KNN). This combination ensures both a fair baseline comparison and a comprehensive evaluation across different ML paradigms, allowing us to assess the robustness and accuracy of the proposed approach.</p>
<section id="Sec45"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Linear regression (LR)</h4>
<p id="Par43">Linear regression is a widely used statistical and ML technique for modeling the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables. When only one predictor is considered, it is called simple LR, while the use of multiple predictors leads to multiple LR. In the basic form of the model, the dependent variable y is expressed as a function of the independent variable x, a slope coefficient (β₁), and an intercept (β₀). The main goal is to estimate the coefficients in a way that minimizes the error term (ε). The accuracy of the model is often evaluated using the sum of squared errors (SSE), which measures the difference between predicted and observed values. To achieve the lowest SSE, the coefficients are optimized through the ordinary least squares (OLS) method.</p>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="Equ2"><tr>
<td class="formula"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/3764/12630880/0380a99c98c3/d33e993.gif" loading="lazy" id="d33e993" alt="graphic file with name d33e993.gif"></td>
<td class="label">2</td>
</tr></table>
<p id="Par44">Multiple LR can be expressed as Eq. (<a href="#Equ3" class="usa-link">3</a>), where <strong>y</strong> represents the dependent variable, while the predictors are denoted as <strong>x</strong><sub><strong>1</strong></sub>, <strong>x</strong><sub><strong>2</strong></sub>, …, <strong>x</strong><sub><strong>n</strong></sub>. The parameters <strong>β</strong><sub><strong>1</strong></sub>, <strong>β</strong><sub><strong>2</strong></sub>, …, <strong>β</strong><sub><strong>n</strong></sub> correspond to the coefficients associated with each predictor, and <strong>n</strong> indicates the total number of variables included in the model. The objective of this approach is to estimate the coefficients in such a way that the sum of squared errors (SSE), which reflects the gap between predicted and observed values, is minimized<sup><a href="#CR44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR44">44</a></sup>.</p>

<p id="Par45">Support Vector Regression (SVR), as illustrated in Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>, adapts the core concept of Support Vector Machines (SVM) for use in predicting continuous values. Rather than reducing the overall error across every observation like traditional regression methods, SVR aims to identify a function that stays within a specified error margin—known as epsilon (ϵ). This methodology emphasizes larger prediction errors while disregarding smaller discrepancies, effectively reducing the influence of data noise and enhancing model robustness<sup><a href="#CR45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR45">45</a>–<a href="#CR48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR48">48</a></sup>.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="Fig2"><h5 class="obj_head">Fig. 2.</h5>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/3764/12630880/05c238cd375a/41598_2025_24505_Fig2_HTML.jpg" loading="lazy" id="MO2" height="896" width="641" alt="Fig. 2"></p>

<figcaption><p>SVR flow chart.</p></figcaption></figure><p id="Par46">SVR applies kernels such as linear, polynomial, and radial basis functions (RBF) to project input variables into expanded dimensional spaces, facilitating the capture of complex nonlinear patterns in the data. One of the most influential parameters in SVR is the regularization constant (<em>C</em>), which controls the trade-off between achieving a low error on the training data and maintaining the ability to generalize to unseen data. A large <em>C</em> value tends to force the model to fit the training data closely, which can lead to overfitting, while a smaller <em>C</em> encourages simpler, more general models. Although SVR can be computationally demanding, especially when working with non-linear kernels, it performs exceptionally well on datasets of limited to moderate size. To ensure optimal results, important preprocessing steps include normalizing input features, selecting an appropriate kernel function, and conducting extensive hyperparameter optimization. By relying on a subset of data points—known as support vectors—SVR offers both accuracy and interpretability, making it a reliable tool for precise regression in areas like finance, engineering, and computational biology<sup><a href="#CR49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR49">49</a>–<a href="#CR51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR51">51</a></sup>.</p></section><section id="Sec49"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">K-nearest neighbors (KNN)</h4>
<p id="Par48">Figure <a href="#Fig3" class="usa-link">3</a> illustrates the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm, a straightforward yet versatile method used for both classification and regression. KNN operates by examining the <em>k</em> closest data points within the training set—identified through distance measures such as Euclidean, Manhattan, or Minkowski—to infer the outcome for a new input. In regression scenarios, it estimates the response by averaging, or weighting and averaging, the target values of these neighboring points<sup><a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">39</a>,<a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">40</a></sup>. Unlike traditional algorithms, KNN does not involve a separate training phase; instead, all computations are deferred until prediction time, classifying it as a lazy learner. The model’s accuracy is highly influenced by the chosen value of <em>k</em>: a small <em>k</em> may result in overfitting due to sensitivity to noise, while a large <em>k</em> can cause underfitting by ignoring localized trends. Proper normalization of input features and careful selection of a distance metric are essential, as disparities in feature scales can distort distance evaluations. While the algorithm can become computationally intensive with large datasets, its ease of implementation and flexibility have made it a popular tool. Efficiency enhancements, such as KD-Trees and Ball Trees, can help mitigate its computational load. Due to its adaptability, KNN is widely applied in fields like recommender systems, image analysis, and healthcare diagnostics<sup><a href="#CR52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR52">52</a>–<a href="#CR54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR54">54</a></sup>.</p>

<p id="Par50">As shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a powerful extension of the Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT) framework, designed to deliver high accuracy and computational efficiency. The model sequentially generates decision trees, with each successive tree aimed at reducing the prediction errors left by the previous ones. XGBoost’s objective function not only focuses on minimizing a chosen loss metric—such as mean squared error—but also integrates L1 and L2 regularization components. These regularization terms help control model complexity and enhance its ability to generalize to new data, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting<sup><a href="#CR55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR55">55</a>–<a href="#CR57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR57">57</a></sup>. XGBoost introduces several advanced features that set it apart from traditional boosting methods. It is particularly adept at managing datasets with missing or sparse values and employs a technique called shrinkage, which moderates the influence of each newly added tree to promote more controlled learning. During the creation of each tree, the algorithm randomly selects a subset of features, which helps reduce model variance and improves generalization. By enabling parallel processing during tree construction, XGBoost significantly accelerates computation, making it well-suited for handling large and complex datasets. Its flexibility, scalability, and resilience have led to widespread adoption across numerous fields, including anomaly detection, personalized recommendations, and forecasting time-dependent data. To achieve optimal outcomes, fine-tuning key hyperparameters—such as the learning rate, maximum tree depth, and sampling proportions—is critical<sup><a href="#CR58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR58">58</a>–<a href="#CR60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR60">60</a></sup>.</p>

<p id="Par52">Figure <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a> illustrates the ETR Regressor, an ensemble learning method that builds numerous decision trees independently by incorporating randomness both in selecting features and determining split points at each node. Unlike Random Forests, which use bootstrapped samples and carefully chosen splits, ETR utilizes the full training set and performs splits in a completely random manner. This approach helps lower variance and prevent overfitting, all while keeping bias relatively low<sup><a href="#CR61" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR61">61</a>,<a href="#CR62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR62">62</a></sup>.</p>

This configurability contributes to the algorithm’s capacity to deliver accurate and reliable predictions without compromising on computational speed<sup><a href="#CR63" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR63">63</a></sup><sup>,</sup><sup><a href="#CR64" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR64">64</a></sup>. <p id="Par72">In order to investigate the effect of different parameters on the output and sensitivity analysis, the SHAP method was used, the results of which are shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig9" class="usa-link">9</a>. <p id="Par79">With the increasing development of H<sub>2</sub>-based technologies, including production, storage, and transportation, understanding and predicting the physical properties of H<sub>2</sub>-containing gas mixtures has become a key issue in energy engineering. One of these properties is the speed of sound, which plays an important role in gas composition monitoring, leak detection, flow dynamics modeling, and acoustic sensor design. Traditional methods for predicting this parameter usually rely on laboratory data or complex equations of state that are limited in variable or real-time operating conditions. In this study, the developed ML models provide a simple, fast, and accurate method for predicting the speed of sound in multi-component gas mixtures, which can be used in real-time monitoring, smart control, and digital twin systems. Sensitivity analysis of models also provides a better understanding of the effect of gas composition on acoustic behavior and can help increase transparency, safety, and efficiency in the design of H<sub>2</sub> infrastructures.</p></section><section id="Sec15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Limitations and future works</h3>
<p id="Par80">In this study, various ML methods were used to model the speed of sound in H<sub>2</sub> mixtures. Despite the good performance of these models and the achievement of very good accuracy, various limitations can also be mentioned for this study. The first limitation is the dataset, although a suitable dataset was used in this study and its appropriate size was also proven by using learning curves. The generalizability and efficiency of the model can still be increased by adding data wider than temperature and pressure, and adding other compounds to the dataset. Second, despite the good accuracy of the learning methods used, including ETR, XGBoost, SVR and KNN, and their ability to understand complex nonlinear relationships, it should be noted that these methods are data-driven and cannot be directly derived from a physical perspective. Future work can use hybrid methods that combine thermodynamic methods with ML, such as Physics-Informed Neural Networks, by expanding the database and applying them to real H<sub>2</sub> systems. In addition, performing uncertainty analyses can also add to the reliability and applicability of these models, along with their speed and flexibility.</p></section></section><section id="Sec16"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p id="Par81">The speed of sound is a fundamental property influencing the design, monitoring, and safety of gas-based energy systems. This study showed, for the first time, the application of ML techniques to predict the speed of sound in H<sub>2</sub>-rich gas mixtures. Using 665 experimental data, several algorithms were trained and optimized, with the ETR Regressor appearing as the most accurate model (R2 = 0.9996, RMSE = 6.3 m/s, MAPE = 0.2%). Other methods, including KNN, XGBoost, and SVR, also achieved high performance, confirming the strong capability of ML models in capturing complex nonlinear relationships that traditional regression methods could not. Beyond predictive accuracy, this work contributes interpretable insights through SHAP sensitivity analysis, which identified H<sub>2</sub> mole fraction and pressure as the dominant factors influencing sound speed. These findings not only align with physical expectations but also demonstrate the potential of explainable artificial intelligence in revealing underlying physical patterns in multicomponent gas systems. The main contribution of this research is the introduction of a fast, accurate, and interpretable data-driven framework for estimating the acoustic properties of H<sub>2</sub>-based mixtures, with direct relevance for real-time monitoring, diagnostics, and infrastructure design in H<sub>2</sub> energy systems. However, limitations remain, particularly regarding dataset size, compositional diversity, and the purely data-driven nature of the models. Future studies should focus on expanding experimental databases, developing hybrid physics-informed approaches, and incorporating uncertainty analysis to enhance robustness and practical deployment. Overall, this study establishes ML as a powerful and reliable tool for predicting the speed of sound in H<sub>2</sub>-rich mixtures, supporting the safe and efficient advancement of H<sub>2</sub> transport, storage, and utilization technologies.</p></section><section id="glossary1" class="glossary"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Abbreviations</h2>
