>Materials and methods   Sample collection and grouping   A total of 49 colorectal cancer (CRC) tissue samples were collected, including 28 RAS-mutant CRC samples and 21 wild-type CRC samples. For each participant, clinical data were recorded, including age, gender, CRC stage, and concentrations of tumor markers. The study was conducted with the approval of the institutional ethics committee, and all participants provided informed consent. Inclusion criteria: Patients with pathologically confirmed primary CRC; no preoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy; complete clinical data.Exclusion criteria: Complicated with other malignant tumors; severe hepatic or renal insufficiency; familial adenomatous polyposis or hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer; insufficient sample size (< 20 mg) or unqualified quality (e.g., the proportion of necrotic tissue > 30%). Sample collection and Preparation   The study involved the collection and analysis of 49 human intestinal tissue samples. After collection, tissue samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen (< 30 min) and then transferred to a −80 °C ultra-low temperature refrigerator for storage. Formalin fixation was not used (to avoid metabolite degradation or chemical modification). Accurately weighed 20 mg of each sample was placed into a 1.5 mL EP tube, followed by adding two small steel beads and 300 µL of methanol-water (V: V = 4:1, containing internal standards at a concentration of 4 µg/mL). The sample was pre-cooled at − 40℃ for 2 min and then homogenized at 45 Hz for 2 min. Subsequently, it underwent sonication in an ice-water bath for 10 min and was left to stand overnight at − 40℃. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm and 4℃ for 20 min, 150 µL of the supernatant was transferred into an LC-MS injection vial with a footed insert tube for analysis. A quality control (QC) sample was prepared by pooling equal volumes of the extract from all samples. Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis   An ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class plus) coupled with a high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo QE plus) was employed for the analysis. The chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm, with a stationary phase of tri-bond bonded C18 silica, suitable for polar metabolite analysis) Maintained at 45℃. The mobile phase consisted of water (containing 0.1% formic acid, eluent A) and acetonitrile (eluent B), with a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min and an injection volume of 3 µL. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0.0–2.0 min, 95% A and 5% B; 2.0–4.0 min, 95% A and 5% B; 4.0–8.0 min, 70% A and 30% B; 8.0–10.0 min, 50% A and 50% B; 10.0–14.0 min, 20% A and 80% B; 14.0–15.0 min, 0% A and 100% B; 15.0–15.1 min, 0% A and 100% B; 15.1–16.0 min, 95% A and 5% B. The mass spectrometer was operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes with the following parameters: spray voltage, 3800 V (positive) and 3000 V (negative); capillary temperature, 320℃; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 350℃; sheath gas flow rate, 35; auxiliary gas flow rate, 8; S - lens RF level, 50; mass range, m/z 70–1050; full MS resolution, 70,000; MS/MS resolution, 17,500; NCE/stepped NCE, 10, 20, 40. Negative controls: Blank solvent (methanol-water = 4:1, containing internal standards at the same concentration) was used as negative controls. These were injected synchronously with samples to monitor background interference. Sample randomization: Samples were injected in an order sorted by a random number table to avoid batch effects; quality control (QC) samples were evenly interspersed in the sequence (1 QC sample inserted every 10 samples) to evaluate system stability. Data processing and analysis   The raw data obtained from LC-MS analysis were processed using XCMS v4.5.1 software for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, and retention time correction [ 18 ,  19 ]. Compound identification was performed based on retention time, accurate mass, fragment ions, and isotopic distribution using databases such as the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), Lipidmaps (v2.3), METLIN, and the LuMet - Animal3.0 local database, which covers over 10,000 common and important metabolites [ 20 ,  21 ]. Data preprocessing steps included relative standard deviation (RSD) screening (RSD > 0.3 ions were deleted), missing value processing (ions with more than 50% missing values within a group were deleted, and remaining 0 values were replaced with half of the minimum ion intensity of all samples), 0-value replacement, and log2 transformation. In addition to log2 transformation, the data were normalized using Probabilistic Quotient Normalization (PQN) to correct for differences in total ion intensity between samples. Additionally, a scoring system was applied for compound identification, with a score threshold of 36 (out of 80), and metabolites with scores below 36 were considered as inaccurate identifications and were removed. The metabolites were classified into different levels (Level 1–4) based on their identification confidence, with Level 1 and Level 2 metabolites recommended for subsequent validation.   Multivariate statistical analysis was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) to distinguish metabolic profiles between groups and identify differential metabolites. To prevent overfitting of the models, 7-fold cross-validation and 200-fold response permutation testing were conducted [ 22 ]. Differential metabolites were screened based on the criteria of P-value < 0.05, fold change (FC) ≥ 1.2 or FC ≤ 1/1.2, VIP(Variable Importance in Projection > 1 [ 23 ,  24 ]. Various visualization tools, such as foldchange_bars plots, volcano plots, heatmaps, boxplots, lollipop plots, and correlation analysis, were employed to present the results of differential metabolite analysis. KEGG and Reactome pathway enrichment analyses were performed to explore the metabolic pathway changes associated with the differential metabolites [ 25 ]. Multivariate statistical analyses (PCA, OPLS-DA) were performed using the ropls package (v1.26.0) in R software (v4.2.1). Visualization of differential metabolites (volcano plots, heatmaps) was carried out using the ggplot2 package (v3.4.0). The P values were calculated using the two-sample t-test for independent samples (assuming normal distribution of the data, which was verified by the Shapiro-Wilk test). Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini - Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at < 0.05. Discussion   Our study provides a comprehensive analysis of the metabolic differences between RAS-mutant and wild-type colorectal cancer, revealing several key findings. The multivariate statistical analysis showed relatively large within-group variation and relatively small between-group variation. However, marked differences still existed between the M1 and W1 groups, with the M1 group showing a certain degree of dispersion and the W1 group appearing relatively tight-knit, indicating distinct metabolic characteristics between the two groups. The differential metabolite analysis identified 70 upregulated and 134 downregulated metabolites in the M1 group compared to the W1 group. Specific metabolites such as omega-hydroxy myristic acid and 4 S-hydroxylauric acid were upregulated, while others like SCHEMBL1056153 and Immepip were downregulated. Correlation analysis revealed that Convolvulinolic acid was positively correlated with omega-hydroxy myristic acid, and omega-hydroxy myristic acid was negatively correlated with Dansylproline. Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the differentially expressed metabolites were mainly enriched in pathways such as Choline metabolism in cancer, Drug metabolism-cytochrome P450, and beta-Alanine metabolism at KEGG Level 3. Reactome enrichment analysis also revealed associations with specific pathways. Collectively, these findings highlight the complex metabolic alterations in RAS-mutant colorectal cancer and offer new insights into its metabolic mechanisms. Our results are consistent with some previous studies that have reported metabolic differences between RAS-mutant and wild-type colorectal cancer, particularly in specific metabolic pathways like Choline metabolism in cancer and beta-Alanine metabolism [ 14 ,  26 ]. This consistency strengthens the reliability of our findings. Meanwhile, our study also uncovers some novel metabolic differences and pathway enrichments, such as the upregulation of specific metabolites like 3-Hydroxyvalproic acid and 4-hydroxy-tridecanoic acid, as well as the enrichment of certain Reactome pathways like (R-HSA-6809583) Retinoid metabolism disease events and (R-HSA-5657560) Hereditary fructose intolerance in RAS-mutant colorectal cancer, which have not been extensively reported in previous studies, these novel metabolic differences need further validation.These novel findings provide potential new directions for future research. The differential metabolites between RAS-mutant and wild-type colorectal cancer may serve as potential diagnostic biomarkers, aiding in the early diagnosis and differentiation of RAS-mutant colorectal cancer and enabling more precise treatment strategies. The metabolic pathways enriched in RAS-mutant colorectal cancer may offer potential therapeutic targets, such as specific enzymes or transporters in the Choline metabolism in cancer or beta-Alanine metabolism pathways, and the correlation analysis of metabolites may help identify key metabolic regulatory nodes, providing new ideas for treatment. Moreover, the metabolic differences between RAS-mutant and wild-type colorectal cancer may have prognostic significance, and further research could explore whether the metabolic characteristics of RAS-mutant colorectal cancer are associated with patient prognosis and survival, assisting in predicting disease progression and outcomes and guiding clinical treatment decisions. The abnormal choline metabolism identified in this study is associated with the PI3K/Akt pathway activated by RAS mutations-choline kinase (CHK) can be phosphorylated and activated by Akt, promoting the synthesis of phosphatidylcholine, which provides membrane components for the rapid proliferation of RAS-mutant cells and, together with enhanced glycolysis, meets the energy and material demands [ 27 – 29 ].However, our study has some limitations, such as a relatively small sample size, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the results, and the specific molecular mechanisms underlying the metabolic differences between RAS-mutant and wild-type colorectal cancer require further exploration as the study was primarily based on metabolic profiling analysis. No independent validation cohort or targeted MS/MS confirmation of key metabolites is provided. Single-cell metabolomics will be integrated in future studies to mitigate heterogeneity-related confounding. In the future, 50 independent CRC patients (25 with RAS mutation and 25 with wild-type RAS) will be included as a validation cohort, and metabolomic analysis will be repeated for verification of the findings. Future studies should build on these findings to further explore their clinical applications and significance, such as conducting multi-center, large-scale clinical studies to validate the diagnostic and prognostic value of the differential metabolites identified in this study, exploring the specific molecular mechanisms of the metabolic differences between RAS-mutant and wild-type colorectal cancer using techniques like gene editing and cell models, investigating the potential therapeutic effects of targeting metabolic pathways enriched in RAS-mutant colorectal cancer through preclinical studies and clinical trials, and further analyzing the correlation between the metabolic characteristics of RAS-mutant colorectal cancer and clinical outcomes to provide more precise guidance for clinical treatment. Conclusion   Our study reveals significant metabolic differences between RAS-mutant and wild-type colorectal cancer, identifying key differential metabolites and enriched pathways. These findings enhance understanding of RAS-mutant colorectal cancer metabolism, suggesting potential diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
