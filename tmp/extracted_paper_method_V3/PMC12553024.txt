<ul id="l0005" class="list" style="list-style-type:none">
<li id="o0005">
<span class="label">•</span><p class="display-inline" id="p0005">Mathematical abstraction is based on the physical and chemical nature of data.</p>
</li>
<li id="o0010">
<span class="label">•</span><p class="display-inline" id="p0010">Topological segmentation filters transition pixels in highly heterogeneous samples.</p>
</li>
<li id="o0015">
<span class="label">•</span><p class="display-inline" id="p0015">Reliable segmentation achieved as selection of processing parameters is not needed.</p>
</li>
</ul></section><section class="abstract" id="ab015"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h3>
<p>Image segmentation is an important challenge in mass spectrometry imaging data processing. Here, we report an unsupervised topological segmentation method adapted to the specific nature of mass spectrometry data. Unlike machine learning clustering algorithms, the proposed method retains the physical and chemical integrity of the mass spectrum, as no dimensionality reduction is required.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>Using the cosine similarity measure, we discard outliers, detect spectrally homogeneous regions, and filter pixels with mixed cell origin on the border of different tissue subtypes. Then, we evaluate the actual data manifold dimensionality to determine spectrally homogeneous regions within samples. The method was implemented to discriminate regions related to sections of aggressive human glial tumours analysed by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>Analysis of parallel sections reveals correlated region allocation throughout the sample. The presence of tumour cells decreases progressively from the tumour core toward the sample edge. Filtering pixels with mixed cellular content is essential for investigating highly heterogeneous tumour tissues and their infiltration regions. Therefore, only homogeneous regions were selected using topological segmentation, as identifying metabolic alterations associated with tumour infiltration and metastasis in the native microenvironment is critical for cancer biology.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h3>
<p>Topological segmentation helps filter pixels from transition zones where cells of different types contribute comparably to the resulting signal. Consequently, the regions identified by spectral similarity are homogeneous data clusters that represent the characteristic molecular composition of the analyzed cells while preserving their natural variability.</p></section></section><section id="s0005"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0020">Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) is a powerful tool for spatial omics research, applicable to a wide range of biological and biomedical questions. With the advancement of high-spatial-resolution ionisation techniques, MSI is increasingly employed to investigate tissue molecular composition at the cellular level [<a href="#b0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0005">[1]</a>, <a href="#b0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0010">[2]</a>, <a href="#b0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0015">[3]</a>, <a href="#b0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0020">[4]</a>, <a href="#b0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0025">[5]</a>, <a href="#b0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0030">[6]</a>]. Investigating the molecular heterogeneity of cells and tissues offers biological insights into both normal and pathological conditions, accounting for genetic and morphological variability as well as the native tissue microenvironment — factors that are difficult to replicate using in vitro models [<a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">7</a>,<a href="#b0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0040">8</a>]. Thus, MSI is a prospective tool in a variety of biomedical applications, especially in cancer biology [<a href="#b0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0045">9</a>,<a href="#b0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0050">10</a>]. The investigation of infiltrating tumours, like aggressive gliomas [<a href="#b0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0055">[11]</a>, <a href="#b0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0060">[12]</a>, <a href="#b0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0065">[13]</a>, <a href="#b0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0070">[14]</a>, <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>], requires spatially resolved metabolomics methods to assess metabolic reprogramming, which accompanies carcinogenesis, malignant transformation and infiltration on the tumour boundaries and metastasis [<a href="#b0080" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0080">[16]</a>, <a href="#b0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0085">[17]</a>, <a href="#b0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0090">[18]</a>, <a href="#b0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0095">[19]</a>]. MSI is a promising tool for investigating highly heterogeneous tumours and tumour infiltration regions, particularly those containing solitary malignant cells. However, the multidimensional data generated by MSI necessitates sophisticated data processing and interpretation techniques [<a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">20</a>,<a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">21</a>].</p>
<p id="p0025">The spatial distribution of an ion with a specific <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value can be used only in rare cases, typically when a predefined metabolite is intended to distinguish one cell type from others. However, in most instances, it is necessary to discriminate image regions corresponding to different tissue types within the sample. Direct comparison of MSI data with histological references is challenging due to geometric distortions introduced during histoprocessing, even when the same section is used for both imaging and histology [<a href="#b0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0110">22</a>,<a href="#b0115" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0115">23</a>]. Moreover, cells with similar morphology can appear in various molecular phenotypes, thus the molecular variability of cells within one type cannot be determined during the routine histological evaluation of a sample [<a href="#b0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0120">24</a>,<a href="#b0125" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0125">25</a>]. For this reason, an essential step in the MSI data analysis pipeline is the detection of homogeneous regions. Various supervised and unsupervised machine learning (ML) methods have been proposed for this task [<a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">20</a>,<a href="#b0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0130">[26]</a>, <a href="#b0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0135">[27]</a>, <a href="#b0140" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0140">[28]</a>]. Supervised methods, such as random forest or logistic regression, require initial knowledge of the exact number of regions, i.e., tissue subtypes in a sample and the location of “ideal pixels” representing spectra for every particular region [<a href="#b0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0145">29</a>,<a href="#b0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0150">30</a>]. Unsupervised methods [<a href="#b0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0135">27</a>,<a href="#b0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0155">31</a>], such as DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise), are more convenient for investigating heterogeneous samples, but the results are highly dependent on the implicit and often non-transparent selection of parameters [<a href="#b0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0130">26</a>]. Additionally, certain clustering approaches, such as k-means, are affected by the random initialization of starting points, which can result in irreproducible outcomes [<a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">20</a>,<a href="#b0160" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0160">32</a>]. Finally, many ML methods are typically insensitive to outliers, meaning every pixel in the image is assigned to a cluster. However, incorporating outlier spectra, such as those originating from dust particles or artificial sample defects, can increase error in the resulting data.</p>
<p id="p0030">A mass spectrum is an example of high-dimensional data, while ML clustering methods suffer from the curse of dimensionality, which drastically reduces clustering quality. Thus, dimensionality reduction is essential prior to the implementation of nearly all ML clustering approaches [<a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">20</a>,<a href="#b0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0155">31</a>,<a href="#b0165" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0165">[33]</a>, <a href="#b0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0170">[34]</a>, <a href="#b0175" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0175">[35]</a>]. Apart from linear dimensionality reduction, e.g., principal component analysis (PCA), manifold learning methods, such as Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), are proposed for nonlinear dimensionality reduction [<a href="#b0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0130">26</a>,<a href="#b0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0135">27</a>,<a href="#b0180" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0180">36</a>]. Indeed, mass spectra are inherently nonlinear due to the physical nature of mass spectrometry data acquisition, e.g., the presence of natural isotopic distribution, charge competition phenomenon, and the limited dynamic range of any given mass spectrometer. Consequently, the entire set of independent molecular features does not span the full mass range; rather, only a limited subset of ions and their isotopologues can be detected in a single experiment for any sample type and ionisation method, whether MALDI, DESI, or SIMS [<a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">20</a>,<a href="#b0180" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0180">[36]</a>, <a href="#b0185" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0185">[37]</a>, <a href="#b0190" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0190">[38]</a>, <a href="#b0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0195">[39]</a>, <a href="#b0200" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0200">[40]</a>, <a href="#b0205" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0205">[41]</a>]. Therefore, data points in a mass spectrometry image lie on a lower-dimensional manifold embedded in the high-dimensional space. Dimensionality reduction methods, however, inevitably distort the original data, generating new geometrical objects that are not physically or chemically accurate, potentially resulting in data simplification and the loss of characteristic features.</p>
<p id="p0035">In this work, we report a geometrical method (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>) for MSI data segmentation that leverages the specific nature of mass spectrometry data. Similar to ML clustering methods, our approach identifies mathematical regions that do not necessarily correspond to the biological regions of the sample. Here and below, the former will be referred to as “regions”, by default. Considering all spectra of an image as vectors in a multidimensional feature space, we use the cosine similarity measure (CSM) [<a href="#b0210" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0210">42</a>] to filter outlier pixels, roughly identify homogeneous regions, evaluate the manifold geometry of each region, and refine those regions—resulting in more accurate clustering of pixels based on their spectra. The parameters derived from manifold geometry enable the delineation of pixels corresponding to mixed cell areas, where different cell types are ionised concurrently at the tumour boundary or within the infiltration region. As a result, the final regions contain only homogeneous spectra, facilitating the identification of metabolic alterations between malignant and surrounding tissues. Here, we demonstrate the implementation of the proposed method on murine brain tissue as a model sample and highlight its advantages for investigating human glial tumour tissues.</p>

<p id="p0095"><a href="#s0045" class="usa-link">Section 3.1</a> focuses on the advantages of CSM implementation for mass spectrometry image segmentation. <a href="#s0050" class="usa-link">Section 3.2</a> introduces several basic notions and discusses their properties and relevance to MSI data. <section id="s0045"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Interpretation of a spectrum as a vector</h3>
<p id="p0100">Each mass spectrum of an image is interpreted as an <em>n</em>-dimensional vector in <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> space where each element is the intensity corresponding to a defined <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value for all pixels of the image (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1A</a>). We introduce the closeness between the vectors according to the formula of CSM [<a href="#b0210" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0210">42</a>]:</p>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="e0005"><tr><td class="formula"><math id="M26" display="block" altimg="si22.svg"><mrow><mi>g</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>u</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow><mo linebreak="goodbreak">=</mo><mfrac><mrow><msubsup><mo>∑</mo><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><mi>N</mi></msubsup><msub><mi>v</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><msub><mi>u</mi><mi>i</mi></msub></mrow><msqrt><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><munderover><mo>∑</mo><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><mi>N</mi></munderover><msubsup><mi>v</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi></mrow><mn>2</mn></msubsup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><munderover><mo>∑</mo><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><mi>N</mi></munderover><msubsup><mi>u</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi></mrow><mn>2</mn></msubsup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></msqrt></mfrac></mrow></math></td></tr></table>
<p id="p0105">This measure is not sensitive to the normalisation of vectors, thus, two spectra which differ only by normalisation will coincide with respect to this measure. Along with CSM, we will consider the cosine distance, which is defined as <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M27" altimg="si23.svg"><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo linebreak="badbreak" linebreakstyle="after">-</mo><mi>g</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>v</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>u</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math></span>. If the cosine distance between two vectors is <em>ε</em>, we will say that they are <em>ε</em>-far from each other. If we fix one “model” vector, then all vectors, which are <em>ε</em>-far from it, constitute an <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M28" altimg="si13.svg"><mrow><mi>n</mi></mrow></math></span>-dimensional cone (with the interior part) with the apex angle equal to <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M29" altimg="si24.svg"><mrow><mi>φ</mi><mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">=</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">arccos</mi><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>1</mn><mo linebreak="badbreak" linebreakstyle="after">-</mo><mi>ε</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></math></span> and the model vector as the axis (here and below, we will illustrate our construction by the 3D-pictures, but all ideas are valid in the space of higher dimension as well). Note that because of the non-negativity of all intensities in a spectrum, the CSM can take values from 0 to 1, thus the angle between any two vectors will never exceed 90 degrees.</p>
<p id="p0110">Mass spectrum interpretation as a multi-dimensional vector is a simple mathematical abstraction and, if more than one spectrum can be attributed to each pixel of an image, extra dimensions can be added to the vector to consider data provided by multiple spectra. This option is attractive regarding the possibility of undertaking dual-polarity MSI [<a href="#b0215" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0215">43</a>] or implementing ion mobility separation prior to mass detection [<a href="#b0220" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0220">[44]</a>, <a href="#b0225" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0225">[45]</a>, <a href="#b0230" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0230">[46]</a>]. The CSM allows straightforward comparison of mass spectra without the necessity of dimensionality reduction. The other important advantage of CSM over many other metrics, e.g., Euclidian distance, is its insensitivity to the normalisation of vectors, thus, two spectra which differ only by normalisation will coincide with respect to CSM. Although such similar spectra are rarely encountered in real data, minor variations in peak intensities result in negligible deviations of the CSM from 1. Implementing a weighted CSM can further mitigate the effects of detector noise and minor pixel-to-pixel variations. However, it also tends to reduce the angular separation between vectors corresponding to distinct sample regions, potentially complicating the delineation of different cell populations in highly heterogeneous samples. Moreover, calculating weighted CSM, as well as other metrics such as Pearson's correlation coefficient, demands increased computational effort, rendering them less suitable for processing high spatial resolution images [<a href="#b0210" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0210">42</a>,<a href="#b0235" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0235">47</a>].</p></section><section id="s0050"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Manifold dimensionality evaluation</h3>
<p id="p0115">Assuming that spectra within a given region are similar and distinct from those in other regions, it is intuitive to search for regions shaped like cones with a small apex angles. However, determining the appropriate apex angle and the axis of a cone corresponding to a specific region is nontrivial. To address this, we introduce the notion of density within a cone, allowing us to identify the densest sheaves of vectors that likely represent coherent spectral regions. First, let us fix a vector <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M30" altimg="si5.svg"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></math></span> and consider a cone with the apex angle <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M31" altimg="si9.svg"><mrow><mi>ψ</mi></mrow></math></span>. All vectors from the image that lie inside this cone we denote by <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M32" altimg="si25.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>ψ</mi></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> and the number of vectors in it by <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M33" altimg="si26.svg"><mrow><mfenced open="|" close="|"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>ψ</mi></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>v</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mfenced></mrow></math></span>. This set increases with the increase of the apex angle <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M34" altimg="si9.svg"><mrow><mi>ψ</mi></mrow></math></span> of a cone. We say that the vectors are located equally dense if, with the increase of the angle by <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M35" altimg="si27.svg"><mrow><mi>k</mi></mrow></math></span> times, the number of the vectors inside the cone <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M36" altimg="si25.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>ψ</mi></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> increases by <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M37" altimg="si28.svg"><mrow><msup><mrow><mi>k</mi></mrow><mi>n</mi></msup></mrow></math></span> times, where <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M38" altimg="si13.svg"><mrow><mi>n</mi></mrow></math></span> is the dimension of a space. Although the enclosing space is <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M39" altimg="si13.svg"><mrow><mi>n</mi></mrow></math></span>-dimensional, the spectral vectors may effectively reside on a lower-dimensional manifold within it. This reflects underlying correlations or constraints in the data, suggesting that the true variability is confined to fewer degrees of freedom than the ambient space implies. We say that the density of a set of vectors does not decrease if, after multiplying the angle by <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M40" altimg="si27.svg"><mrow><mi>k</mi></mrow></math></span>, the number of vectors in <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M41" altimg="si25.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>ψ</mi></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> increases by <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M42" altimg="si29.svg"><mrow><msup><mrow><mi>k</mi></mrow><mi>α</mi></msup></mrow></math></span> times, where <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M43" altimg="si30.svg"><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow></math></span> is the dimension of a subspace (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1B</a>). Thus, in the case of a two-dimensional subspace, after tripling the angle, the number of vectors must increase by 9 times.</p>
<p id="p0120">However, as the dimension <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M44" altimg="si30.svg"><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow></math></span> of a manifold is unknown, we will solve the other problem: we start to increase the apex angle and each time find such <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M45" altimg="si30.svg"><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow></math></span> that the number of vectors increases by <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M46" altimg="si29.svg"><mrow><msup><mrow><mi>k</mi></mrow><mi>α</mi></msup></mrow></math></span> if the angle is increased by <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M47" altimg="si27.svg"><mrow><mi>k</mi></mrow></math></span> times:</p>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="e0010"><tr><td class="formula"><math id="M48" display="block" altimg="si31.svg"><mrow><mfrac><mrow><mfenced open="|" close="|"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>ψ</mi></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>v</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mfenced></mrow><mrow><mfenced open="|" close="|"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>0</mn></msub></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>v</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mfrac><mo linebreak="goodbreak">=</mo><msup><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mfrac><mi>ψ</mi><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>0</mn></msub></mfrac></mrow></mfenced></mrow><mi>α</mi></msup></mrow></math></td></tr></table>
<p id="p0125">If the initial angle <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M49" altimg="si32.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>0</mn></msub></mrow></math></span> is small enough (smaller than <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M50" altimg="si33.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mi mathvariant="italic">min</mi></mrow></msub></mrow></math></span>, which is the angle between the closest vectors in the whole image), then <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M51" altimg="si34.svg"><mrow><mfenced open="|" close="|"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>0</mn></msub></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>v</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mfenced><mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></math></span> and thus</p>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="e0015"><tr><td class="formula"><math id="M52" display="block" altimg="si35.svg"><mrow><mi>α</mi><mo linebreak="goodbreak">=</mo><mfrac><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">ln</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>ψ</mi></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>v</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">ln</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mfrac><mi>ψ</mi><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>0</mn></msub></mfrac></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></mfrac></mrow></math></td></tr></table>
<p>This value, considered as a function of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M53" altimg="si9.svg"><mrow><mi>ψ</mi></mrow></math></span>, starts to decrease for rather large <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M54" altimg="si9.svg"><mrow><mi>ψ</mi></mrow></math></span> as the number of vectors in the entire image is finite. Moreover, as not all spectra are identical, at first this function increases (i.e., the number of vectors inside the cone increases). Thus, we can expect that it has a point of maximum (see <a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1C</a>). The angle <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M55" altimg="si9.svg"><mrow><mi>ψ</mi></mrow></math></span> corresponding to the maximal dimension will be called optimal to the given axis <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M56" altimg="si5.svg"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></math></span>. Numerically, we calculate the function at selected points <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M57" altimg="si36.svg"><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>×</mo><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>0</mn></msub></mrow></math></span> (where <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M58" altimg="si37.svg"><mrow><mn>0</mn><mo>&lt;</mo><mi>i</mi><mo>&lt;</mo><mi>π</mi><mo stretchy="false">/</mo><mn>2</mn><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>0</mn></msub><mo>,</mo><mi>i</mi><mo>∈</mo><mi mathvariant="double-struck">N</mi></mrow></math></span>), and choose the largest of these values as the optimal.</p>
<p id="p0130">So, to each vector <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M59" altimg="si5.svg"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></math></span> we assign two numbers: the maximal dimension <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M60" altimg="si38.svg"><mrow><mi>α</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> and the optimal angle <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M61" altimg="si6.svg"><mrow><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span>. Note that the values of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M62" altimg="si30.svg"><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow></math></span> do not have to be integer by the mathematical definition. Although we refer to <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M63" altimg="si30.svg"><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow></math></span> as the dimension, the nonlinearity of data originated from the molecular basis of the mass spectrum [<a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">20</a>], especially ion intensity correlations due to natural isotopic distribution and adduct formations, allows the consideration of manifold dimensions as fractional values.</p></section><section id="s0055"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3. Image segmentation principle</h3>
<p id="p0135">The actual manifold dimension varies across spectra, and it is reasonable to assume that the central vector of each sheaf exhibits the maximal value of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M64" altimg="si38.svg"><mrow><mi>α</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span>. This assumption enables the identification of spectrally dense regions in the image using the following algorithm: (a) select the initial vector <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M65" altimg="si5.svg"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></math></span> with maximal <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M66" altimg="si38.svg"><mrow><mi>α</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> across the image; (b) consider a set of vectors <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M67" altimg="si39.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mrow><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> belonging a cone with an apex angle <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M68" altimg="si6.svg"><mrow><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> around the axis <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M69" altimg="si5.svg"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></math></span>; (c) Select the next set of vectors <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M70" altimg="si40.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mrow><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>u</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>u</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> where <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M71" altimg="si41.svg"><mrow><mi>u</mi></mrow></math></span> is a vector with maximal <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M72" altimg="si42.svg"><mrow><mi>α</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>u</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> among the remaining vectors, ensuring that <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M73" altimg="si39.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mrow><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M74" altimg="si40.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mrow><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>u</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>u</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> are disjoint; and (d) iteratively repeat the process until no further disjoint sets can be formed. After several iterations, we obtain several homogeneous regions composed of the densest vector sheaves, each characterized by a relatively small apex angle <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M75" altimg="si18.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mi>i</mi></msub></mrow></math></span>. However, this algorithm has one major disadvantage: if <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M76" altimg="si9.svg"><mrow><mi>ψ</mi></mrow></math></span> is too small, which often holds for the vector <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M77" altimg="si5.svg"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></math></span> corresponding to maximal <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M78" altimg="si30.svg"><mrow><mi>α</mi></mrow></math></span>, the resulting region will be homogeneous, but contain too few spectra, failing to capture the full variability of the area (<a href="#s0100" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S1</a>A). As a consequence, the region with high chemical variability may fragment into several tiny subregions, each capturing only a limited portion of the true variability present within the cells. To solve this problem, we need to increase the value of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M79" altimg="si18.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mi>i</mi></msub></mrow></math></span> which is physically reasonable since the spectra for different regions differ more from each other than the spectra within a single region. Note that different regions can generally require different apex angles, but it is essential to select appropriate values for each region to prevent disruption and maintain disjointness. However, if we simply increase the value of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M80" altimg="si18.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mi>i</mi></msub></mrow></math></span> to the optimal angle in every iteration, the angle will increase too quickly, resulting in fewer disjoint sets of vectors being selected (<a href="#s0100" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S1</a>B). Thus, we propose selecting preliminary regions using a fixed angle <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M81" altimg="si1.svg"><mrow><mi>φ</mi></mrow></math></span>, which will be consistent across all regions. The value <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M82" altimg="si1.svg"><mrow><mi>φ</mi></mrow></math></span> will be called a zero-approximation, as the regions obtained using this apex angle will require further refinement and, consequently, adjustment of cone angle values. The preliminary regions will then be refined according to the actual dimensions of the corresponding manifolds. However, accurate estimation of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M83" altimg="si1.svg"><mrow><mi>φ</mi></mrow></math></span> necessitates outlier filtering prior to segmentation.</p>
<p id="p0140">Note that if the angle between two vectors is <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M84" altimg="si43.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">χ</mi></mrow></math></span>, then cones with apex angles less than <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M85" altimg="si44.svg"><mrow><mi>χ</mi><mo stretchy="false">/</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></math></span> and these vectors as axes will not intersect. Moreover, any two vectors belonging to the union of two non-disjoint cones with apex angles equal to <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M86" altimg="si43.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">χ</mi></mrow></math></span> will form an angle less than or equal to <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M87" altimg="si45.svg"><mrow><mn>4</mn><mi mathvariant="normal">χ</mi></mrow></math></span>. Thus, if the two farthest vectors in the image form an angle <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M88" altimg="si46.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Φ</mi></mrow></math></span>, then any two cones with apex angles equal to <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M89" altimg="si47.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Φ</mi><mo stretchy="false">/</mo><mn>4</mn></mrow></math></span>, each containing only one of those vectors, will be disjoint. Therefore, in the “model” case (where there are no outliers and every vector belongs to one of two regions), the apex angle must be <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M90" altimg="si47.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Φ</mi><mo stretchy="false">/</mo><mn>4</mn></mrow></math></span>. However, the most distant vectors lying outside both regions can form angles up to 90 degrees. The presence of such outliers makes <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M91" altimg="si47.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Φ</mi><mo stretchy="false">/</mo><mn>4</mn></mrow></math></span> unreasonably large. To address this problem and filter outliers, we use the optimal angle <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M92" altimg="si6.svg"><mrow><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> discussed above. Vectors for which <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M93" altimg="si6.svg"><mrow><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> is relatively large tend to lie outside the major set, as the number of vectors inside the corresponding cone increases significantly with the angle. By excluding such points, we decrease the maximal angle <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M94" altimg="si46.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Φ</mi></mrow></math></span>, along with the value of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M95" altimg="si47.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Φ</mi><mo stretchy="false">/</mo><mn>4</mn></mrow></math></span>. It is therefore reasonable to set a threshold of 45 degrees, since any two vectors form an angle not exceeding 90 degrees, and any cone with an apex angle of 45 degrees or greater will contain vectors located only on “one side” of it.</p>
<p id="p0145">Similarly, for segmentation into <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M96" altimg="si13.svg"><mrow><mi>n</mi></mrow></math></span> regions, the preliminary angle can be taken as <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M97" altimg="si12.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Φ</mi><mo stretchy="false">/</mo><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></mrow></math></span>, since the “worst” case occurs when the cone axes lie on a single plane. However, in the context of detecting multiple regions, the value of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M98" altimg="si12.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Φ</mi><mo stretchy="false">/</mo><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></mrow></math></span> may become unreasonably small due to the low probability of such a “worst” case occurrence in a real tissue sample. Therefore, we recommend using <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M99" altimg="si14.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mi mathvariant="italic">med</mi></mrow></msub></mrow></math></span> – median value of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M100" altimg="si6.svg"><mrow><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> across the entire image, as the value for <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M101" altimg="si1.svg"><mrow><mi>φ</mi></mrow></math></span>, which appears to be reasonable in the general case (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>, <a href="#s0100" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figs. 1C, 2, and 3</a>).</p>

For each vector <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M106" altimg="si5.svg"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></math></span> we consider a set <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M107" altimg="si48.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>φ</mi></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>v</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></math></span><em>.</em> Next, we examine all pairs (<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M108" altimg="si48.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>φ</mi></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>v</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></math></span>, <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M109" altimg="si49.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>φ</mi></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>u</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></math></span>) of disjoint sets (note that the corresponding cones need not be disjoint, as their intersection may contain no vectors from the image), and among these pairs, we select the one for which the total number of vectors |<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M110" altimg="si48.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>φ</mi></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>v</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></math></span>|+|<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M111" altimg="si50.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>φ</mi></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>u</mi></mrow></mrow></math></span>)| is maximal. The corresponding sets <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M112" altimg="si51.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">=</mo><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>φ</mi></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>v</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M113" altimg="si52.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">=</mo><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>φ</mi></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>u</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></math></span> will be the preliminary regions (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1D</a>).</p></section><section id="s0060"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.4. Apex angle refinement</h3>
<p id="p0155">Suppose we determined the regions <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M114" altimg="si53.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mn>1</mn></msub></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M115" altimg="si54.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mn>2</mn></msub></mrow></math></span> corresponding to the zero-approximation <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M116" altimg="si1.svg"><mrow><mi>φ</mi></mrow></math></span> of an apex angle. Now, we try to improve them using the same reasonings as described above: on the one hand, the cone with an excessive value of the angle will enclose superfluous vectors that do not belong to an actual region; on the other hand, a too small value of the angle provides a cone with poor variability of vectors. Thus, it was decided to take the median value <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M117" altimg="si55.svg"><mrow><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M118" altimg="si56.svg"><mrow><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></mrow></math></span> of optimal angles along the preliminary regions <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M119" altimg="si53.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mn>1</mn></msub></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M120" altimg="si54.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mn>2</mn></msub></mrow></math></span> as the refined value of the apex angle for the final cone.</p>
<p id="p0160">All the reasonings above are based on the assumption that the axis vector of a cone is already known. However, using only the initial data, it is impossible to predict these vectors even approximately. To find the optimal axes, we search for the cones containing as many points as possible with refined angles <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M121" altimg="si55.svg"><mrow><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M122" altimg="si56.svg"><mrow><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></mrow></math></span>, respectively, and axes belonging to the corresponding preliminary regions. Note that if we find <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M123" altimg="si5.svg"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></math></span> from the first preliminary set such that the number of points in <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M124" altimg="si57.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> is maximal, and do the same with the second preliminary set to find <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M125" altimg="si58.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>u</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span>, then <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M126" altimg="si57.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M127" altimg="si58.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>u</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> can be non-disjoint (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a> column 2, orange dots). So, we search for disjoint sets <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M128" altimg="si57.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M129" altimg="si58.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>u</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span>, where <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M130" altimg="si5.svg"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></math></span> is a vector from the first preliminary region and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M131" altimg="si41.svg"><mrow><mi>u</mi></mrow></math></span> is a vector from the second preliminary region, such that <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M132" altimg="si59.svg"><mrow><mfenced open="|" close="|"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>v</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow><mo>+</mo><msub><mi>S</mi><mover accent="true"><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo stretchy="false">∼</mo></mover></msub><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi>u</mi></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></mfenced></mrow></math></span> is maximal (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1E</a>). Such disjoint sets will provide the final regions. Note that all these reasonings can be carried out similarly for the general case of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M133" altimg="si13.svg"><mrow><mi>n</mi></mrow></math></span> regions.</p></section><section id="s0065"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.5. Tissue samples analysis</h3>
<p id="p0165">To prove the method capabilities, we analyse MALDI-TOF images of human glioblastoma tissue (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>, <a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>, <a href="#s0100" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S1</a>) and MALDI-Orbitrap image of rat brain tissue (<a href="#s0100" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S2</a>). Parallel sections of a sample from tumour border (astrocytoma WHO CNS grade 4 [<a href="#b0240" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0240">48</a>]) show the correlated regions allocation through the sample. The presence of tumour cells increases through the sections (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>): tumour region revealed by H&amp;E staining is clearly visible on a deep section (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3C</a>), while the presence of tumour cells decreases towards the edge of the sample (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3B</a>). No pixels related to tumour were segregated on the edge section (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3A</a>), only the tumour infiltration zone remains which illustrates the diffuse and infiltrative nature of glial tumours. Unlike topological segmentation, typical machine learning techniques fail to provide comparable results in the general case (<a href="#s0100" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figs. S4-S6</a>). Each ML clustering approach provides different regions, which are not always comparable to each other, even in case of alternating numbers of selected regions, with stained section or reference atlas. Individual selection of parameters for each image will improve the ML clustering results [<a href="#b0245" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0245">49</a>], however, the lack of the necessity for such a tuning is an essential advantage of topological segmentation.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="f0015"><h4 class="obj_head">Fig. 3.</h4>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=12553024_gr3.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/d9e8/12553024/85552b578f57/gr3.jpg" loading="lazy" id="lk00015" height="302" width="787" alt="Fig. 3"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/f0015/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>Human astrocytoma tissue images. The parallel sections are obtained consecutively (from left to right). No distortion correction was applied; the H&amp;E-stained section is heavily distorted due to the lack of fixation. Section thickness is 12 μm, the axial distance between each pair of sections is approximately 24 μm.</p></figcaption></figure><p id="p0170">By discrimination of the infiltration region and rejection of pixels not related to homogeneous parts of regions of interest, we obtain mass spectra related solely to the tumour or reactive lesions without interference from each other, which is common in the infiltration zone, or from necrosis, which is also typical for high-grade brain tumours. The discrimination of reactive lesions from the infiltration region is a crucial task, since reactive lesions may be used as non-tumour controls for the investigation of tumour metabolic alterations while still being considered non-tumour pathological tissue [<a href="#b0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0095">19</a>,<a href="#b0250" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0250">50</a>], and might be resected during surgical intervention, in contrast to non-pathological brain tissues that must not be resected under any circumstances for ethical reasons.</p>
<p id="p0175">Interestingly, the <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M134" altimg="si14.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mi mathvariant="italic">med</mi></mrow></msub></mrow></math></span> value can be close to <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M135" altimg="si12.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Φ</mi><mo stretchy="false">/</mo><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></mrow></math></span> for the optimal number of regions <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M136" altimg="si13.svg"><mrow><mi>n</mi></mrow></math></span> (<a href="#s0100" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3</a>), so it appears to be natural to use it to determine the number of regions as <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M137" altimg="si60.svg"><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo linebreak="goodbreak" linebreakstyle="after">=</mo><mi>r</mi><mi>o</mi><mi>u</mi><mi>n</mi><mi>d</mi><mrow><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Φ</mi><mo stretchy="false">/</mo><mn>2</mn><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mi mathvariant="italic">med</mi></mrow></msub></mrow></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></span>. However, for the other section obtained from the same glioblastoma tissue sample (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>, <a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>), the reasonable number of regions increases from two to three, while <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M138" altimg="si14.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mi mathvariant="italic">med</mi></mrow></msub></mrow></math></span> and <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M139" altimg="si46.svg"><mrow><mi mathvariant="normal">Φ</mi></mrow></math></span> remain almost unchanged. The third region clearly segregates on the section, so it becomes possible to follow the tumour invasion as the tumour region appears inside the infiltration region (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>). In contrast, the previous section (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3A</a> and <a href="#s0100" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S2</a>) does not show the presence of a tumour region since it is not possible to find the third region using any value of zero-approximation <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M140" altimg="si1.svg"><mrow><mi>φ</mi></mrow></math></span>. The overestimation of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M141" altimg="si1.svg"><mrow><mi>φ</mi></mrow></math></span> will lead to an invalid result of segmentation (<a href="#s0100" class="usa-link">Supplementary Fig. S3</a>, top right panel); while underestimation (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>, middle right panel) leads to a reasonable result, but the total number of pixels attributed to any of the regions decreases, so some variability of regions is lost. In the latter case, the reduced size of the tumour infiltration region, as well as the total number of pixels in the preliminary sets, indicates the incorrect choice of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M142" altimg="si1.svg"><mrow><mi>φ</mi></mrow></math></span>. Thus, the median value <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M143" altimg="si14.svg"><mrow><msub><mi>ψ</mi><mrow><mi mathvariant="italic">med</mi></mrow></msub></mrow></math></span> can be used for a rough estimation of the number of regions if all regions within an image consist of different cell types, but not in the case of tracking the simultaneous invasion and malignant transformation of cells.</p></section></section><section id="s0070"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0180">We present here a method for mass spectrometry imaging data segmentation based on linear algebra and topology approaches. The method does not require any dimensionality reduction, so it preserves the physical and chemical interpretability of the data and minimises the loss of the characteristic features during data processing. The segmentation is based on spectra similarity, which filters out pixels from transitional zones where signals from multiple cell types overlap. Thus, the regions determined using our method are homogeneous clusters that reflect the characteristic molecular composition of specific cell types, while preserving their intrinsic variability. The resulting regions can then be used for further analysis to determine statistically different <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> features between regions, their identification, and biological interpretation.</p>
<p id="p0185">The implementation of topological segmentation to heterogeneous tissue samples helps to obtain the lipid profile of different cell types grown in a physiological environment. This approach holds strong potential for studying cancer cell metabolism under conditions that closely mimic the native tissue environment.</p></section><section id="s0075"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Funding Support</h2>
