</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="S1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Purpose</h3>
<p id="P1">Previous studies in phantoms and animals using animal MR systems have shown promising results in using oscillating gradient spin echo (OGSE) diffusion acquisition to depict microstructure information. The OGSE approach has also been shown to be a sensitive biomarker of tumor treatment response and white matter-related diseases. Translating these studies to a human MR scanner faces multiple challenges due to the much weaker gradient system. The goals of the current study are to optimize the OGSE acquisition for a human MR system and investigate its applicability in the in vivo human brain.</p></section><section id="S2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p id="P2">An analytical analysis of the OGSE modulation spectrum was provided. Based on this analysis and thorough simulation experiments, the OGSE acquisition was optimized in terms of diffusion waveform shape, waveform timing, and sequence timing – to achieve higher diffusion sensitivity and better sampling of the diffusion spectrum.</p></section><section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p id="P3">The trapezoid-cosine waveform was found to be the optimal OGSE waveform. At the three employed peak encoding frequencies of 18 Hz, 44 Hz, and 63 Hz, the waveform polarity for the least blurry sampling of the diffusion spectrum was 90+/180−, 90+/180+, and 90+/180+, respectively. For the highest diffusion to noise ratio (DNR) at 63 Hz, the <em>b</em>-value was 200 s/mm<sup>2</sup> and the echo time was 116 ms. Using the optimized sequence, a frequency dependence of the measured ADCs was observed in white-matter-dominant regions such as the corpus callosum.</p></section><section id="S4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p id="P4">The obtained results demonstrate, for the first time, the potential of utilizing an OGSE acquisition for investigating microstructure information on a human MR system.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> oscillating gradient, OGSE, restricted diffusion, gradient encoding spectrum, diffusion-to-noise ratio, 3 T human MR</p></section></section><section id="S5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p id="P5">In the presence of cellular barriers, such as tissue membranes and intracellular organelles, water diffusion in biological tissue is restricted. The microstructural composition and the restrictions these barriers impose on diffusive motion lead to apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) measurements that depend on the “shutter speed” (or formally the diffusion time) of the diffusion-probing technique (<a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">1</a>,<a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">2</a>). Traditionally, MR diffusion methods employ pulsed-gradient spin-echo (PGSE) encoding with a diffusion time on the order of 20–40 milliseconds (in human MR systems) to achieve high diffusion sensitivity. At these long diffusion times, the measured ADC reflects the effects of restricting boundaries at the 10 – 15 μm length scale. In other words, at these diffusion times the size-dependent ADC measurements are “saturated” for neuronal tissue and the effects of small cells or sub-cellular diffusion restriction cannot be observed.</p>
<p id="P6">Oscillating gradient spin echo (OGSE) encoding has been proposed as an alternative method to significantly reduce the diffusion time while maintaining reasonable diffusion sensitivity (<a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a>,<a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a>). However, the conventional <em>q</em>-space interpretation of the PGSE diffusion measurement is not suitable for the more complicated OGSE diffusion measurement (<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>). Instead the velocity autocorrelation function framework proposed by Stepisnik (<a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a>) can be used. As with any type of motion, restricted diffusion can be fully characterized by the velocity autocorrelation function, the Fourier transform of which is the diffusion spectrum. In the presence of barriers, molecules rebound and/or experience a reduction in velocity, yielding negative velocity autocorrelation (<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>). As a result, the corresponding diffusion spectrum has a deficit at a low frequency and increases toward a constant with increased frequency. It has been shown in simple geometries that this slope of change in the diffusion spectrum depends on the size of the restriction (<a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a>). Therefore, an OGSE acquisition combined with subsequent diffusion spectrum analysis (<a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a>) can reveal sub-cellular microstructural information of the tissue based on the dependence of the measured diffusion spectrum on the frequency of the employed OGSE waveform.</p>
<p id="P7">Several OGSE studies have been recently reported in phantoms (<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a>–<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">13</a>) and in rodent brains (<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>,<a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a>). In a study on mice, a new tissue contrast was discovered in the maps of the rate of change in ADC with respect to diffusion gradient oscillation frequencies (<a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a>). The same study also demonstrated the high sensitivity of OGSE to microstructural changes associated with cuprizone-induced demyelinating lesions. In another study on rats, OGSE was shown to be a potentially earlier and more sensitive indicator of tumor treatment response than conventional PGSE (<a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">15</a>).</p>
<p id="P8">The great potential of OGSE reported in previous phantom and animal studies urges its translation into human studies. The difficulty in applying OGSE in the in vivo human brain is related to the reduced gradient strength of a human MR system. <a href="#T2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a> compares the specifications of the gradient hardware used in a typical animal MR system and a typical human MR system. With the same diffusion-encoding waveform, the limited maximum gradient strength of 50 mT/m achievable on a typical human system (compared to 1000 mT/m achievable on an animal system) significantly reduces the maximum <em>b</em>-value, maximum encoding frequency, and increases the echo time. As a result, the signal-to-noise ratio of the estimated ADC (or equivalently the diffusion-to-noise ratio, DNR) of an OGSE experiment in a human MR system drops significantly (12 times for the example given in <a href="#T2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>) as compared to that in an animal MR system. Additionally, since the maximum OGSE frequency achieved at a certain <em>b</em>-value also drops significantly, the range of frequencies on the diffusion spectrum that is measurable using human MR systems is very limited. As a result, sensitivity to tissue microstructure of OGSE measurements deployed on human MR systems might be reduced (<a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a>).</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/T2/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section><p id="P9">Another challenge in implementing OGSE is the finite duration of the diffusion encoding waveform. This challenge is common to both animal and human MR systems. Due to the finite duration of the waveform, the resulting diffusion gradient modulation spectrum has non-zero bandwidth (<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>). Therefore, the measured ADC no longer reflects the value of the diffusion spectrum at a single frequency. Rather, the measured ADC is the weighted sum of the diffusion spectrum over the non-zero bandwidth of the diffusion gradient modulation spectrum. Therefore, the larger the bandwidth of the diffusion gradient modulation spectrum, the more “blurred” the estimated diffusion spectrum.</p>
<p id="P10">The objective of the current study is to investigate the applicability of OGSE in detecting the frequency dependence of the measured ADC resulting from restricted diffusion in the in vivo human brain. To help surmount the gradient limitations of typical human MR systems, the OGSE diffusion waveform was optimized to exploit the full capacity of the available gradient system – with the aim to maximize the DNR, OGSE frequency, and the localization of the diffusion gradient modulation spectrum. The proposed waveform optimization is described in detail in the Theory section. First, an analytical description of the practical finite-length OGSE waveform was derived, which facilitated the choice of waveform timing and polarity for the optimal diffusion gradient modulation spectrum. Next, the waveform shape was modified to utilize the maximum slew rate and amplitude of the gradient system. Finally, sequence parameters (echo time, <em>b</em>-value) were optimized for DNR. Thorough simulation experiments were carried out to show the superiority of the optimized OGSE waveform over the previously employed OGSE waveforms. The obtained in vivo results are the first to show the frequency/time dependence of ADC using OGSE on a human MRI scanner, potentially revealing the new type of microstructural contrast previously achieved on animal MR systems (<a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a>).</p></section><section id="S6"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">THEORY</h2>
<p id="P11">This section describes our derivation of the analytical formula of the diffusion gradient modulation spectrum (when the OGSE waveform has finite duration), and describes how the OGSE waveform can be optimized. For completeness, a brief overview of the basics of the spectral analysis of restricted diffusion is provided.</p>

Consequently, based on <a href="#FD5" class="usa-link">Eq. [5]</a>, the waveform polarity should be selected so that it yields a higher value for

<p id="P27">As discussed previously, since cosine and double-sine waveforms have a tunable peak frequency, they typically have been the waveforms of choice in previous studies. However, when the maximum gradient amplitude is limited, such as in the case of a human MR system, a cosine waveform is superior to a double-sine waveform. The reason is that for a given peak encoding frequency and echo time, the maximum achievable <em>b</em>-value using a cosine waveform is 
<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M15" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mfrac><mrow><mn>8</mn><mi>N</mi><mo>-</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><mrow><mn>6</mn><mi>N</mi></mrow></mfrac></mrow></math></span> times higher than the maximum achievable <em>b</em>-value using a double-sine waveform (where <em>N</em> is the total number of cosine periods or half the number of sine periods (<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>)).</p>
<p id="P28">A further improvement of the cosine waveform is possible in terms of increasing the maximum achievable <em>b</em>-value: when operating at the maximum slew rate and the maximum gradient amplitude, trapezoid lobes have at least 
<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M16" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mfrac><mrow><mi>π</mi><mo>-</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><mn>2</mn></mfrac></mrow></math></span> times higher gradient-time integrals than sine/cosine lobes with the same duration, resulting in a higher <em>b</em>-value. Therefore, intuitively, the cosine waveform can be approximated by an oscillating time-matched trapezoid waveform – called “trapezoid-cosine” – to achieve an increased maximum b-value while ideally maintaining the encoding spectrum.</p></section><section id="S10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Optimization of diffusion-to-noise ratio (DNR)</h3>

<section id="S12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Simulations</h3>
<p id="P32">Simulation studies were performed to confirm the superiority of the trapezoid-cosine waveform over the cosine waveform and to show the dependence of the frequency localization on the OGSE waveform polarity. Without loss of generality, the dependence of frequency localization on the OGSE waveform polarity was investigated for the case where the frequency of the waveform is 62.5 Hz.</p>
<p id="P33">Simulations were also performed to find the acquisition parameters (TE, <em>b</em>-value) that would yield the optimal DNR using a wide range of T<sub>2</sub> values that covers both gray and white matter at 3 T: T<sub>2</sub> = 50 – 100 ms (<a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a>), and a wide range of diffusion coefficients that covers different level of diffusivity: D = 250 – 1500 × 10<sup>−6</sup> mm<sup>2</sup>/s. In general, the higher the encoding frequency of the diffusion-encoding gradient waveform, the lower the maximum <em>b</em>-value that can be achieved for the same TE (i.e., lower DNR). Therefore, we chose to optimize our DNR at the highest encoding frequency that would be implemented, which is 63 Hz. The reason for this highest encoding frequency is that at diffusion-encoding frequency &gt; 63 Hz, the resulting echo time is longer than 120 ms even for a b-value of only 100 s/mm<sup>2</sup>. The DNR simulation was performed with different echo times for double-sine, cosine, and trapezoid-cosine diffusion-encoding waveforms.</p>
<p id="P34">All simulations assumed gradient hardware limits: a maximum gradient amplitude of 50 mT/m and a maximum slew rate of 100 mT/m/ms. The sequence timing was calculated based on an in-plane resolution of 2 × 2 mm<sup>2</sup>; 3-interleaf EPI readout trajectory with partial Fourier encoding with 24 over scan lines; and a readout bandwidth of +/−125kHz.</p></section><section id="S13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">In vivo Measurements</h3>
<p id="P35">Four healthy volunteers were scanned on a 3T GE MR750 scanner (General Electric Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA) using an eight-channel head coil. Written informed consent was obtained from each volunteer. The maximum gradient strength is 50 mT/m. Although the maximum slew rate of the system is 200 mT/m/ms, a maximum slew rate of 100 mT/m/ms was used to avoid peripheral nerve stimulation.</p>
<p id="P36">Trapezoid-cosine OGSE waveforms were designed to replace the traditional PGSE waveform in a single-refocused spin-echo diffusion-weighted sequence. Trapezoid-cosine OGSE waveforms corresponding to three encoding frequencies (<em>ω</em> = 18 Hz, 44 Hz, and 63 Hz) were implemented. The <em>b</em>-value was kept constant at 200 s/mm<sup>2</sup>. To keep the <em>b</em>-value and TE constant for all OGSE experiments, an increasing number of periods of the waveform were used with increasing encoding frequency as follows: 2, 4, and 6 periods for encoding frequencies of 18Hz, 44 Hz, and 63 Hz, respectively. Notice that the total diffusion encoding duration <em>δ</em> on either side of the refocusing pulse is constant (48 ms) across encoding frequencies. For comparison, a PGSE DWI experiment with a diffusion time of 20 ms and a matched <em>b</em>-value was also carried out. Tetrahedral diffusion encoding was used to simultaneously exploit the maximum strength of the G<sub>x</sub>, G<sub>y</sub>, and G<sub>z</sub> gradients and reduce the TE compared to standard diffusion gradient encoding where only the principal gradient direction is applied (<a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a>).</p>
<p id="P37">For all experiments, a 3-interleaf EPI trajectory was used. Parallel imaging with acceleration factor of three (<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a>) was used to fill in the <em>k</em>-space gap of each interleaf. Other parameters are: TE/TR = 116/3000 ms, FOV= 240mm, acquisition matrix = 128 × 128, 2 mm<sup>2</sup> resolution in-plane, 5 mm slice thickness with no slice gap, 8 sagittal slices, partial Fourier encoding with 24 over scan lines (i.e. half scan factor = 68.75%), and 15 repetitions of the diffusion scheme. For anatomical reference, a 15-direction <em>b</em> = 1000 s/mm<sup>2</sup> DTI data set with one <em>b = 0</em> image was acquired (TE/TR = 45/3000 ms) using a matched resolution, slice prescription, and readout trajectory.</p>
<p id="P38">FOV/2 EPI ghosting parameters were selected based on the best of the 15 fully-sampled <em>b = 0</em> scans (as defined by the lowest grappa fit-error to the data (<a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a>)) through the use of an entropy-based method (<a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">24</a>,<a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">25</a>). After the ghost correction, each interleaf was reconstructed separately using GRAPPA (<a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a>,<a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a>). Non-acquired phase encoding lines (partial Fourier encoding) were reconstructed by projection onto convex sets (<a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">28</a>,<a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29">29</a>). For all experiments, rigid-body registration of diffusion-weighted images to the <em>b = 0</em> image was performed.</p></section><section id="S14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Estimation of ADC and Diffusion Spectrum</h3>

<p id="P44"><a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a> shows the estimated mean ADCs of one subject in the corpus callosum area, depicted by the red rectangle in the reference image. For anatomical reference, the fractional anisotropy (FA) map and color-coded FA map in this area are also shown. As the encoding frequency of the OGSE experiments increases, regions in the genu and splenium of the corpus callosum (red arrows) exhibit increased ADC as expected for restricted diffusion (<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>,<a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a>). To get a clearer observation of the trend, the mean and standard deviation within ROIs of the genu and splenium of the corpus callosum are plotted in <a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>. A similar frequency dependence of the ADC was also observed in the three other subjects. The frequency dependence of the ADC of the OGSE experiment was also compared to the ADC of the PGSE experiment (denoted as 0 Hz frequency). Although the peak of the frequency-encoding spectrum of the PGSE encoding is at 0 Hz, its wide bandwidth (FWHM of 50 Hz) largely blurs the frequency selectivity of the measured ADC. The non-localized frequency selectivity of the PGSE experiment should partially explain the fact that the ADC of the PGSE is higher than that of the OGSE at the frequency of 18 Hz. It should be also emphasized that additional experiments performed on a water phantom (i.e. no diffusion restriction) found no dependencies on the encoding frequency chosen in the ADC maps (i.e. the ADC remained constant) (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Figure 7a</a>).</p>

<p id="P45">Several human studies have attempted to investigate the dependence of measured ADC with diffusion times (<a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a>,<a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">30</a>,<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a>). Interestingly, regarding the ADC being time-dependent, the findings were positive in the study by Horsfield <em>et al</em> (<a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">30</a>) and negative in those by Le Bihan <em>et al</em> (<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a>) and Clark <em>et al</em> (<a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a>). The positive finding by Horsfield <em>et al</em>, however, is quite controversial due to the concomitant changes of both diffusion time and <em>b</em>-value in the experiments. The latter two studies used either stimulated-echo diffusion-encoding (<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a>), which is PGSE-like, or oscillating trapezoid-sine encoding (<a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a>), which is sine-like. From Stepisnik’s diffusion spectrum point of view, it is important to note that these two diffusion-encoding schemes are primarily sensitive to the 0 Hz frequency regardless of the waveform timing (<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a>), and therefore obscure the frequency/time-dependence of the measured ADC. Furthermore, the effective diffusion times employed in these studies were relatively long with minimum effective diffusion times of 8 ms (<a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a>). These are diffusion times where the ADC is already saturated by diffusion restriction in neuronal tissue.</p>
<p id="P46">The present study provides two significant contributions. Firstly, the presented formulation enables a thorough optimization of the OGSE diffusion-encoding waveform, both with regards to the shape and the timing of the waveform. The maximum gradient slew rate and gradient amplitude were fully exploited through the use of a trapezoid-cosine waveform, which yielded a higher <em>b</em>-value while maintaining a similar echo time and encoding spectrum as its cosine counterpart (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>). Taking into consideration the DNR and T<sub>2</sub>-decay further optimized the waveforms as shown in <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>.</p>
<p id="P47">An interesting finding of the present study was the dependence of the diffusion-encoding spectrum on the polarity of the OGSE waveforms on the two sides of the refocusing pulse and the timing of the waveform (<em>δ, Δ</em>). For a given waveform duration <em>δ</em> and frequency <em>ω<sub>0</sub></em>, the best frequency selectivity (in terms of the FWHM of main lobes and the ripples) is achieved with the value of <em>Δ</em> and the waveform polarity that maximize 
<span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="M26" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><msup><mrow><mi>H</mi></mrow><mn>2</mn></msup><mrow><mo>(</mo><mrow><mfrac><mrow><msub><mrow><mi>ω</mi></mrow><mn>0</mn></msub><mi mathvariant="normal">Δ</mi></mrow><mn>2</mn></mfrac></mrow><mo>)</mo></mrow></mrow></math></span>. Additionally, <a href="#FD5" class="usa-link">Eq. [5]</a> shows that given a fixed diffusion-encoding duration <em>δ</em> and an optimal <em>Δ</em>, OGSE waveforms with different frequencies will have encoding spectrums with the same FWHM and ripple. The number of waveform periods fitted in <em>δ</em> thus becomes irrelevant to the frequency localization. This finding is important since it implies that the FWHM and ripple of OGSE encoding spectrum is limited only by <em>δ</em> and hence the echo time (TE).</p>
<p id="P70">Secondly, with these carefully designed OGSE waveforms, we were able to observe in vivo and on a human MR system the dependence of the measured ADC on the diffusion-encoding gradient waveform. Currently, these observed changes are limited to white-matter-dominant regions such as the corpus callosum where large coherent fiber bundles exist, as can be seen in the color-coded FA reference maps (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a>, <a href="#F7" class="usa-link">7</a>). Nevertheless, this result serves as the first proof of the feasibility of OGSE on a human MR system. Since the employed cosine-trapezoid waveform is time-matched and spectrum-matched (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>) with the cosine waveform, heuristically, the effective diffusion time of the trapezoid-cosine waveform can be approximated by that of its cosine waveform counterpart. In this case, the effective diffusion times used in the current experiment are 4 ms, 6 ms, and 12 ms (<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>), corresponding to the 63 Hz, 44 Hz, and 18 Hz encoding spectrums, respectively. Considering a diffusion coefficient of ~ 3 × 10<sup>−3</sup> mm<sup>2</sup>/s (that of unrestricted water at 37°C (<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>)), the 1D root-mean-squared displacements are 4.9 μm, 6 μm, and 8.5μm in 4ms, 6ms, and 12 ms, respectively. Therefore, the observed frequency/time dependence of ADC suggests that the typical restriction diameter in the human genu and splenium of the corpus callosum is of several micrometers. In a previous study using PGSE acquisition and the single-diameter fiber model, a mean fiber diameter of 6 – 10 μm was reported in human genu and splenium of the corpus callosum (<a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">32</a>), which agrees well with our data.</p>
<p id="P71">Multiple factors resulting from the in vivo experimental setting can bias the ADC frequency dependence analysis. First and foremost, the <em>b</em>-value (200 s/mm<sup>2</sup>) employed in the current study is relatively low. As suggested by Le Bihan (<a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">33</a>), the measured diffusion signal at this low <em>b</em>-value might be contaminated with intra-voxel incoherent motion (IVIM) signal. Two models were previously proposed to explain the source of IVIM signal (<a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34">34</a>):</p>
<ul id="L1" class="list" style="list-style-type:disc">
<li><p id="P48">Model 1: Blood flow goes through multiple capillary segments with random orientation during the diffusion time.</p></li>
<li><p id="P49">Model 2: There exist multiple capillary segments with random orientation within a voxel, however, blood flow does not change from one capillary segment to another segment during the diffusion time.</p></li>
</ul>
<p id="P50">Since our proposed trapezoid-cosine waveform is velocity-compensated (see <a href="#APP1" class="usa-link">Appendix</a>), the contribution of IVIM signal from model 2, which is flow-like, is quite minimal. Further investigation is still needed to assess the contribution of the IVIM signal from model 1.</p>
<p id="P51">For fast data acquisition, the readout trajectory of choice in the current study is EPI. The additional diffusion weighting that the readout waveform introduces is negligible. With the employed acquisition parameters (24 over scan lines, 24 cm × 24 cm FOV, readout bandwidth +/−125 kHz, echo spacing 692 μs), the additional <em>b</em>-value at the center of <em>k</em>-space is 0.02 s/mm<sup>2</sup>.</p>
<p id="P52">Important artifacts induced by EPI acquisitions are ghosting due to gradient delays and misregistration due to motion between different volumes within an experiment (that is, between the acquisition of one encoding frequency). These artifacts can reduce the accuracy of ADC frequency dependence analysis, and subsequently bias our results if unaccounted for. Ghosting artifacts can arise from an inadequate ghost correction and GRAPPA parameter estimation during the calibration phase. However our data was unlikely to have been affected by ghost-induced artifacts as the best ghost parameters of 15 <em>b = 0</em> scans were used for the ghost correction procedure. Motion that can occur across image volumes within an experiment was mitigated through the use of rigid registration of diffusion-weighted images to the <em>b = 0</em> image. To minimize the effects of noise and additional partial voluming from experiment-to-experiment registration, the ADC frequency dependence analysis is currently done only on ROIs in white matter regions with known predominant uni-directional fibers, such as the genu and splenium of the corpus callosum. We anticipate that the incorporation of navigator-free prospective motion correction techniques, such as in (<a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35">35</a>), would benefit the current study.</p>
<p id="P53">Additional artifacts can arise from B<sub>0</sub> inhomogeneity and eddy currents induced by the OGSE waveform, the effects of which become worse with increasing echo spacing in the EPI readout train. In this study, susceptibility artifacts were reduced significantly by employing a 3-interleaf trajectory. Regarding eddy currents, their effects are reduced in OGSE compared with PGSE through the presence of closely placed, sign-alternate diffusion-encoding gradient lobes. Also note that the OGSE waveform reduces the sensitivity of the estimated ADC to background and imaging gradient cross terms (<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>,<a href="#R36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R36">36</a>).</p></section><section id="S22"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CONCLUSION</h2>
<p id="P54">Through analytic solutions and experimental verifications, the trapezoid-cosine was found to be the OGSE waveform that achieves an optimal DNR and frequency localization. Using this waveform, the frequency-dependence of the measured ADCs was observed in white-matter-dominant regions, such as the corpus callosum. The results obtained demonstrate the potential of utilizing an OGSE acquisition for investigating restricted diffusion on a human MR system. OGSE may provide a new diffusion contrast mechanism that can probe tissue at the sub-cellular level, which could in turn become an early marker for disease or treatment response. Despite promising preliminary results, with more powerful gradient systems on the horizon (e.g. ‘Connectome gradients’), the acquisition OGSE in humans is likely to demonstrate an even further benefit.</p></section><section id="S23" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>

<p id="P63">The velocity-sensitivity of a gradient waveform is proportional to its first moment (<a href="#R37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R37">37</a>). Therefore, to show that the proposed OGSE trapezoid-cosine waveform is velocity-compensated, we need to show that its first moment is 0. The following property of the first moment of a gradient waveform is key to our proof (<a href="#R37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R37">37</a>):
</p>
<blockquote class="text-italic" id="Q1"><p id="P64">If a gradient waveform <em>g(t)</em> is symmetric around <em>t</em> = <em>τ</em>, i.e. <em>g(t)</em> = <em>g(2τ−t))</em>, its first moment at the end of the waveform is</p></blockquote>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="FD18"><tr>
<td class="formula"><math id="M37" display="block" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><msub><mrow><mi>m</mi></mrow><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>=</mo><msub><mrow><mi>m</mi></mrow><mn>0</mn></msub><mi>τ</mi><mo>,</mo></mrow></math></td>
<td class="label">[A8]</td>
</tr></table>
<blockquote class="text-italic" id="Q2"><p id="P65">where <em>m</em><sub>0</sub> is the zeroth moment at the end of the waveform. Therefore, from <a href="#FD18" class="usa-link">Eq. [A8]</a>, if the gradient waveform is symmetric and its zeroth moment at the end of the waveform is 0, its first moment at the end of the waveform is also 0.</p></blockquote>
<p id="P66">Consider the OGSE trapezoid-cosine waveform as given in <a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Figure A1</a>. It can be seen that the zeroth moments of the left and right waveforms equal 0 at their respective ends. Furthermore, when considered separately, the left and the right trapezoid-cosine waveforms are symmetric around
