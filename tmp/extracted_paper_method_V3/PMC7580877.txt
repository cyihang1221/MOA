</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ABS1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="P4">Calorie restriction (CR) enhances health span (the length of time that an organism remains healthy) and increases longevity across species. In mice, these beneficial effects are partly mediated by the lowering of core body temperature that occurs during CR. Conversely, the favorable effects of CR on health span are mitigated by elevating ambient temperature to thermoneutrality (30°C), a condition in which hypothermia is blunted. In this study, we compared the global metabolic response to CR of mice housed at 22°C (the standard housing temperature) or at 30°C and found that thermoneutrality reverted 39 and 78% of total systemic or hypothalamic metabolic variations caused by CR, respectively. Systemic changes included pathways that control fuel use and energy expenditure during CR. Cognitive computing-assisted analysis of these metabolomics results helped to prioritize potential active metabolites that modulated the hypothermic response to CR. Finally, we demonstrated with pharmacological approaches that nitric oxide (NO) produced through the citrulline-NO pathway promotes CR-triggered hypothermia, and that leucine enkephalin directly controls core body temperature when exogenously injected into the hypothalamus. Because thermoneutrality counteracts CR-enhanced health span, the multiple metabolites and pathways altered by thermoneutrality may represent targets for mimicking CR-associated effects.</p></section><section id="S1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p id="P5">Calorie restriction (CR) is a controlled reduction of calorie intake that increases health span and life span across species(<a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">1</a>). CR also delays the onset and reduces the incidence of several disorders including cancer, cardiovascular and degenerative diseases(<a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">2</a>–<a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a>). Determining the mechanisms by which the beneficial effects of CR occur is fundamental in developing strategies to promote health span including the use of endogenous metabolites to mimic the physiological metabolic responses to CR (CR mimetics), as has been proposed with several drugs(<a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a>).</p>
<p id="P6">An important feature of CR is its ability to reduce core body temperature (T<sub>b</sub>). Endotherms maintain a nearly constant T<sub>b</sub> by balancing heat production and dissipation. However, during CR they reduce T<sub>b</sub>, a phenomenon observed in humans, nonhuman primates, and rodents(<a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a>–<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a>). This adaptive response is believed to have evolved to reduce energy expenditure when nutrients are scarce, effectively “gaining time” to find food(<a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a>, <a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">12</a>). Time is exactly what the organism gains when experimentally subjected to CR, because life span is prolonged. Research on rodents has demonstrated that T<sub>b</sub> reduction is not merely a consequence of CR but also contributes to its beneficial impact. Transgenic mice with lowered T<sub>b</sub> have increased median life span independent of CR(<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">13</a>) and epidemiology provides support for the notion that low T<sub>b</sub> is also associated with longevity in humans. The National Institutes of Aging (NIA)-funded Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging found that participants with lower median body temperatures at study entry live substantially longer than those with higher body temperatures(<a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a>). An independent study analyzing data from over 18,000 individuals showed that body temperature is lower in subjects in the oldest cohorts(<a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a>). The analysis of over 600,000 human temperature measurements spanning over 150 years (1860–2017) demonstrated that average temperature has decreased 0.03°C per birth decade, alongside with an increase in life span during the same time period(<a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">15</a>). Finally, work in <em>D. melanogaster</em> and in <em>C. elegans</em> has indicated that the effects of temperature on longevity are not simply thermodynamics but are also mediated by specific biochemical and genetic pathways(<a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">16</a>–<a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">19</a>).</p>
<p id="P7">Temperature homeostasis is maintained at the expense of energy. That energetic cost is inversely correlated with ambient temperature (T<sub>a</sub>). Energetic cost is minimal for mice and humans at 30°C, a value at which both species are thermoneutral. Thermoneutrality minimizes heat loss, thereby preventing the hypothermic response observed during CR at lower T<sub>a</sub>.</p>
<p id="P8">Endotherms, including humans and mice, tend to prefer to spend most of their time in a thermoneutral comfort zone and do so primarily by behavioral thermoregulation. This includes choosing to occupy or generate ambient temperature that is thermoneutral or it is near thermoneutrality. Humans achieve this most often with clothing or by regulating ambient temperature. Mice most typically burrow and/or cuddle. Yet, most of our knowledge on the effects of CR comes from experiments carried out in rodents that are single caged and forcibly housed at 22°C and may provide limited information on the mechanisms that regulate health span during CR. Moreover, housing mice at ~30 °C suffices to increase inflammation, atherosclerosis, and fatty liver disease during normal feeding (<a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a>–<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a>) and antagonizes the beneficial effects of CR on lymphoma and longevity (<a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a>).</p>
<p id="P9">In this study, we present experiments designed to determine the effects of thermoneutrality on the metabolome during CR. Mice were housed at standard room temperature or at thermoneutrality and were either fed ad libitum or subjected to CR. Comprehensive untargeted metabolomics was performed on the hypothalamus and on plasma. The hypothalamus was chosen because this brain region regulates feeding and T<sub>b</sub>. We anticipated that some of the changes observed may provide information on pathways that integrate nutrient and temperature homeostasis. Plasma was chosen because it is the effector of the metabolic responses to CR that occur in peripheral tissues, as well as a good indicator of microbiome variability, and thus represents a good vehicle to evaluate system-wide changes. Our hope was that the metabolites identified would include some that could highlight the pathways that may contribute to the antagonizing effects of thermoneutrality on CR. Of the omics disciplines, metabolomics most closely reflects the phenotypic changes, and in addition to its pivotal role in the discovery of altered biochemical pathways, metabolomics has the unique ability of characterizing metabolites that can alter an organism’s phenotype(<a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">24</a>, <a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">25</a>). We also employed artificial intelligence for the post-analysis of metabolomics results, which has been effective for the prediction of metabolites with biological activity(<a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a>, <a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a>).</p>
<p id="P10">We found that thermoneutrality greatly affected the metabolic changes associated with CR, counteracting nearly 40% of changes in the plasma and up to 78% of changes in the hypothalamus. Many of these metabolites affected by thermoneutrality play a pivotal role in energy exchange with the environment, as well as in fuel consumption and energy expenditure during calorie restriction. Moreover, the citrulline-nitric oxide cycle and leucine enkephalin affected T<sub>b</sub>.</p></section><section id="S2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS</h2>
<section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Overview of untargeted metabolomics results</h3>
<p id="P11">Untargeted metabolomics analyses were performed on plasma and hypothalamic samples of mice fed ad libitum (AL) and calorie restricted (CR) to 50% of their AL intake, housed at both 22˚C and 30°C (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1a</a>). CR was performed by first feeding animals with 100% of the AL diet for 2 days to habituate them to daily rations of food, which was followed by 75% of AL calorie intake for 4 days, and then 50% of AL amounts for the final 8 days before harvesting. During the CR regimen used here, similar body weight reductions were observed in animals housed at 22˚C and at 30°C (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S1a</a> and <a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S11</a>), and the ratios between fat or lean body mass and body weight at both temperatures were similar (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S1b</a>). At the time of analysis, the body composition was similar between CR animals housed at 22˚C and 30°C (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S1b</a>). Tissues were harvested at three different time points. The onset time point (O, ZT15–18, ZT: Zeitgeber Time) begins a few hours after feeding (ZT12) and is defined by the initiation of T<sub>b</sub> reduction for animals housed at 22°C. The maintenance time point (M, ZT23.5–2.5) is when maximum hypothermia is achieved for mice at 22°C. The termination time point (T, ZT8–11) is when T<sub>b</sub> returns to levels above 34.0 °C without feeding or energy input (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1a</a>, <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S1c</a> and <a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S7</a>). The same collection time windows were used for the animals housed at 30 °C, which showed only a modest T<sub>b</sub> change and did not enter into the hypothermia range (T<sub>b</sub>&lt;34.0 °02DAC) (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1a</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S1c</a>). Metabolic changes during the three collection points of CR were normalized to the levels of the same metabolites in mice fed ad libitum for each temperature.</p>

Total detected features were subjected to statistical filtering to reveal metabolic features that could represent statistically altered metabolites during CR (q&lt;0.05, one-way ANOVA followed by a local false discovery rate correction). Mice housed at room temperature (22°C) had more statistically significant features than mice housed at thermoneutrality (30°C) (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1b</a>). 20% of the total features in the hypothalamus of mice housed at 22°C were statistically significant (q&lt;0.05), compared to only 8.5% of total features at 30°C. Thus, many of the changes in metabolic features due to CR at room temperature were not observed in mice housed at thermoneutrality (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1b</a>).</p>
<p id="P13">After computational annotation of altered features(<a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">28</a>, <a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29">29</a>) and fragmentation, 90 metabolites were identified from plasma samples and 43 originating from the hypothalamus (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1c</a>). Among those characterized metabolites, most corresponded to lipids (~40% of total) followed by amino acids, amino acid derivatives and metabolites engaged in energy metabolism (acylcarnitines and redox cofactors) (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1c</a>). Notably, even though the total number of metabolites found in plasma was double than that found in the hypothalamus, the relative distribution of metabolite classes remained similar within each tissue (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1d</a>).</p>
<p id="P14">The variation of the metabolites over the three phases of the CR time course (onset, maintenance and termination) was calculated as the logarithmic fold change of the average peak areas of all metabolites normalized to AL conditions within each housing temperature (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data Files S1</a>–<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">S2</a>). Overall, most of metabolites were decreased during CR at 22°C and this trend was partially or fully reversed for several metabolites when mice were housed at thermoneutrality, especially in the hypothalamus, as was anticipated by the analysis of altered features (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1e</a>). Fewer metabolites had increased intensities relative to AL during CR, with the exception of several fatty acids and acylcarnitines in the plasma and two peptides in the hypothalamus (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1e</a>).</p>
<p id="P15">In summary, calorie restriction promoted remodeling of the metabolome. These changes were affected by thermoneutrality, providing a framework for the discovery and validation of metabolites that may mediate the hypothermic response to CR or the effects of CR on health span and life span.</p></section><section id="S4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">METABOLIC CHANGES TO CALORIE RESTRICTION IN ANIMALS HOUSED AT 22°C OR AT 30°C</h3>
<p id="P16">Thermoneutral housing is detrimental in several models of disease(<a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a>–<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a>) and antagonizes some of the beneficial effects ascribed to CR(<a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a>). To make progress towards identifying the changes that may mediate these effects, we compared and subtracted the metabolite changes caused by CR in mice housed at 30°C from those of mice housed at 22°C to find the metabolites whose changes promoted by CR are reverted by thermoneutrality. Metabolites were computationally sorted in three different groups based on the trend of the fold-change over time (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S2a</a>). Because thermoneutrality prevented the hypothermic response, those metabolites that were altered at 30°C but not at 22°C or vice versa were arbitrarily classified as temperature-dependent. In contrast, metabolites that were changed similarly at 30°C and at 22°C were classified as diet-dependent. Finally, those metabolites altered at 30°C and 22°C, but with an opposite trend, were classified as diet-and-temperature dependent (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2a</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S2a</a>).</p>

S2b</a>). When metabolites measured in plasma and the hypothalamus were examined separately, the regulation of plasma metabolites was biased towards diet dependency, whereas the regulation of hypothalamic metabolites were biased towards temperature dependency (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2b</a>). 55 out of 90 plasma metabolites were diet-dependent, indicating the influence of food reduction in the systemic response to CR. Still, one third of total plasma metabolites were temperature-dependent, pointing to the systemic processes that are highly affected by thermoneutrality (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2b</a>). In contrast, most of metabolites found in the hypothalamus (24 out of 37) were classified as temperature-dependent, with only 8 being diet-dependent (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2b</a>).</p>
<p id="P18">With the altered metabolites sorted into groups based on the dominant factor influencing their regulation, we further categorized the metabolites by their chemical classes (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2c</a>–<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">d</a>). In mice housed at room temperature, CR primarily affected energy metabolism because 83% of the observed changes were in energy metabolites and 77% were lipids (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2c</a>). By contrast, thermoneutrality affected predominantly amino acid-related groups (amino acids, amino acid derivatives and peptides) (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2c</a>). In the hypothalamus, temperature played a greater role than diet in regulating all metabolite classes and the regulatory contribution of diet was notable only for lipids (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2d</a>).</p>
<p id="P19">To extract information about the global regulation of metabolites sorted in different groups, hierarchical clustering analyses were carried out, based on similarity of the metabolic regulatory trends over the course of the CR. In plasma diet-dependent metabolites, two main clusters were formed. One main cluster contained all lipids and acylcarnitines, whereas the other contained amino acids and amino acid derivatives, suggesting a global regulation of the lipid-energy axis and of certain amino acid metabolism pathways during CR (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2e</a>). Only eight hypothalamic metabolites were diet-dependent. In the hierarchical clustering, sphinganine and sphingosine, the two main sphingoid bases(<a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">30</a>), grouped together with a strong linear correlation (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2f</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S2c</a>). This similar response could indicate that the entirety of sphingolipid metabolism is changed during calorie restriction, which was predicted in our pathway enrichment analysis (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S3</a>).</p>
<p id="P20">Temperature-dependent plasma metabolites formed two main clusters (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2g</a>). One branch was mainly composed of lipids, namely free fatty acids (FFAs), which changed in abundance during CR at 22°C only (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2g</a>). The other cluster mainly contained various amino acids and amino acid derivatives. Most amino acids grouped together. The three branched-chain amino acids (BCAA) leucine, isoleucine and valine, clustered next to each other (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2g</a>). This similar response of the BCAAs to CR, based on clustering location and a strong linear correlation (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S2c</a>), suggests a regulated systemic metabolism of BCAA upon CR that depends on thermoneutrality (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S2d</a>).</p>
<p id="P21">In the clustering of temperature-dependent hypothalamus metabolites, the four amino acids measured (arginine, tryptophan, citrulline and proline) grouped in close proximity (<a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2h</a>). Because purine metabolism was predicted to be altered in the hypothalamus by our pathway analysis (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S3</a>), the presence of three purine metabolites as temperature-dependent suggests that this pathway activation during CR is influenced by T<sub>a</sub>.</p>
<p id="P22">Finally, five metabolites in each tissue were classified as diet-and-temperature-dependent. This group of metabolites was characterized by alteration at both ambient temperatures (T<sub>a</sub>) but in opposite directions (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S2e</a>). Overall, the temperature-dependent metabolites and pathways identified in this study using untargeted metabolomics technologies are molecules that may directly mediate hypothermia during CR and/or regulate the beneficial physiological consequences of CR.</p></section><section id="S5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">SYSTEMIC RESPONSE TO CALORIE RESTRICTION IN ANIMALS HOUSED AT 22°C OR AT 30°C</h3>
<p id="P23">Changes observed in plasma can be considered an index of the systemic responses to CR. On the other hand, because the hypothalamus regulates nutrient and temperature homeostasis, it is not only subject to the effects of CR but also coordinates the physiological responses to it. Thus, to gain insight into the tissue specificity and the integrative response to CR, we measured the intra- and the inter-tissue correlation of the metabolites altered at 22°C or 30°C.</p>
<p id="P24">All plasma and hypothalamic metabolites were plotted in correlation networks that connected all metabolites with a Pearson correlation coefficient of |ρ|&gt;0.6. Correlations between two metabolites were found within the same tissue (intra-plasma or intra-hypothalamus) and across the tissues investigated (inter-tissue correlation between plasma and hypothalamus) (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3a</a>). Mice housed at room temperature showed a total 1022 correlations of which 759 (~74%) were intra-plasma,175 (~17%) were intra-hypothalamus and 88 (~9%) were between plasma and hypothalamus. Thermoneutrality reduced the number of correlations by nearly half to a total of 465, of which 405 (~87%) were intra-plasma, 175 (~11.7%) were inter-hypothalamus and only 6 (~1.3%) were between plasma and hypothalamus (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3a</a>).</p>

In plasma, the most common intra-tissue correlations at both T<sub>a</sub> were lipid-lipid connections and their number at thermoneutrality was ~50% of that at 22°C (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3b</a>). A similar scenario was found for the number of lipid-energy metabolites correlations that at 30°C was only ~25% of that seen at room temperature (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3b</a>). Thermoneutrality also reduced the amino acid-amino acid and amino acid-amino acid derivative correlations found in the plasma of mice housed at 22°C by ~75% and ~78%, respectively (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3b</a>). With only a few exceptions (lipid-peptide), all correlations between different metabolite classes in plasma were diminished when mice were housed at thermoneutrality (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3b</a>). The effects of thermoneutrality on the number of intra-tissue correlations in the hypothalamus were particularly strong for lipid-amino acid and lipid-amino acid derivatives, which were reduced by ~97% and ~100% compared to those found at 22°C, respectively (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3c</a>). Similarly, thermoneutrality reduced most of the correlations observed between a metabolite in plasma and a metabolite in the hypothalamus, generally lipids and amino acid-related metabolites (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3d</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S3</a>). The interaction between lipid and amino acid metabolism coordinates the response to food reduction during CR at room temperature(<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a>). Our data suggest that such response depends on T<sub>a</sub> and is nearly abolished at thermoneutrality.</p>
<p id="P26">These data indicate that thermoneutrality impinged on both the intra- and the inter-tissue correlations by reducing their number. The effects were particularly strong on the inter-tissue correlation, suggesting that a portion of the changes occurring in the hypothalamus in response to CR may contribute to the systemic physiological response to CR.</p></section><section id="S6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">ENERGY EXPENDITURE AND FUEL USAGE DURING CALORIE RESTRICTION</h3>
<p id="P27">Thermoneutrality prevents heat loss and affects energy homeostasis by eliminating the cost associated with the maintenance of constant T<sub>b</sub>. Thus, endotherms housed at thermoneutrality have reduced energy expenditure. This was evident when comparing animals fed AL and housed at 22°C or 30°C (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4a</a> and <a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S10</a>). Eventually, differences in energy expenditure were reduced in mice on CR because animals housed at 22°C developed hypothermic responses and gradually lowered their energy expenditure (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4a</a> and <a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S10</a>). By day 8, when samples were collected for the metabolomics analyses, these differences were marginal (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4a</a> and <a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S10</a>). Thermoneutrality also affected the respiratory exchange ratio (RER) (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4b</a>), an indirect index of the relative utilization of lipids or carbohydrates as source of energy. RER was higher at thermoneutrality indicating that mice housed at 30°C used a higher ratio of carbohydrates than those housed at 22°C which utilized mostly lipids. At day 8 of CR, this difference was also statistically significant (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4b</a> and <a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S9</a>).</p>

Lipolysis is one of the first responses to CR(<a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">9</a>). It increases the availability of FFAs in plasma to provide substrates for β-oxidation in peripheral organs and tissues. CR elevated total FFAs in the plasma but did so more (2-fold difference) in mice housed at room temperature than at thermoneutrality (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4c</a>). The level of FFAs was the highest when hypothermia was maximal in animals at 22°C and to the corresponding timepoint in animals at 30°C. Once generated, FFAs are converted into acylcarnitines to be shuttled into the mitochondria. The profile of total plasma acylcarnitines, as well as that four out of the total five individual acylcarnitines measured, was similar to that of FFAs (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4d</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S4a</a>). Free carnitine, a necessary substrate for the synthesis of acylcarnitines from FFAs, was decreased during CR more so in mice housed at room temperature than in mice housed at thermoneutrality (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4e</a>). Overall, global untargeted metabolomics results unveiled a high degree of dependence on T<sub>b</sub> of the main products of lipolysis during CR, suggesting that thermoneutrality is a relevant factor for lipid and energy metabolism of animals during adaptation to CR.</p>
<p id="P29">Gluconeogenesis from free amino acids has been described as a mechanism to maintain glucose levels in models of CR(<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a>, <a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">32</a>). We evaluated the abundance of amino acids in response CR. We found that up to 15 glucogenic amino acids were altered in the plasma (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S5</a>). Pathway analysis also predicted several biochemical pathways involving amino acid metabolism dysregulated during CR (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S3</a>). The total amount of glucogenic amino acids was also calculated (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4f</a> and <a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S5</a>). CR decreased the level of glucogenic amino acids in plasma, a trend that might explain the activation of gluconeogenesis(<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a>, <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">33</a>). However, these changes were greater in animals housed at 22°C than in mice housed at thermoneutrality. At thermoneutrality, changes in total glucogenic amino acids were only statistically significant at the termination phase (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4f</a>).</p>
<p id="P30">Calorie restriction increases protein turnover (reviewed in (<a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34">34</a>)). Here, we found that the plasma level of creatinine, an index of muscle mass(<a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35">35</a>), decreased during CR (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4g</a>). Consistently, plasma 3-methylhistidine, an index of muscle protein turnover(<a href="#R36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R36">36</a>), was slightly elevated by CR towards the end of the hypothermic phase (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4h</a>). Changes in creatinine and 3-methylhistidine were larger in animals housed at 22°C (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4g</a>, <a href="#F4" class="usa-link">h</a>).</p>
<p id="P31">The plasma level of glucose was also altered during CR and was at both T<sub>a</sub>s (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4i</a>). It decreased at the onset of the hypothermic response (3 hours after feeding) in a similar way for animals housed at 22°C and at 30°C. This decrease corresponded with the barely altered levels of total glucogenic amino acids, creatinine and 3-methylhistidine at onset (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4f</a>–<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">i</a>). Blood glucose continued to drop during the maintenance of CR for both housing temperatures before increasing towards the AL levels at the termination phase. Recovery of the original AL glucose values was higher in mice housed at room temperature (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4i</a>).</p>
<p id="P32">Overall, these data show that thermoneutrality alters the effects of CR on energy expenditure and fuel utilization. These changes could be a consequence of the different physical activity of mice at both housing temperatures. However, even when locomotor activity oscillated during daily cycles, the total amount of physical activity for mice in both housing temperatures was nearly identical (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S4b</a> and <a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S8</a>).</p></section><section id="S7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">COGNITIVE COMPUTING-GUIDED PRIORITIZATION OF HYPOTHALAMIC ACTIVE METABOLITES</h3>
<p id="P33">Our previous data showed that during CR, thermoneutrality altered T<sub>b</sub>, energy expenditure, fuel usage and certain metabolites. Because thermoneutrality antagonizes the effects of CR in several models of disease(<a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a>), some of these metabolites and their pathways may represent targets for mimicking CR, but their validation will require extensive and expensive survival studies. Instead, other pathways may contribute to the physiological changes caused by thermoneutrality. Among these, there may be molecules that modulate the hypothermic response to CR, a more accessible parameter for validation. We searched for such molecules among the metabolites identified in the hypothalamus. This brain region controls nutrient and temperature homeostasis and was greatly affected by thermoneutrality (&gt;78% of the metabolites changed due to CR were altered by thermoneutrality). We utilized cognitive accelerated literature mining to rank hypothalamic metabolites according to their known and predicted literature based relationships with biological processes, in this case temperature control by the hypothalamus(<a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a>). This artificial intelligence platform uses natural language processing (NLP) to interpret the text of over 25 million MEDLINE abstracts and to rank literature based predicted relationships between the metabolites - candidates to be ranked - and specified biological conditions(<a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a>) (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5a</a>).</p>

These scores prioritized those metabolites that changed upon CR at 22°C (when mice enter in hypothermia) and those that remained invariant at thermoneutrality (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. <p id="P35">LeuEnk and MetEnk levels increased during CR in hypothermic mice but remained unchanged at thermoneutrality (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5d</a>), a behavior that was not mimicked by any other metabolite in the hypothalamus (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1e</a>). Because these peptides are opioid neurotransmitters that contribute to regulate metabolic responses(<a href="#R37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R37">37</a>, <a href="#R38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R38">38</a>), we hypothesized that they could modulate the hypothermic responses during CR. Actually, activation of the δ opioid receptor by the synthetic LeuEnk analog [D-Ala2, D-Leu5]-Enkephalin (DADLE) increases body temperature under normal feeding when injected into the hypothalamus(<a href="#R39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R39">39</a>). Intracerebroventricular administration of LeuEnk modified T<sub>b</sub> in ad libitum fed mice (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5e</a>) and allowed the identification of a related signaling pathway that modulates hypothermia, energy expenditure and body weight in mice (<a href="#R40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R40">40</a>). These results indicate the value of cognitive computing-guided prioritization of endogenous metabolites to streamline discovery of active metabolites controlling temperature and raises the possibility for the validation of other metabolites on that list as temperature or calorie restriction mimetics or even life span enhancers.</p></section><section id="S8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">ROLE OF THE CITRULLINE-NITRIC OXIDE CYCLE IN THE HYPOTHERMIC RESPONSE TO CALORIE RESTRICTION</h3>
<p id="P36">Arginine and its structurally and functionally related metabolite symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA)(<a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">41</a>, <a href="#R42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R42">42</a>) were ranked among the top 3 metabolites that might regulate temperature in the hypothalamus (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5c</a>). Moreover, CR altered several of the metabolites in the urea cycle in both the hypothalamus and plasma, as revealed by pathway analysis (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6a</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S6a</a>). Changes in the urea cycle were focused on the metabolites that belong to the citrulline-nitric oxide cycle: arginine, argininosuccinic acid and citrulline (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6a</a>, green dashed area). The citrulline-nitric oxide cycle is characterized by the synthesis of nitric oxide (NO) and citrulline from arginine by the nitric oxide synthases (NOS), using NADPH as cofactor. Citrulline may be recycled back to arginine, using argininosuccinic acid as an intermediate product(<a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">41</a>) (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6b</a>). In the hypothalamus, metabolites of the citrulline-NO cycle decreased over the 24 hour course of CR in mice housed at 22°C compared to mice fed AL (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6b</a>), indicating a coordinated activation of this pathway in the hypothalamic response to CR for mice at 22°C. However, the three metabolites remained unaltered when mice were housed at thermoneutrality (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6b</a>). Additionally, NADP<sup>+</sup>, a cofactor produced together with NO in the conversion of arginine into citrulline, was up-regulated during CR-induced hypothermia (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6c</a>), suggesting that NO synthesis may be also activated. One function of NO is activating guanylate cyclase to promote the synthesis of cGMP from GMP(<a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43">43</a>, <a href="#R44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R44">44</a>). GMP levels decreased in the hypothalamus of mice housed at 22°C, indicating that cGMP synthesis was activated. In contrast, GMP levels remained unaltered in mice at thermoneutrality (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6d</a>). Similar changes in arginine and citrulline were seen in the plasma, although argininosuccinic acid also decreased at thermoneutrality, suggesting it might have been used to maintain arginine levels (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6b</a>). Aspartic acid, a substrate for the recycling of citrulline (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6a</a>), was lower in mice housed at 22°C than at 30°C (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6e</a>). Finally, other metabolites structurally and functionally related to arginine, such as symmetric dimethylarginine and homoarginine(<a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">41</a>, <a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43">43</a>), were dysregulated in plasma and the hypothalamus (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S6b</a>).</p>

<p id="P38">The citrulline-NO cycle is closely related to the urea cycle and polyamine metabolism (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S6c</a>). We observed that the plasma level of ornithine, but not that of spermidine, was transiently lowered by up to 50% during maintenance and termination phase of CR in animals housed at 22 °C or at thermoneutrality (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S6c</a>).In contrast, the level of spermidine increased transiently during the maintenance and termination phase of CR (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. Furthermore, we demonstrated that NO synthesis by a coordinated activation of the citrulline-NO cycle contributes to the hypothermic response to CR only in animals housed at room temperature but not at thermoneutrality (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6h</a>).</p></section></section><section id="S9"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">DISCUSSION</h2>
<p id="P39">In this work, we performed untargeted metabolomic analysis to compare the effects of CR in animals housed at two different ambient temperatures. One was 22°C, which approximately corresponds to the standard “room temperature” housing conditions at which experiments are typically performed in laboratories across the world. The other was 30°C, a temperature at which most mammals, including mice and humans, are thermoneutral. The rationale for our study is that thermoneutrality exacerbates inflammatory and metabolic disorders(<a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a>–<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a>), prevents the hypothermic response to CR and antagonizes the beneficial effects that CR has on longevity(<a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a>).</p>
<p id="P40">The 50% CR used here can be considered a model of severe CR. In fact, experiments investigating the beneficial effects of CR on life span typically provide 60% of the AL diet or more. However, such regimens are usually applied for the entire duration of the life span experiments. Here, we subjected animals to 75% CR (animals received 25% less of the amount of food consumed AL) for 4 days followed by 50% CR for 8 days. The regimen and time-points were chosen because a parameter we wanted to investigate was the reduction of core body temperature that occurs during CR at room temperature but not at thermoneutrality, and such a hypothermic response is larger at 50% CR, compared to milder CR regimens. However, in moving forward with the investigation, it will be important to determine if the changes observed here on a 50% CR regimen applied for 8 days are similar to those that may be induced by a milder CR regimen and for longer periods of time.</p>
<p id="P41">The data collected using the experimental model proposed here indicate that thermoneutrality greatly affected the metabolome of animals subject to CR. Many such changes can be attributed to differences in core body temperature observed in animals housed at 22 °C compared to that in animals at thermoneutrality. However, further work is required to determine the specific factors that may affect the changes observed. Among them are the sex specificity of the metabolomic profile. In fact, data were collected from female animals only because females show changes in core body temperature to CR that are larger than those seen in male mice, as we have described before(<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a>, <a href="#R50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R50">50</a>, <a href="#R51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R51">51</a>). Another factor that might contribute to the differences observed is the contribution of the microbiome which can affect both the response to CR as well as the metabolic rate(<a href="#R52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R52">52</a>, <a href="#R53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R53">53</a>). However, plasma metabolomics should have captured most of the changes introduced by the microbiome in our model, and several models that characterize the plasma metabolome use it as a surrogate measure of microbiome changes (<a href="#R54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R54">54</a>, <a href="#R55" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R55">55</a>). Finally, it will be interesting to determine whether and to which extent the sympathetic nervous system, which regulates physiological thermoregulatory response, may contribute to the differences observed. The autonomic nervous system may play a role in hypothermia through neuronal NOS (nNOS), which is expressed in peripheral nitrergic nerves. Smooth muscle tissues are innervated by nitrergic neurons expressing nNOS, which generates NO, which acts on the surrounding cells by decreasing vascular tone, helping to trigger hypothermia through heat loss(<a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43">43</a>, <a href="#R56" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R56">56</a>).</p>
<p id="P42">We showed in animals subjected to long-term CR how the prevention of the CR-induced hypothermia facilitates body weight loss by limiting the hypothermic response that reduces energy expenditure(<a href="#R40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R40">40</a>). During the short-term CR used here, body weight was still similar in CR animals housed at room temperature and at thermoneutrality (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S1a</a>). Additionally, no differences were found in the lean-to-body weight and fat-to-body weight ratio in groups of animals housed at room temperature or at thermoneutrality (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S1b</a>). These data, together with the fact that samples were collected 20 hours after last meal, indicate that the effects of food on the metabolome were minimized and that the experimental setting offered a reasonable platform to investigate the metabolites and pathways associated with temperature. We anticipated that changes in the metabolome may be used to investigate at least two biological functions: Pathways that may contribute to the beneficial effects of CR on longevity and those that may be involved in regulating the hypothermic response to CR. The first set of pathways is more likely to be identified by evaluating the changes found in the plasma, which was used as an index of systemic changes. The latter set of pathways could be identified by evaluating changes occurring in the hypothalamus because this brain region is pivotal in regulating temperature homeostasis. In this study, validation was carried out for metabolites involved in the regulation of the hypothermic response to CR.</p>
<p id="P43">We found that thermoneutrality reversed 39% of those metabolic changes observed in plasma upon CR and 78% of those in the hypothalamus. In both tissues, these changes were primarily due to amino acids and their derivatives and to a lesser extent due to lipids and energy metabolites as demonstrated with the total free fatty acid and acylcarnitine levels (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4c</a>,<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">d</a>). One way to interpret these data is by considering that reduced calorie intake triggers the utilization of stored energy and that such energetic requirement is different in animals housed at distinct ambient temperatures, because thermoneutrality reduces the energetic cost required to maintaining a constant T<sub>b</sub>(<a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a>). Thus, the 75% decrease in lipid-energy metabolite correlations in plasma at thermoneutrality is a likely indication that mice at 30°C do not need as much acylcarnitines as mice at 22°C to produce energy through β-oxidation (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3b</a>). The alteration in amino acids can in part also be interpreted in terms of energy requirements because gluconeogenesis from free amino acids mainly in the liver has been described as a mechanism to maintain glucose levels during CR(<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a>, <a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">32</a>). Plasma glucogenic amino acids may be shuttled to the liver and/or other glucogenic organs to be converted into the different intermediates of gluconeogenesis, potentially explaining that decrease in plasma during CR to regulate energy balance. In addition, prolonged dietary restriction can activate protein catabolism in muscles increasing the level of circulating free amino acids, which can be used as an alternative source of energy for gluconeogenesis(<a href="#R57" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R57">57</a>). Markers of muscle catabolism were higher at 22˚C (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4g</a>,<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">h</a>).</p>
<p id="P44">Amino acid levels during CR may not only mirror metabolic requirements but also exert other contributions to the beneficial effects of CR and are potential candidates for CR mimetics (reviewed in (<a href="#R58" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R58">58</a>)). For instance, reduction of the dietary intake of BCAAs is sufficient to improve glucose tolerance, cardiometabolic health, aging and longevity(<a href="#R59" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R59">59</a>, <a href="#R60" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R60">60</a>). Conversely, BCAA supplementation increases body weight, fat mass, promotes insulin resistance and induces the expression of proinflammatory genes(<a href="#R61" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R61">61</a>). We found that the response of the three BCAAs to CR was similar and blunted by thermoneutrality (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S2c</a>,<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">d</a>), indicating how this approach can help to identify endogenous molecules that modulate pivotal phenotypic responses.</p>
<p id="P45">Artificial intelligence technology was incorporated to streamline the discovery and development of temperature mimetics for increasing health span, as was demonstrated with leucine enkephalin (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5a</a>–<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">c</a>) and validated in a separate study(<a href="#R40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R40">40</a>). In addition, arginine and SDMA played a role in hypothermia during CR. Lysophospholipids, many of them ranked in the top 15 of the cognitive-driven prioritization (<a href="#SD2" class="usa-link">Data File S6</a>), are endogenous ligands of the transient receptor potential M8 (TRPM8) (<a href="#R62" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R62">62</a>). Activation of TRPM8 in the central nervous system triggers certain physiological responses leading to heat generation which maintains thermoregulation when body temperature is low. Conversely, blocking TRPM8 leads to physiological responses resulting in body heat dissipation (<a href="#R63" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R63">63</a>). In summary, the incorporation of cognitive prioritization strategies on top of untargeted metabolomics results streamlines the assignment of biological activity to altered endogenous molecules, as has been demonstrated for other models of disease(<a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a>, <a href="#R64" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R64">64</a>).</p>
<p id="P46">Cognitive-guided prioritization of metabolites, together with pathway analysis, led us to hypothesize that the citrulline-NO pathway metabolites could play an active role in the hypothermic response to CR. We confirmed our hypothesis pharmacologically by showing that inhibition of nitric oxide synthase blocked CR-induced hypothermia. CR affected components of the citrulline-NO cycle in both plasma and the hypothalamus, indicating that NO might regulate temperature during CR by acting both centrally and peripherally. Because the inhibitor used crosses the blood brain barrier and inhibits eNOS and nNOS, both might contribute to hypothermic responses. However, whether L-NAME subcutaneous administration has other side effects unrelated to the observed changes in the citrulline-nitric oxide cycle is yet to be explored in this model. In addition, untargeted analysis revealed that other arginine-related metabolites (dimethylarginine and homoarginine) were altered (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S6b</a>). These endogenous molecules affect NO metabolism due to their structural similarity with arginine(<a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">41</a>). Among them, SDMA is of special interest because it was ranked as the second metabolite that could impact temperature in the hypothalamus (<a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5c</a>); however, this hypothesis is yet to be confirmed in this model.</p>
<p id="P47">Due of its role in life span extension and health span improvement(<a href="#R45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R45">45</a>–<a href="#R49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R49">49</a>) and its metabolic connection with the citrulline-NO cycle (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S6c</a>), we measured spermidine in plasma and the hypothalamus. At 22 °C, we showed that the activation of the citrulline-NO cycle during CR resulted in the synthesis of NO (reflected in the decrease of arginine and citrulline), which helps to trigger hypothermia (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Figs. 6b</a>, <a href="#F6" class="usa-link">6g</a>). However, we did not observe changes in spermidine at 22°C. On the other hand, at thermoneutrality, no variation in arginine and citrulline was observed because NO synthesis was not required (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6b</a>). However, at 30 °C, CR elevated the level of spermidine and reduced that of ornithine and argininosuccinic acid in plasma without modifying arginine and citrulline (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6b</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S6c</a>), suggesting the activation of the polyamine pathway during CR at thermoneutrality. Together, these data indicate that ambient temperature determines whether arginine is used to generate NO (22 °C) or spermidine (30 °C) during CR. However, the biological importance of the increase in endogenous spermidine in our CR model at thermoneutrality remains to be demonstrated.</p>
<p id="P48">The changes in arginine, citrulline, argininosuccinic acid and SDMA in the hypothalamus was significant only in samples from animals housed at 22°C but not in those from animals housed at thermoneutrality. This observation indicates that the absence of a hypothermic response during CR at thermoneutrality is not only a passive phenomenon due to the lack of a temperature gradient between ambient and organismal surface temperatures, but also is an active response that includes the lack of activation of the physiological response that would be required to reduce T<sub>b</sub>. Thus, the hypothermic response during CR it is not dependent only on energy intake, but also on information about the environmental temperature, a parameter that ultimately influences energy expenditure. In the future, it will be interesting to investigate whether the biochemical pathways that regulate physiology of the hypothermic response also affect health span and how this may be affected by ambient temperature.</p></section><section id="S10"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">MATERIALS AND METHODS</h2>
<section id="S11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">MICE AND HUSBANDRY</h3>
<p id="P49">All procedures were approved by TSRI IACUC. Female C57BL/6 mice that were four to five months old were used for the experiments. Animals were singly caged in standard 12:12 h light:dark (lights on 11:00 am; Zeitgeber Time 12 or ZT12) at a controlled ambient temperature (T<sub>a</sub>) of 22.0 ± 0.5 °C or 30.0 ± 0.5 °C. Water was available AL during CR experiments. Food (Lab Diet 5053 Irradiated Pico Lab containing 3.41 kcal/g, 20.0% protein, 52.9% carbohydrates, 10.6% fat, 4.7% crude fiber and 6.1% ash) was provided AL or restricted during the CR experiments as specified.</p></section><section id="S12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">CALORIE RESTRICTION SETUP</h3>
<p id="P50">Four to five month old female mice regularly fed ad libitum were subjected to CR. CR was carried out providing a percentage of the amount of food consumed during AL diet, which was calculated as an average of the calories consumed by mice during a period of 4 days fed AL. Animals were given 100% of the AL diet for 2 days to habituate them to daily rations of food. They were then given 75% of AL for 4 days and 50% for 4 days. Food was provided daily at a one-hour window before lights were turned off (ZT12). All animals ate the entire amount of food provided during CR experiments. To identify the metabolic changes that were due to CR or T<sub>b</sub> variation, two different litters underwent the same CR protocol, but were housed at room temperature (22°C) and thermoneutrality (30°C).</p></section><section id="S13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">COLLECTION OF TISSUE</h3>
<p id="P51">On day 8 of 50% CR, mice were anesthetized through exposure to isoflurane for 3 minutes and perfusion with saline was performed to remove traces of blood from tissues. Before perfusion, blood from the right atrium was collected in microvette capillary blood 300 EDTA (Kent Scientific Corporation, Torrington, CT). To obtain plasma, blood was centrifuged at 2,000 × g at 2 °C for 10 minutes. Supernatants (plasma) were transferred to polypropylene tubes using a Pasteur pipette. Finally, samples were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen for 1 minutes and stored at −80 °C until their analysis by LC-MS.</p>
<p id="P52">The hypothalamus (~3 mm) was collected after perfusion with saline. Dissection was carried out with sterile forceps, scissors and scalpel. The brain was placed in a brain coronal matrix and a razor blade was placed on the optic chiasm to isolate the first section (anterior hypothalamus); from there, the second blade was placed on the 3 mm mark (generating the posterior hypothalamus). The hypothalamic nucleus was then isolated from the brain slice, placed in polypropylene tubes, flash-frozen and stored at −80 °C until their analysis by LC-MS.</p></section><section id="S14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">MONITORING OF PHYSIOLOGICAL PARAMETERS</h3>
<p id="P53">Core body temperature (T<sub>b</sub>) was monitored using a rectal temperature probe for the animals used for the metabolomics analysis or by radiotelemetry for the physiological data presented. Rectal measurements were carried out using RET-3 probes and thermo recorder HH506A (Omega) and were completed within 40 s after first moving the cage to avoid recording thermogenesis due to handling. Radiotelemetry for measuring T<sub>b</sub> and locomotor activity was performed using a transmitter (TA10TA-F10; Data Sciences, Inc.) surgically implanted into the peritoneal cavity, as described (<a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">13</a>). Animals were allowed a recovery time of 14 d postsurgery and 2 d of habituation to the experimental environment before experiments were begun. Data were recorded by placing a cage containing an animal implanted with a radio transmitter on a receiver plate (RPC-1; DataScience, Inc.). Data collection and offline analysis were performed using the DATAQUEST A.R.T. software (DataScience, Inc.). Energy expenditure and respiratory exchange ratio (RER) were determined by using a computer-controlled open-circuit system (Oxymax System) that is part of an integrated Comprehensive Lab Animal Monitoring System (Columbus Instruments), as previously described(<a href="#R65" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R65">65</a>). Body composition during calorie restriction was measured during the first hours of the light period using the Echo3-in-1 NMR analyzer (Echo Medical) as previously described by our group(<a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a>).</p></section><section id="S15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">PHARMACOLOGY</h3>
<p id="P54">Leucine Enkephalin (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK) was dissolved in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) for intracerebroventricular (ICV) administration through a previously placed cannula during AL feeding. A 7mm, 26 gauge guide cannula and 7.2 mm injector, 33 gauge internal cannula were used. The guide cannula was implanted at the following stereotactic coordinates: third ventricle, posterior to bregma: 0.50 mm, ventral 4.75 mm from the skull, final ventral coordinate 4.95 mm. L-NAME (N<sup>G</sup>-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester) hydrochloride (Tocris, 0655) was dissolved in saline and administered subcutaneously at 50 mg/kg in mice undergoing 50% CR.</p></section><section id="S16"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">METABOLITE EXTRACTION</h3>
<p id="P55">For metabolite extraction from plasma, 450 μL of cold acetonitrile/methanol/water (4:4:1, by vol.) was added to 50 μL of previously thawed plasma, to reach a final volume of 500 μL of 2:2:1 (acetonitrile/methanol/aqueous, by vol.). To precipitate plasma proteins, samples underwent 3 cycles of freeze/thaw. Each cycle included a flash freezing step in liquid nitrogen for 1 minute, vortexing for 30 seconds and sonication in an ice-cold bath for 15 minutes.</p>
<p id="P56">For metabolite extraction from the hypothalamus, a section of tissue (10–30 mg) was weighed and 0.6 mL of cold methanol/water (4:1, v/v) was added per each 10 mg of tissue. Homogenization was performed by adding glass beads to the samples in a homogenizer and samples were sonicated in an ice-cold bath for 10 minutes. Samples were transferred to another tube and rinsed with additional 200 μL of methanol/water (4:1, v/v).</p>
<p id="P57">After these different homogenization protocols for both plasma and the hypothalamus, all samples were incubated at −20 °C for 60 minutes. Finally, proteins were pelleted by centrifuging samples at 16000 × g for 15 minutes at 4 °C. Supernatants containing all metabolites were transferred to another tube and dried down in a vacuum concentrator. Finally, dry metabolite extracts were reconstituted with acetonitrile/water (1:1, vol/vol). Metabolite extracts were vortexed for 1 minute, sonicated for 15 minutes and centrifuged at 16,000 × g and 4 °C for 15 minutes to remove nonsoluble debris. Plasma samples were reconstituted in 100 μL. For hypothalamus samples, the volume of acetonitrile/water was adjusted using the initial mass of tissue used for extraction, to employ the same amount of starting material for the untargeted metabolomics analyses. Supernatants were transferred to LC glass vials and stored at −80 °C prior to their analysis by liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry (LC-MS).</p></section><section id="S17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">UNTARGETED METABOLOMICS</h3>
<p id="P58">Samples from mice housed at 22°C and 30°C were harvested and analyzed by mass spectrometry independently. Metabolites extracted from plasma and the hypothalamus were analyzed by reversed-phase (RP) and hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC)(<a href="#R66" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R66">66</a>). Plasma samples analyses were carried out in a Bruker Impact II quadrupole/time-of-flight (q-ToF) mass spectrometer coupled to a Bruker Elute UHPLC (Bruker, Billerica, MA). Data was acquired over a <em>m/z</em> range 50–1,000 Da. The electrospray source conditions were as follows: end plate offset, 500 V; dry gas temperature, 200 °C; drying gas, 6 L/min; nebulizer, 1.6 bar; and capillary voltage, 3500 V. Sodium formate was used for post-run mass calibration. The same mobile phases were used for both RP and HILIC chromatography, consisting of 0.1% formic acid in water (v/v) as phase A and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (v/v) as phase B. The flow through the column in both cases was 150 μL/min. An ACQUITY BEH C18 column (1.0 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm, Water Corporation, Milford, MA) was used for the RP analysis and an ACQUITY BEH Amide column (1.0 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm, Water Corporation, Milford, MA) was used for the HILIC analysis. The gradient for RP chromatography consisted of 99% phase A for 1 minute, 1% phase A over 9 minutes and held at 1% phase A for an additional 3 minutes. The gradient for HILIC consisted of 1% phase A for 1 minute, 35% phase A over 13 minutes, 60% phase A over 3 minutes and held at 60% phase A for 1 additional minute. Data was acquired in positive ion mode. The injection volume was 2 μL. For identification purposes, putative molecules of interest were fragmented at 3 different collision energies (10, 20 and 40 eV).</p>
<p id="P59">Analyses of hypothalamic samples were carried out in a Synapt G2-Si q-ToF mass spectrometer equipped with an online UHPLC (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA). Data was acquired over a <em>m/z</em> range 50–1,000 Da. The electrospray source conditions were as follows: capillary, +3.00 kV; sampling cone, 40 V; source temperature, 100°C; desolvation temperature, 250°C; desolvation gas flow, 600 L/hr; and cone gas flow, 50 L/hr, respectively. Leucine–enkephalin (m/z 556.2771) was used for lock mass correction. For tissue samples, the same solvents and gradients as above were used. However, the flow through the column was 400 μL/min and columns used were: An ACQUITY BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm, Water Corporation, Milford, MA) for the RP analysis and an ACQUITY BEH Amide column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm, Water Corporation, Milford, MA) for the HILIC analysis. Data was acquired in positive ion mode. The injection volume was 2 μL. For identification purposes, putative molecules of interest were fragmented at three different collision energies (10, 20 and 40 eV).</p></section><section id="S18"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">MASS SPECTROMETRY DATA ANALYSIS</h3>
<p id="P60">Raw LC-MS data were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard MS Converter version 3.0.7529(<a href="#R67" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R67">67</a>). The mzXML files were uploaded to XCMS Online for data processing(<a href="#R68" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R68">68</a>). Peaks were first detected, aligned across samples and integrated. Then, features (a set of integrated peaks with a particular <em>m/z</em> and retention time) underwent isotope removal and adduct and in-source ion annotation(<a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">28</a>, <a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29">29</a>). Data were processed using the following parameter settings: centWave for feature detection (Δ m/z = 15 ppm, minimum peak width = 10 s, and maximum peak width = 60 s); obiwarp settings for retention time correction (profStep = 0.5); and parameters for chromatogram alignment, including mzwid = 0.015, minfrac = 0.5, and bw = 5. Finally, after the statistical analysis, only features with q&lt;0.05 were selected for identification through MS/MS experiments. To identify these metabolites, the resulting MS/MS spectra were matched against the METLIN database(<a href="#R69" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R69">69</a>). For dodecanamide, a metabolite whose MS/MS was not contained in any spectral databases, the analytical standard was synthesized.</p></section><section id="S19"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">TARGETED ANALYSIS OF SPERMIDINE</h3>
<p id="P61">Spermidine was analyzed by UPLC (Agilent 1290 Series, Santa Clara, CA) using an Agilent HILIC-z column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.7 μM. Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). The gradient started at 100% phase B, 10% phase B over 20 minutes and held at 10% phase B for 2 additional minutes before re-equilibration (250 μL/min flow rate). The composition of mobile phase A consisted of water with 10mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid and mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile/water 9:1 (v/v) with 10mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid. The injection volume was 5 μL. UPLC was coupled to an Agilent 6495 Series triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) operated in positive ion multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode to monitor a quantitative (146→72) and qualitative (146→30) transition. These transitions were retrieved from METLIN MRM(<a href="#R70" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R70">70</a>) and instrument parameters were optimized using the spermidine analytical standard. The same analytical standard was used to run a calibration curve for spermidine for quantitation.</p></section><section id="S20"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">SYNTHESIS OF DODECANAMIDE (LAURAMIDE)</h3>
<p id="P62">Lauric acid (0.54mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous dichloromethane (DCM) and converted to the acid chloride by reaction with thionyl chloride (2.72mmol) under nitrogen atmosphere. DCM and excess thionyl chloride were removed by rotary evaporation in vacuo, and the remaining acid chloride was subjected to ammonia by addition of ammonium hydroxide solution. The product was extracted by liquid phase extraction with chloroform and purified by flash column chromatography with ethyl acetate and hexane as eluent to give a white solid.</p>
<p id="P63">Dodecanamide structure was verified by nuclear proton magnetic resonance (<sup>1</sup>H NMR) and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) accurate mass as follows: <sup>1</sup>H NMR (600 MHz, CDCl3) δ 5.42 (s, 2H), 2.24 (m, 2H), 1.66 (dd, J = 7.2 Hz, 2H), 1.35–1.28 (m, 16H), 0.90 (t, J = 6.9 Hz, 3H). <sup>13</sup>C NMR (150 MHz, CDCl3) δ 175.05, 35.49, 31.45, 29.15, 29.02, 28.89, 28.87 (d, J = 1.7 Hz), 28.79, 25.09, 22.23, 13.66. HRMS (ESI), <em>m/z</em> calculated for C12H26NO ([M+H]<sup>+</sup>): 200.2009, found: 200.2013.</p></section><section id="S21"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">BIOINFORMATICS</h3>
<p id="P64">Hypothalamic metabolites were ranked using IBM Watson for Drug Discovery platform. A list of keywords and entities related to temperature control by the hypothalamus were used as the known set against which candidate metabolites were ranked. The hypothalamic candidate metabolites were inputted and disambiguated into drug or chemical entities, which contain chemical and structural information, common annotations and known synonyms. Medline Abstracts were queried, and the candidate metabolites were given a similarity score and ranked based on similarity to keywords and entities known to be related to temperature control. Results were validated by re-ranking the metabolites against any random three of the five biological entities used as the know set.</p>
<p id="P65">Because each metabolite’s relative concentration was measured across four time points, we computationally determined the group to which attribute each statistically significant altered metabolite by employing a probability estimation based on the fold-changes of each time point. Three normal distributions were created with the same mean centered at 1 and with a standard deviation equal to 0.05 corresponding to the minimum fold-change (1.05 or −1.05) for which we consider a metabolite quantitatively altered (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">fig. S3a</a>). Left and right distributions (colored) were inverted and clipped to zero below and above fold-change 1, respectively. These distributions were used to calculate a probability for a certain metabolite to be classified as diet-dependent, temperature-dependent or diet-and-temperature dependent. Distribution 1 corresponded to up-regulated metabolites upon CR, distribution 2 corresponded to unchanged metabolites upon CR and distribution 3 corresponded to down-regulated metabolites upon CR. Then, if data for a metabolite in animals housed at both 22°C and 30°C lay in distribution 1 or 3, that metabolite was sorted as diet-dependent. If data for a metabolite in animals housed at one ambient temperature lay in distribution 1 or 3 and data for the same metabolite in animals housed at the other ambient temperature lay in distribution 2, that metabolite was sorted as temperature-dependent. Finally, if a data for a metabolite lay in distribution 1 in animals housed at one ambient temperature and distribution 3 in animals housed at the other ambient temperature, this metabolite was classified as diet-and-temperature dependent. Because statistical significance was not considered in the classification, we introduced a post-hoc factor to correct for false positives. Metabolites classified as distribution 1 or 3 by the algorithm whose q&gt;0.05 (one-way ANOVA followed by a local false discovery rate (FDR) correction) were automatically moved to distribution 2. Because 6 hypothalamic metabolites could not be measured at 30°C, they were not classified into any group.</p>
<p id="P66">Metabolite clustering was performed with the function <em>hclust</em> from the R package stats, grouping objects based on Pearson correlation distance. Outliers were not removed.</p>
<p id="P67">Network analysis was performed with the R package <em>igraph</em> by considering only pairs of metabolites with a Pearson correlation coefficient above 0.6. Outliers were not removed. 10 mice were used for each group, except for AL at 22 °C (9 mice). All 90 plasma metabolites and 37 hypothalamic metabolites were used to compute the correlations (all metabolites found except those hypothalamic metabolites that were measured at 22 °C, but could not be found at 30 °C: PC(32:0), SM(d18:1/17:0), erythro-sphingosine, arachidonoylglycerol, NADP+ and homoarginine). The number of samples used to calculate the correlation between any two metabolites was the same (39 samples at 22 °C and 40 samples at 30 °C).</p>
<p id="P68">Chord diagrams were depicted with the R package <em>circlize</em> by considering only pairs of metabolites with a Pearson correlation coefficient above 0.6. The arc of circumference is proportional to the total number of correlations of each group and sub-group. For the sake of simplicity in the visualization of correlations, ribbons are duplicated. For example, ribbon size showing the number of correlations between lipids and amino acids is shown in red starting in the lipid sector and in blue (with the same size) starting from the amino acid sector. Outliers were not removed.</p>
<p id="P69">The intensity and diameter of the circumferences corresponds to the mean Pearson correlation coefficient between two metabolites that appear in plasma and the hypothalamus. Outliers were not removed. Pathway enrichment was performed by using the Wikipathways database and a custom script using each metabolite’s fold change.</p></section><section id="S22"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">STATISTICS</h3>
<p id="P70">For untargeted metabolomics experiments, 10 mice per time point were used, except for the AL group at 22°C which contained 9 animals. Data were presented as box-and-whisker plots showing the data from all mice analyzed. Outliers were not removed. Multigroup statistical significance of each feature was automatically determined by XCMS Online (<a href="#R68" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R68">68</a>) using a one-way ANOVA followed by a local false discovery rate (FDR) correction of the p-values to generate the corresponding q-values. Features were considered as statistically significant altered when the false discovery rate was q&lt;0.05. Once metabolites were identified, for the pairwise comparison of each calorie restriction time point with AL feeding, a Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test (parametric) was carried out. Metabolites were considered as statistically significant altered at either calorie restriction time point (O, M or T) with respect to AL feeding when p&lt;0.05.</p>
<p id="P71">For physiological parameters, repeated measures 2-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparison correction was used to analyze differences between treatments and ambient temperature groups on T<sub>b,</sub> locomotor activity, energy expenditure and RER. GraphPad Prism 7 was used for data analysis of physiological parameters.</p></section></section><section id="SM1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
