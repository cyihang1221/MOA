</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="P2">The Pulse Couple Neural Network (PCNN) was developed by Eckhorn to model the observed synchronization of neural assemblies in the visual cortex of small mammals such as a cat. In this paper we show the use of the PCNN as an image segmentation strategy to crop MR images of rat brain volumes. We then show the use of the associated PCNN image ‘signature’ to automate the brain cropping process with a trained artificial neural network. We tested this novel algorithm on three T2 weighted acquisition configurations comprising a total of 42 rat brain volumes. The datasets included 40 ms, 48 ms and 53 ms effective TEs, acquisition field strengths of 4.7T and 9.4T, image resolutions from 64x64 to 256x256, slice locations ranging from +6 mm to −11 mm AP, two different surface coil manufacturers and imaging protocols. The results were compared against manually segmented gold standards and Brain Extraction Tool (BET) V2.1 results. The Jaccard similarity index was used for numerical evaluation of the proposed algorithm. Our novel PCNN cropping system averaged 0.93 compared to BET scores circa 0.84.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> PCNN, brain cropping, small mammals, neural networks, segmentation</p></section></section><section id="S1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="P3">A common precursor to several neuroimaging analyses is the use of Brain Extraction Algorithms (BEAs) designed to crop brain tissue from non brain tissues such as cranium, eyes, muscles, skin etc. Following a BEA application, also described as intracranial segmentation or skull stripping, several downstream and independent applications are applied, such as registration of subjects to an atlas for Region Of Interest (ROI) analysis (<a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">Grachev et al., 1999</a>), brain tissue segmentation (<a href="#R46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R46">Shattuck et al., 2001</a>), functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) analysis preprocessing (<a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">Beckmann et al., 2006</a>) and monitoring brain volume as a function of time to study brain atrophy (<a href="#R2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R2">Battaglini et al., 2008</a>). Although these researchers applied BEA and subsequent neuroimaging techniques on human subjects, the number of neuroimaging studies on animal models such as the rat is growing rapidly, providing new insights into brain function as well as improved translation to/from analogous clinical studies. <a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43">Schwarz et al. (2006)</a> cropped 97 brain volumes in the development of a stereotaxic Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) template for the rat brain. The processing pipeline of the somatosensory pathway mapping fMRI study of <a href="#R28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R28">Lowe et al. (2007)</a>, the pharmacological fMRI study of <a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">Littlewood et al. (2006)</a> included rat brain cropping. <a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">Ferris et al. (2005)</a> registered rat brain volumes to an atlas for ROI analysis. Yet, an efficient brain cropping algorithm focused on small mammals is lacking.</p>
<p id="P4">Automated brain extraction is a subset (<a href="#R47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R47">Smith, 2002</a>; <a href="#R53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R53">Zhuang et al., 2006</a>), of general image segmentation strategies which delineates edges between regions frequently exhibiting similar texture and intensity characteristics. However, there is no definitive line separating extraction (cropping) and segmentation functions. All published automated BEAs use various combinations of basic segmentation (<a href="#R36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R36">Pham et al., 2000</a>) techniques on individual slices or on entire 3D volumes to crop brain tissue from non brain tissue. Frequently (<a href="#R47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R47">Smith, 2002</a>; <a href="#R44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R44">Ségonne et al., 2004</a>), automated BEAs have been clustered into the following broad classes: thresholding with morphology based methods (<a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">Lee et al., 1998</a>; <a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">Lemieux et al., 1999</a>; <a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">Mikheev et al., 2008</a>), deformable surface based (<a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">Aboutanos et al., 1999</a>; <a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">Dale et al., 1999</a>; <a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">Kelemen et al., 1999</a>; <a href="#R47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R47">Smith, 2002</a>; <a href="#R53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R53">Zhuang et al., 2006</a>) and hybrid methods (<a href="#R39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R39">Rehm et al., 2004</a>; <a href="#R40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R40">Rex et al., 2004</a>; <a href="#R44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R44">Ségonne et al., 2004</a>). Each of these methodologies have advantages and all areas are being advanced. There is clear evidence (<a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">Lee et al., 2003</a>; <a href="#R40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R40">Rex et al., 2004</a>; <a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">Fennema-Notestine et al., 2006</a>; <a href="#R53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R53">Zhuang et al., 2006</a>) that no single BEA is suitable for all studies or image acquisition protocols. Generally, human intervention is employed for satisfactory cropping.</p>
<p id="P5">Our review of automated BEAs noted a fundamental lack of these algorithms applied to small animals. The methodology has been applied dominantly on human subjects. Most brain tissue cropping in small laboratory animals continues to be manual or semi automatic (<a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35">Pfefferbaum et al., 2004</a>; <a href="#R50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R50">Wagenknecht et al., 2006</a>; <a href="#R45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R45">Sharief et al., 2008</a>). Some studies such as <a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43">Schwarz et al. (2006)</a> working with T2 weighted Rapid Acquisition with Relaxation Enhancement (RARE) sequences have successfully used semi automatic segmentation tools (<a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">Kovacevic et al., 2002</a>) developed for the human brain in animal models. <a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">Kovacevic et al. (2002)</a> had reported a histogram based technique involving the use of co registered Proton Density (PD), T2 weighted anatomy data to crop T1 weighted anatomy images of the human brain. This idea has been supported by the skull and scalp stripping work of <a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">Dogdas et al. (2005)</a> and <a href="#R52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R52">Wolters et al. (2002)</a> who establish that the inner skull boundary can be determined more accurately by the use of PD images. Another example <a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">Roberts et al. (2006)</a> uses an adaptation of Brain Extraction Tool (BET) (<a href="#R47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R47">Smith, 2002</a>) with manual correction for extraction of the rat brain from RARE anatomy data. However, the overall quality of the small animal brain extraction is significantly lower than that obtained for human images (<a href="http://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fslfaq/#bet_animal" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fslfaq/#bet_animal</a>).</p>
<p id="P6">This article presents a novel Pulse Coupled Neural Network (PCNN) based approach to automatically crop rat brain tissue. The proposed method takes advantage of the specificity accorded by T2 weighted images in terms of contrast for the proton rich brain environment and the inherent segmentation characteristics of the PCNN to rapidly crop the rat brain. The method described here does not attempt a second level segmentation to differentiate, for instance, White matter from Grey matter. Rather, the focus is to crop the brain quickly and automatically so that subsequent operations, such as registration can proceed immediately.</p>
<p id="P7">Artificial Neural Network (ANN) and Pattern Recognition methods (<a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">Egmont-Petersen et al., 2002</a>) have been widely applied on the brain tissue type segmentation problem (<a href="#R38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R38">Reddick et al., 1997</a>; <a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">Dyrby et al., 2008</a>; <a href="#R37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R37">Powell et al., 2008</a>). However, there have been very few neural network approaches that specifically address the problem of automatic brain extraction. <a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">Congorto et al. (1996)</a> used a Kohonen Self Organizing Map approach which combines self-organization with topographic mapping and classifies image regions by similarities in luminance and texture. They applied this technique on 2 dimensional T1 slices to segment the image into 3 classes: scalp, brain and skull. <a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">Belardinelli et al. (2003)</a> used an adaption of a LEGION (Locally Excitatory Globally Inhibitory Network) for segmenting T1 weighted 2D images. The LEGION is a neural network model that simulates the human visual cortex in which each pixel is mapped to an individual oscillator and the size of the network is the same as that of the input image. Both <a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">Congorto et al. (1996)</a> and <a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">Belardinelli et al. (2003)</a> provided qualitative results but did not report extensive testing of their respective algorithms on large datasets.</p>
<p id="P8">The underlying algorithm used in this paper is the standard Eckhorn PCNN model (<a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">Johnson and Padgett, 1999</a>). The PCNN is a neural network model based on the visual cortex of a cat, which captures the inherent spiking nature of the biological neurons. The brain extraction is accomplished using the feature extraction property that (<a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">Eckhorn et al., 1990</a>), described in the ‘Linking’ part of their neural network model, which associates regions of input images that are similar in intensity and texture. <a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">Lindblad and Kinser (2005)</a> cover numerous aspects of the PCNN model tuned for image processing applications. Several independent research groups have applied the basic Eckhorn model for various applications; image segmentation (<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">Kuntimad and Ranganath, 1999</a>), image thinning (<a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">Gu et al., 2004</a>) and path optimization (<a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">Caulfield and Kinser, 1999</a>). A recent pattern recognition procedure (<a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">Muresan, 2003</a>) involved the use of the PCNN to generate a 1D time signature from an image. This time signature was then trained using a back propagation neural network model for image recognition. The method proposed in this article follows a similar approach.</p></section><section id="S2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1 Overview</h3>
<p id="P9">The proposed brain extraction algorithm operates on individual 2D grayscale data (slices), <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>. For purposes of illustration of the proposed algorithm we follow the various operations on the representative 2D slice highlighted in <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>. Intensity rescaling to [0 1] is the first operation on each 2D slice, as noted on the highlighted slice in <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>. The PCNN algorithm is then applied in the ‘accumulate’ mode (discussed subsequently) on individual 2D slices, <a href="#F2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>. A morphological operator is employed to break ‘narrow bridges’ that might link the brain tissue with other regions, like the skull, <a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>. A contour operation is used with level set to unity. Only the largest contiguous region from each PCNN iteration is selected, <a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>. The contour outlines corresponding to the selected regions are then overlaid on the corresponding grayscale image, <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>. At this stage the problem is simply one of choosing a particular iteration that best outlines the brain region. The accumulated response as a function of iteration has a characteristic behavior as shown in, <a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>. Several techniques can be used to identify the first plateau in <a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>. A previously trained ANN can be used to identify the iteration that best represents the brain outline. In this mode, one has the option to view the predicted selection with override ability, <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>. This process is repeated for each slice resulting in a set of mask slices that can be used in a marching cube routine (<a href="#R54" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R54">Wu and Sullivan, 2003</a>) to create a full 3D geometry representation of the cropped brain, <a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a>.</p>

<p id="P13">The PCNN is implemented by iterating through <a href="#FD1" class="usa-link">equations (1)</a>–<a href="#FD6" class="usa-link">(6)</a> with <em>n</em> as the current iteration index and ranging from 1 to <em>N</em> (the total number of iterations). <p id="P17">A binary morphological operation breaks ‘narrow bridges’ or clusters of pixels with a radius less than <em>p</em> pixels. Each pixel <em>i</em>, <em>j</em> value (0 or 1) within a PCNN iteration must be continuous in at least two orthogonal directions. That is IF (<em>i</em> ± <em>p</em>,<em>i</em>,…,<em>i</em> ± 1,<em>i</em>) is 1 AND (<em>j</em> ± <em>p</em>, <em>j</em>, …, <em>j</em> ± 1, <em>j</em>) is 1, THEN pixel <em>i</em>, <em>j</em> =1.</p>
<p id="P18">Perimeters or contours of isolated islands are created. The largest area within each PCNN iteration is selected. All pixels within the selected perimeter are filled with ‘ones’. This process results in only one contiguous segment for each PCNN iteration. We denote each PCNN iteration at this stage by <em>C<sub>ij</sub></em> [<em>n</em>]with iteration <em>n</em> ranging from [1, N]. <a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a> is used to illustrate the outcome of the described morphological and contour operations on the same coronal section shown in <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>.</p>
<p id="P19">A successful brain extraction results when an appropriate PCNN iteration n is selected. A 1D time signature is constructed for the PCNN iterations similar to that of <a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">Muresan (2003)</a>. The abscissa or timeline is the iteration count. The ordinate is the total number of pixels within the largest contoured area for each PCNN iteration.</p>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="FD8"><tr><td class="formula"><math id="M8" display="block" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mi>G</mi><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mi>n</mi><mo stretchy="false">]</mo><mo>=</mo><munder><mo>∑</mo><mrow><mi>i</mi><mi>j</mi></mrow></munder><mrow><msub><mrow><mi>C</mi></mrow><mrow><mi>i</mi><mi>j</mi></mrow></msub><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mi>n</mi><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></mrow></math></td></tr></table>
<p id="P20">Where <em>n</em> ranges from [1, N]. This image signature has a characteristic shape for similar images with similar regions of interest. This information is used as a surrogate time series in a traditional ANN training sequence to automatically extract the brain tissue. It is also used as the surrogate time in the first order response fitting. The maximum number of iterations (N) of the PCNN is established when the sum of the array <em>Y</em>[<em>n</em>] (<a href="#FD6" class="usa-link">equation (6)</a>) exceeds 50% of the image space. This maximum iteration count varies somewhat for each slice and subject.</p></section><section id="S6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4 Traditional ANN based selection of brain mask</h3>
<p id="P21">A previously trained ANN receives the accumulated response as a function of iteration and outputs an iteration number, n. Multi Layer Perceptron (MLP) is a widely used (<a href="#R17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R17">Haykin, 1998</a>) supervised, feedforward ANN model which can be trained to map a set of input data to a desired output using standard backpropagation algorithms. Since each grayscale brain coronal section <em>S<sub>ij</sub></em> is now represented by the PCNN iterations <em>C<sub>ij</sub></em> [<em>n</em>] with <em>n</em> ranging from [1, N] and an image signature <em>G</em>, it is possible to create a training set for the MLP.</p>
<p id="P22"><a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a> shows the characteristic shape of the image signature for the sample mid section coronal brain slice. In the illustrated example, iteration numbers corresponding to 0.4 to 0.6 will produce very similar brain masks. This characteristic step response behavior can be fitted easily. It requires few training volumes to create a reliable trained ANN. For the work presented herein, the number of rat brain volumes used to train the network was 7.</p>
<p id="P23">The neural architecture of the MLP used in this article consists of one input layer, one hidden layer and a single output neuron. The input layer neurons simply map to the image signature which is a vector of dimension N. The vector is normalized for the purposes of efficient supervised training using the back propagation algorithm. The hidden layer consisted of about half the number of neurons in the input layer and the single output neuron mapped the desired PCNN iteration corresponding to the brain mask.</p></section></section><section id="S7"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Experiment details and description</h2>
<section id="S8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1 Data</h3>
<p id="P24">T2 weighted RARE anatomical images (<a href="#R48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R48">Spenger et al., 2000</a>; <a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">Ferris et al., 2005</a>; <a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">Roberts et al., 2006</a>; <a href="#R43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R43">Schwarz et al., 2006</a>; <a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">Canals et al., 2008</a>) are widely used in rat brain studies. Three different coronal datasets representing different imaging field strengths, T2 weightings, resolution and coil manufacturers were assembled to demonstrate the proposed algorithm. The field of view was adjusted to span the entire cranium of the rat. The images were acquired along the coronal section of the rat brain. The data were obtained over multiple imaging sessions and multiple studies.</p>
<section id="S9"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">4.7T anatomy dataset (30 volumes)</h4>
<p id="P25">The imaging parameters of this dataset are similar to those published by <a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">Ferris et al. (2005)</a>. Adult Long-Evans rats were purchased from Harlan (Indianapolis, IN, USA) and cared for in accordance with the guidelines published in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health Publications No. 85–23, Revised 1985) and adhere to the National Institutes of Health and the American Association for Laboratory Animal Science guidelines. The protocols used in this study were in compliance with the regulations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University Massachusetts Medical School.</p>
<p id="P26">All data volumes were obtained in a Bruker Biospec 4.7 T, 40 cm horizontal magnet (Oxford Instruments, Oxford, U.K.) equipped with a Biospec Bruker console (Bruker, Billerica, MA, U.S.A) and a 20 G/cm magnetic gradient insert (inner diameter, 12 cm; capable of 120 μs rise time, Bruker). Radiofrequency signals were sent and received with dual coil electronics built into the animal restrainer (<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">Ludwig et al., 2004</a>). The volume coil for transmitting RF signal features an 8-element microstrip line configuration in conjunction with an outer copper shield. The arch-shaped geometry of the receiving coil provides excellent coverage and high signal-to-noise ratio. To prevent mutual coil interference, the volume and surface coils were actively tuned and detuned. The imaging protocol was a RARE pulse sequence (Eff TE 48 ms; TR 2100 ms; NEX 6; 7 min acquisition time, field of view 30 mm; 1.2 mm slice thickness; 256 ×256×12 (nrow×ncol×nslice) data matrix; 8 RARE factor).</p></section><section id="S10"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">4.7T functional dataset (6 volumes)</h4>
<p id="P27">This dataset was obtained with the same hardware and animal specifications as those described in the 4.7 T anatomy dataset. The imaging protocol was a multi-slice fast spin echo sequence (TE 7 ms; Eff TE 53.3 ms; TR 1430 ms; NEX 1; field of view 30 mm; 1.2 mm slice thickness; 64×64×12 (nrow×ncol×nslice) data matrix; 16 RARE factor). This sequence was repeated 50 times in a 5 minute imaging session of baseline data on 6 different rats. The dataset comprised of MRI functional volumes at the 35<sup>th</sup> time step of the study.</p></section><section id="S11"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">9.4T anatomy dataset (6 volumes)</h4>
<p id="P28">The imaging parameters of this dataset are similar to those published by <a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29">Lu et al. (2007</a>, <a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">2008)</a>. The volumes were of a Sprague-Dawley rat, scanned with a Bruker coil setup, 72 mm volume coil for RF transmission with a 3 cm flat receiver surface coil. The imaging protocol was a RARE sequence (Eff TE 40 ms; TR 2520 ms; field of view 35 mm ×35 mm; 1 mm slice thickness; matrix size 192×192, zero-padded to 256×256 for reconstruction). For the purposes of this study 18 slices from +6 mm to −11 mm AP (<a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34">Paxinos and Watson 1998</a>) in a coronal plane passing through the Bregma were considered.</p></section></section><section id="S12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2 Parameters employed</h3>

For purposes of training, 7 rat volumes were used, each containing 12 slices. The activation function of the hidden layer was chosen to be a nonlinear hyperbolic tangent function while that of the output layer was linear. The ‘<em>newff’</em> and ‘<em>train’</em> functions available in Matlab 2007b’s Neural Network toolbox V5.1 was used to train the classifier using the gradient descent with momentum backpropagation algorithm.</p></section></section><section id="S13"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Discussion</h2>
<section id="S14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.1 Results</h3>
<p id="P32">The PCNN based automated algorithm was tested on 42 volumes acquired on the three different rat brain acquisition parameter settings, described in Section 3. These volumes were different from the 7 data volumes used to train the ANN for automatic cropping. The compute time of the algorithm including original volume input (4.7 T, 256×256×12 anatomy volume) to cropped and mask volume outputs is about 5 minutes on a modern Pentium 4 class machine with 4GB RAM. <a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a> provides a qualitative handle of the results obtained using the proposed PCNN based brain extraction algorithm compared to BET.</p>

<p id="P36">The PCNN as an algorithm has outstanding segmentation characteristics and as such independent of the image orientation and voxel dimension scaling. The PCNN readily segments the entire rat brain volume as delineated by <a href="#R34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R34">Paxinos and Watson 1998</a>, (+6 to −15mm AP in a coronal plane passing through Bregma). However, our current selection strategy identifies the largest area within the PCNN iteration mask. This poses a problem in extreme coronal slices (&gt; +7mm AP) where the eyes are larger and brighter as a result of T2 weighting, than the brain region. Surface coils can inherently lower sensitivities in regions distant from the coil diminishing overall image intensities. The PCNN operates only on 2D regions and one of the PCNN iterations would normally capture the brain anatomy and that iteration would be on the plateau (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>) identified by the proposed selection strategy.</p></section><section id="S15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">4.2 Alternate PCNN iteration selection strategies</h3>

Several alternate strategies may be employed to automate or otherwise train the classifier. Conclusion</h2>
<p id="P39">A novel, brain extraction algorithm was developed and tested for automatic cropping of rat brain volumes. This strategy harnessed the inherent segmentation characteristics of the PCNN to produce binary images. These image masks were mapped onto a timeline curve rendering the task into an appropriate iteration selection problem. The surrogate ‘time’ signature was passed to a previously trained ANN for final iteration selection. The algorithm was tested on rat brain volumes from 3 different acquisition configurations and quantitatively compared against corresponding manually created masks which served as the reference. Our results conclusively demonstrate that PCNN based brain extraction represents a unique, viable fork in the lineage of the various brain extraction strategies.</p></section><section id="S18" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
