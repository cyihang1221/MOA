>Methods   Study population and study design   The study population is a random sample of 60 participants from the FACT study, an RCT that recruited 610 adults 20–75 years of age from Araihazar, Bangladesh, starting in 2010. This population is chronically exposed to arsenic-contaminated drinking water, and this clinical trial was initially designed to determine if FA supplementation could increase arsenic methylation and thereby lower blood arsenic concentrations [ 41 , 47 ].   Exclusion criteria for the FACT study included pregnant females, health conditions such as renal disease or gastrointestinal disease, and previous use of nutritional supplements (uncommon in this region). Eligible participants were previously randomly assigned, conditioned on biological sex, to 1 of 5 treatment groups: placebo, 400 (roughly equivalent to the recommended dietary allowance in the United States) [ 48 ], or 800 μg FA/day, 3 g/day creatine, or 3 g/day creatine + 400 μg/day FA. The median compliance with treatment assignment as assessed through pill counts was 99.5% [ 47 ].   For this study, we selected a total of 60 individuals: 30 individuals who received 800 μg/day FA treatment for 12 weeks and 30 sex-matched controls who received placebo for 12 weeks. We excluded participants who smoked tobacco or chewed betel nut.   All participants provided informed consent and ethical approval was obtained by the Institutional Review Board of Columbia University Medical Center and the Bangladesh Medical Research Council. The study protocol has been previously described in detail [ 49 ]. A flowchart of the study participants is presented in  Supplemental Figure 1 . Plasma metabolomic profiling   Collection of biological samples and detection of 5mTHF and UMFA.   Venous blood samples were collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid vacutainers at baseline and at 12 weeks. Further details of blood collection have been previously described[ 47 ]. Plasma 5mTHF and UMFA were determined by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), and red blood cell folate was measured by a radioprotein binding assay as previously described [ [50] ,  [51] ,  [52] ]. Nontargeted metabolomic profiling.   Plasma samples from baseline and week 12 were analyzed using established methods for nontargeted metabolomics [ 53 ]. Briefly, plasma samples previously stored at −80°C without a freeze–thaw cycle, were treated with 2 volumes of acetonitrile containing 14 stable isotope standards, and then transferred to a low-volume vial at 4°C before analysis [ 54 ]. Samples were analyzed in triplicate by liquid chromatography-Fourier transform mass spectrometry (Thermo Scientific Ultimate 3000 liquid chromatography system interfaced to a Fusion Tribrid Orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometer) [ 54 , 55 ] using dual-column chromatography with C 18  run in negative electrospray ionization (ESI) mode (for the retention of nonpolar compounds) and hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC), run in positive ESI (for the retention of polar compounds). All samples were run 6 times, with 3 analytical replicates for each separation method (C 18  and HILIC). The median coefficient of variation (CV) for technical replicates was calculated for each detectable feature, and those with CV >30% were excluded. After data processing, our dataset included information on mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), retention time (seconds), intensity, CV, and related descriptive characteristics, including minimal information standards for metabolomics data using apLCMS [ [56] ,  [57] ,  [58] ] and xMSanalyzer [ 59 ]. In total, nontargeted metabolomics analysis included 12,413 HILIC+ and 14,128 C 18 - features, of which 99 (HILIC+) and 73 (C 18 -) had confirmed chemical metabolite identities via internal libraries generated from commercial standards (that is, “identified” metabolites). Metabolite data processing.   Raw spectral data were processed following established filtering and normalization criteria [ 60 ]. Daily batches were normalized using ComBat [ 61 ]. Two outliers were identified during quantile normalization and again via visualization of exploratory principal component analysis and were subsequently removed. Features that were not detected in at least 50% of the overall samples (2000 features, 7%) were removed and for the remaining features, values below detection were imputed with the lowest observed value divided by square root of 2. For identified metabolites, 2 C 18  metabolites were dropped because of the low detection rate, resulting in 98 HILIC+ and 72 C 18 - metabolites available for analysis. For the statistical analysis, plasma metabolite levels were quantile normalized and log 2 -transformed. Statistical analysis   Association with targeted plasma metabolome.   As our primary analysis, we used linear and logistic regression with Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) penalty [ 62 ] to identify the targeted metabolites ( n =  170) that are most predictive of FA treatment, 5mTHF, or UMFA. We modeled FA treatment as a binary outcome as this formulation allows the identification of metabolites associated with treatment assignment. The difference of plasma 5mTHF between baseline and week 12 was modeled as a continuous outcome and linear regression models were used. The models for UMFA used only week 12 concentrations as a continuous variable because the study population were FA-naïve and UMFA levels at baseline were expected to be negligible and only detectable at week 12 [ 63 , 64 ]. We use this modeling framework to identify the collections of variables that are most strongly associated with FA supplementation and with the levels of plasma 5mTHF and plasma UMFA over the study period. The tuning parameters controlling the degree of penalization (that is, λ) across all 3 endpoint models were selected using leave-one-out cross-validation. For predictors, the difference of  n =  170 identified plasma metabolite levels between baseline and week 12 for each metabolite was included. All models were adjusted for sex, age, and BMI by including them as unpenalized covariates in the models, because these characteristics may have an impact on plasma 5mTHF levels, UMFA levels, and the metabolome. The models for 5mTHF were further adjusted by the treatment group. Sensitivity analyses were run using elastic-net models, again using cross-validation to choose tuning parameters [ 65 ].   As a secondary approach, to quantify the magnitude of the association for the metabolites identified by LASSO, we fitted unpenalized regression models. For the treatment group and plasma 5mTHF, we used generalized estimating equation (GEE) models (which account for the repeated measurements for each individual) and linear regression models for UMFA using only week 12 data. In these models, each person-visit was treated as an observation and each metabolite identified by LASSO was treated as the outcome variable. For interpretation, we calculated the change in the concentration of metabolites per a 10 nm/L increase in the concentration of plasma 5mTHF (∼1 SD) and per 1 nM/L increase in the concentration of UMFA. The model output was then exponentiated to obtain geometric mean ratios (GMR) of metabolite levels. All models were adjusted for sex, age, and BMI. These models were only intended to refine the understanding of the potential directions of association and only run for a limited number of metabolites.   We extracted the KEGG IDs for the metabolites identified by the LASSO analyses and visualized them on the KEGG human metabolic map using iPATH [ 66 ]. Association with nontargeted plasma metabolome and pathway analysis.   In separate models, we fitted unpenalized linear models where FA supplementation, 5mTHF, UMFA levels were predictor variables, and nontargeted metabolites levels (as continuous, log 2 -transformed) were outcome variables. For FA supplementation and 5mTHF, we included both week 0 and week 12 data using GEE models. All models were adjusted for sex, age, and BMI. The outputs from these models were evaluated using Mummichog [ 67 ], a validated annotation-free pipeline specifically designed to analyze nontargeted high-resolution metabolomics data. For each model, we ran the Mummichog algorithm on the MetaboAnalyst platform [ 68 ] specifying mixed mode (both positive and negative ion modes), 5 parts per million mass tolerance, and included retention time,  P  values, and  z -scores from the models.   For the statistical analysis, we used R software (version 4.3.0), and for the LASSO analysis, we used “glmnet” and “caret” R packages [ 65 , 69 ]. Results   Descriptive features and 5mTHF and UMFA distribution   The mean age of the study sample was 34.4 years, and 50% were males. The baseline sociodemographic characteristics and nutritional status variables were similar between the placebo and the treatment groups ( Table 1 ). At baseline, there were no differences between placebo and treatment groups with respect to plasma 5mTHF (7.66 nmol/L ± 3.61 compared with 7.58 nnmol/L ± 2.88) or UMFA (2.19 nmol/L ± 0.81 compared with 2.17 nmol/L ± 0.71). After 12 weeks, the supplementation group had notably higher levels of 5mTHF (35.3 nmol/L ± 15.0 compared with 8.39 nmol/L ± 4.22) and UMFA (4.85 nmol/L ± 10.61 compared with 1.93 nmol/L ± 0.62). Confirmed metabolite associations with FA   In LASSO regression models including 170 plasma metabolites with confirmed identity, 16 unique metabolites were selected. Plasma 5mTHF levels were associated with the largest number of metabolites ( n =  15), followed by FA supplementation ( n =  1) and plasma UMFA ( n =  1). The identified associations include one overlap of arachidonic acid between 5mTHF and UMFA ( Table 2 ). In the LASSO model for FA supplementation compared with placebo, choline was selected as positively associated with FA supplementation. In GEE models, FA supplementation was associated with 32% higher plasma choline levels (95% CI: 11%, 56%) as compared with those with placebo. In LASSO regression models for 5mTHF concentrations, 15 metabolites were selected ( Table 2 ). Seven metabolites were positively associated with higher plasma 5mTHF concentrations, including 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (HIAA), acetylmethionine, creatinine, guanidinoacetate (GAA), hydroxyproline/N-acetylalanine, docosahexanoic acid, and linoleic acid. Eight metabolites were negatively associated with plasma 5mTHF concentrations; these included acetylglutamate, acetyllysine, carnitine, propionyl carnitine, arachidonic acid, cinnamic acid, homogestisate, and nicotine. Lastly, plasma concentrations of UMFA were negatively associated with arachidonic acid ( Table 2 ). Discussion   Folate is an essential nutrient with roles in numerous biological pathways largely because of its central role in OCM. Folate deficiency is a major risk factor for NTDs. In 1998, the United States implemented mandatory fortification of grains with FA, leading to the successful reduction of risk for NTDs. Since then, more than 80 countries have followed suit. In addition, FA supplement use is widespread. Consequently, increasing levels of FA intake through combined food fortification and supplement use has led to a growing acknowledgment that there are “knowledge gaps” related to potential unanticipated effects of moderate-to-high FA intake and/or increased UMFA; these are detailed in the proceedings of an National Institutes of Health workshop [ 29 ]. To begin to address this issue, we leveraged banked samples from an RCT of FA supplementation in a population naïve to FA fortification and applied HRM techniques to identify the metabolomic impact of FA supplementation and of plasma concentrations of 5mTHF and UMFA. This work builds on our previous findings on metabolites more directly related to the FACT study’s primary and secondary outcomes, including choline, betaine, dimethylglycine (described by Hall et al. [ 70 ]) and GAA (described by Peters et al. [ 71 ]). This new expanded approach suggests that FA supplementation induces numerous metabolomic changes using both identified metabolites and nontargeted metabolome profiling.   Consistent with prior knowledge, our analyses identified FA treatment and changes in 5mTHF levels to be associated with changes in several metabolites directly or indirectly involved in OCM. For example, FA treatment was significantly associated with higher levels of choline, consistent with observations by Hall et al. [ 70 ], in which FA was also found to increase betaine (i.e., trimethylglycine) as choline can be oxidized to form betaine. In the liver and kidney, betaine and 5mTHF can serve as alternative methyl donors for the remethylation of homocysteine to contribute to the synthesis of SAM [ 3 , 72 , 73 ]. The most likely reason for increased choline with FA treatment is that the methylation via 5mTHF can spare methyl groups from betaine, thereby increasing the precursor, choline.
