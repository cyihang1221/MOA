>Material and methods   Animals and ethics approval   Our study was conducted in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines ( https://arriveguidelines.org ) to ensure rigorous reporting standards for animal research. All experimental procedures were performed in strict compliance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Animal Welfare and Research Ethics Committee of Anhui Agricultural University, China (Approval Number: 2021012-1). Ours was a prospective case–control study with cats from 3 clinical centers (Anhui Agricultural University Teaching Animal Hospital, Daqing Renxin Pet Hospital, and Daqing Kang’an Pet Hospital) consecutively enrolled into the study from January 2023 to May 2024. All FHL cases were newly diagnosed using standardized criteria (histopathologic confirmation of liver biopsy specimens and abdominal ultrasonography) without prior therapeutic interventions. Importantly, all FHL cases comprised first-opinion patients presenting at initial diagnosis, ensuring that the study population represented the spectrum of cases encountered in primary care settings. Written informed consent was obtained from all cat owners before enrollment of cats in the study. During the selection process, we excluded cats with other underlying diseases or complications associated with FHL using comprehensive evaluations, including CBC, serum biochemistry, and ultrasonography. Hepatic lipidosis (free fatty acid [FL]) group cats were diagnosed based on clinical signs and confirmed by liver biopsy and ultrasonography ( n  = 7;  Figure 1 ). Healthy control cats (cardiolipin [CL];  n  = 7) were recruited during the same study period using standardized criteria and requiring normal ultrasound examination findings without hepatic echogenicity changes or structural abnormalities, laboratory testing demonstrating liver and kidney function tests within species-specific reference ranges, and normal CBC findings, documenting absence of chronic illness with no medication exposure 30 days pre-enrollment, and deliberately including cats across the 1-10 year age range without restriction to enhance population representativeness while maintaining diagnostic consistency with FHL cases (all receiving identical liver biopsy and ultrasound protocols). All controls underwent identical diagnostic protocols and were confirmed normal histologically before enrollment ( Figure 1 ).   Figure 1. Sample collection   Serum and liver tissue samples collected in the study were aligned with methodologies employed in our previous research. 19  Venous blood was collected from fasting cats before their morning feeding to measure various blood analytes using an automatic blood cell analyzer. Simultaneously, blood was placed in centrifuge tubes, kept at 4°C for 2 h, and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The serum samples were prepared from the supernatant and tested for biochemical indicators using an automatic biochemical analyzer. For general anesthesia during liver biopsy, cats received an SC injection of atropine sulfate (0.02-0.04 mg/kg) and an IV injection of propofol (6-8 mg/kg) 15 min before the procedure. A 16G, 20-cm-long biopsy needle (Pro MAG biopsy needle; Argon Medical Device) was used for percutaneous ultrasound-guided liver biopsy under general anesthesia. The liver biopsy tissue samples were divided into 2 parts: one part was preserved in paraformaldehyde for immunohistochemistry and Oil Red O staining, and the other part was stored at −80°C for metabolomics detection. None of the experimental cats experienced any complications post-biopsy. Oil Red O staining and immunohistochemistry of liver tissue   Paraffin-embedded sections: Deparaffinization was achieved by sequential immersion in xylene (2 × 20 min), absolute ethanol (2 × 5 min), and 75% ethanol (5 min), followed by tap water rinsing. The hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining protocol consisted of: (1) nuclear staining with hematoxylin (3-5 min), (2) differentiation in 1% acid-alcohol, (3) blueing in running tap water, and (4) cytoplasmic counterstaining with eosin (5 min). Dehydration through graded ethanol series (85% → 95% → absolute ethanol; 5 min each) and xylene clearing (2 × 5 min) preceded neutral balsam mounting. Microscopic analysis confirmed blue nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm.   For frozen sections, air-dried tissue slices were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), rinsed, and subjected to Oil Red O staining as follows: (1) lipid droplet visualization by immersion in 0.5% Oil Red O solution (8-10 min in light-protected container), (2) destaining in 75% ethanol (30 s), and (3) nuclear counterstaining with Mayer’s hematoxylin (3 min). Sections were mounted with glycerol gelatin, demonstrating orange-red lipid droplets against blue nuclei under brightfield microscopy. Metabolite extraction   Metabolite screening and analysis were performed by BIOTREE PROFILE (Shanghai, China) using the following protocol: Liver tissue samples (50 ± 1 mg) were homogenized in 2 mL Eppendorf tubes containing 500 μL of pre-cooled methanol-based extraction solvent (methanol/water, 3:1 v/v) supplemented with ribitol as an internal standard. Mechanical disruption was achieved by adding steel beads followed by grinding at 35 Hz for 4 min, with subsequent triple ultrasonication cycles (5 min each) in an ice water bath. After centrifugation at 13 800 ×  g  (4°C, 15 min), 200 μL of supernatant was transferred to fresh 1.5 mL EP tubes, with pooled quality control (QC) samples generated by combining 100 μL aliquots from individual extracts. Solvent removal was conducted using vacuum concentration followed by derivatization with 40 μL methoxyamine hydrochloride (20 mg/mL in pyridine) at 80°C for 30 min and subsequent silylation using 50 μL N,O-bis (trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide (1% trimethylsilyl chloride, v/v) at 70°C for 90 min. After cooling to ambient temperature, 5 μL fatty acid methyl esters in chloroform were added as retention index markers. Final derivatives were subjected to randomized sequence analysis via GC–MS/LC–MS platforms to minimize instrumental bias. Metabolite identification   Chromatographic and mass spectrometric analyses were performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled with a 7890B gas chromatography–time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC/TOF-MS) platform (Agilent Technologies, USA). For LC separation, a hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) was maintained at 25°C with 0.5 mL/min flow rate and 2 μL injection volume. Mobile phases consisted of (A) 25 mM ammonium acetate/ammonia in water (pH 9.0) and (B) acetonitrile, employing the following gradient: 95% B (0-0.5 min), linear decrease to 65% B (0.5-7 min), 40% B (7-9 min), re-equilibration at 95% B (9.1-12 min). Gas chromatography separation utilized a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) with helium carrier gas at 1 mL/min. The temperature program was initiated at 50°C (1-min hold), and ramped to 310°C at 20°C/min (6-min hold), with injector/transfer line temperatures at 280°C. Mass spectrometry parameters included: electron impact ionization (−70 eV), ion source at 250°C, mass range 50-500  m/z , acquisition rate 12.5 spectra/s, and solvent delay 4.88 min. Automated sample injection at 4°C incorporated randomized sequences and periodic QC samples (10% injection frequency) to monitor system stability and data reproducibility. Bioinformatics analysis of differential metabolites   Mass spectrometry data were processed using ChromaTOF software (V4.3x, LECO) for peak extraction, baseline correction, deconvolution, peak integration, and alignment. Qualitative analysis was performed using the LECO Fiehn Rtx5 database with mass spectrometry and retention time index matching. Peaks with detection rates < 50% or relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% in QC samples were excluded. Data were centralized and analyzed in SIMCA (V16.0.2, Sartorius). Principal component analysis (PCA) addressed data dimensionality reduction for visualizing high-dimensional metabolomics data, evaluated sample grouping quality by natural separation between FHL and control groups, and detected batch effects or outliers using QC sample distribution patterns, whereas orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) enhanced group discrimination via orthogonal signal correction. The multivariate analysis framework incorporated 3 key validation procedures to ensure methodological rigor. Variable suitability was first confirmed using Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin testing (> 0.6), followed by 7-fold cross-validation to assess model stability. Finally, OPLS-DA models were subjected to 200 permutation tests, which collectively demonstrated the absence of overfitting ( Figure 2 ).   Figure 2. Differential metabolites were screened using combined criteria: variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 (effect size η 2  > 0.14) with false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted  P  < .05. Volcano plot analysis enabled simultaneous evaluation of both effect size (fold-change) and statistical significance ( P -value), thereby overcoming false negatives induced by multiple testing correction. Metabolites exhibiting area under the curve (AUC) > 0.8 in univariate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis were prioritized as candidate biomarkers with robust analytical discriminative capacity. The AUC analyses were conducted by generating ROC curves for each metabolite, which plotted sensitivity against 1-specificity across all classification thresholds. Using the trapezoidal rule, we calculated the area under each ROC curve with 95% CIs derived from 2000 stratified bootstrap replicates, and the Youden index ( J  = sensitivity + specificity − 1) determined optimal cutoff thresholds. This approach quantified each metabolite’s discriminative power (AUC > 0.8 indicating excellent diagnostic accuracy) and estimated the probability that cases would exhibit higher metabolite concentrations than controls. Our goal was to identify metabolites demonstrating both statistical significance ( P -value) and clinical relevance, with AUC benchmarks of > 0.7 for practical utility, > 0.8 for strong discrimination, and > 0.9 for outstanding performance. Differential metabolite clusters were generated using Euclidean distance matrices with complete linkage. Metabolic pathways were annotated using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Pathway Database ( http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html ).   The internal validation framework incorporated four key methodological safeguards: 1000 iterations of bootstrap resampling were performed to establish robust CI, followed by stratified partitioning of the dataset into 70% training and 30% validation subsets to evaluate model generalizability. To address potential class imbalance, synthetic minority oversampling technique was systematically applied during model training. Finally, model performance was quantified using the F₁-score metric, which provides the harmonic mean of sensitivity and specificity for balanced evaluation of classification accuracy. Analytical validation of serum 3-HB assay   Our study represents an analytical validation to establish the assay’s capability to quantify 3-HB concentration differences between FHL cases and healthy controls under standardized laboratory conditions. Serum samples were collected from 2 cohorts: (1) an initial exploratory group consisting of histologically confirmed FHL cases ( n  = 7) and clinically healthy control cats ( n  = 7), and (2) an expanded multicenter cohort including additional FHL cases ( n  = 10, comprising both primary and secondary causes) randomly selected from veterinary hospitals in Hefei and Daqing, along with healthy controls ( n  = 50) meeting standard health screening criteria.   All samples were processed using standardized protocols. The 3-HB concentrations were measured using enzymatic colorimetric assays (initial group: Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute kit on Hitachi 7180 analyzer; expanded cohort: AU2700 automated analyzer, Beckman Coulter) with blinded sample analysis to minimize operator bias. Statistical analysis   Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 18.0. Two-tailed Student’s  t -tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction were utilized to compare differences in baseline indicators between the 2 groups. Data were presented as mean ± SD, with an α level of 0.05 used to determine statistical significance. Volcano plot visualization was conducted using R (version 4.2.0) with the EnhancedVolcano package. Column charts and ROC curves were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 software to determine the optimal cutoff values for biomarkers. Sensitivity and specificity estimates (based on  n  = 10 cases and  n  = 50 controls) were reported with 95% CI using the Clopper–Pearson exact method to quantify measurement precision. Results   Ultrasonographic findings   Hepatic evaluation indicated hepatomegaly with diffuse hyperechogenicity ( Figure 1A ). The portal vein exhibited wall thickening accompanied by irregular bile duct dilatation. Vascular demarcation was obscured by posterior acoustic attenuation (depth-dependent signal loss > 50%). Extrahepatic structures were normal based on sonographic parameters, and showed no evidence of mass lesions or pathological alterations in the spleen, pancreas, or kidneys. Morphological characteristics of hepatic tissues   Microscopic evaluation of H&E-stained specimens indicated distinct morphological differences between normal and fatty liver tissues ( Figure 1B ). Control liver samples (CL1-CL3) exhibited hepatocytes with well-demarcated cellular margins, homogeneous cytoplasmic staining, and centrally located nuclei displaying characteristic basophilic chromatin patterns, with no observable lipid vacuolization. In contrast, the FHL group (FL4-FL6) showed substantial cytoplasmic vacuolization consistent with macrovesicular steatosis. Nuclear displacement to the cell periphery and ballooning degeneration (evidenced by rarefied cytoplasm and ruptured cellular membranes) were prominent features in steatotic hepatocytes. Oil Red O staining results   As shown in  Figure 1C , the Oil Red O staining results of frozen liver tissue sections from cats were distinctly different between the control group (CL1, CL2, and CL3) and the FHL group (FL4, FL5, and FL6). In the control group, the liver cells contained a small number of red lipid droplets of a smaller size, and the hematoxylin-stained nuclei were clearly visible. In contrast, liver cells in the FHL group exhibited a substantial increase in the number and size of red lipid droplets. The Oil Red O staining showed bright and vivid coloration, with occasional visibility of purple nuclei, indicating increased lipid deposition in liver cells and a pronounced fatty liver condition. Quality assurance-driven multivariate analysis in GC–MS metabolomics   The total ion chromatograms of QC samples analyzed by GC/MS demonstrated exceptional analytical reproducibility ( Figure 2A ), precise retention time alignment (mean deviation < 0.1 min) and peak area consistency (RSD < 5%) across all QC injections, confirming robust instrumental stability. Statistical evaluation quantified measurement precision ( Figure 2B ), where 98.7% of QC data points fell within the ±2 SDs, validating the high reliability and analytical robustness of the acquired experimental dataset. Principal component analysis of FL and CL group samples identified homogeneous distribution within the 95% confidence ellipse, as depicted in  Figure 2C  where all samples identified maintained positional integrity without outlier dispersion.  Figure 2D  shows a distinct clustering pattern between groups, indicating substantial differences in their metabolic profiles. GC–MS metabolomics profiling identifies between-group differential metabolites   In this metabolomic profiling study, metabolites demonstrating both VIP scores > 1.0 and significant differential expression ( P  < .05) were designated as discriminatory biomarkers. Comparative analysis of hepatic specimens from cats identified 10 significant differential metabolites between the CL and FL groups ( Table 1 ), with 4 compounds achieving structural identification: γ-lactone ribonic acid, β-glycerophosphoric acid, synephrine isomer 1, and myristic acid. The distribution pattern of these metabolites was computationally delineated using volcano plot analysis ( Figure 3A ). In this volcano plot, the x-axis represents the log2-fold change (log2FC) of metabolite expression between groups, with positive values indicating up regulation and negative values indicating down regulation. The y-axis represents the negative logarithm of the  P -value (−log10  P -value), with higher values reflecting larger statistical significance. The dashed vertical line at log2FC = 0 and the horizontal line at −log10  P -value = 1.3 (corresponding to  P -value = .05) serve as thresholds for distinguishing significant upregulation (red dots,  n  = 9) and downregulation (blue dots,  n  = 1) from nonsignificant changes (gray dots). Notably, metabolites such as myristic acid, ribonic acid, γ-lactone, β-glycerophosphoric acid, and synephrine 1 are marked as significantly upregulated. Quality assurance-driven multivariate analysis in LC–MS metabolomics   Principal component analysis was performed on hepatic samples from FL and CL cohorts, with all samples clustering within the 95% confidence ellipse ( Figure 5A ). Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis subsequently demonstrated marked intergroup separation ( Figure 5B ), confirming distinct metabolic profiles between the cohorts. Model validity was rigorously assessed using 200-iteration permutation testing, identifying progressive decreases in R 2 Y (cumulative model fit) and Q 2  (predictive capability) as permutation retention decreased ( Figure 5C ). These validation outcomes confirm the statistical robustness of the original model, excluding overfitting risks and supporting its analytical reliability.   Figure 5.
