sample_description: The samples used were grab samples taken from a wastewater treatment plant effluent in Warburg (Stadtwerke Warburg GmbH, Warburg, Germany), selected for non-target screening and centroiding algorithm development and validation. These effluent samples contain a high load of organic substances, inducing strong matrix-associated effects in high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) such as signal overlay—making them ideal for rigorously testing the robustness of the centroiding algorithm. Sampling and sample preparation followed detailed protocols published by Hohrenk et al. (2020). The algorithm was developed and validated using both MS1 and MS2 spectra acquired in ESI-positive and ESI-negative modes; however, all examples presented in the paper specifically refer to MS1 spectra in ESI-positive mode. Notably, the chromatographic domain (i.e., retention time dimension) was deliberately neglected during algorithm development, as consecutive mass spectra were assumed to be statistically independent—ensuring the algorithm’s universal applicability to both chromatographically separated and direct-infusion HRMS data.
software_configuration_description: The instrumental measurement raw files (Thermo *.raw format) were converted to open-format profile mass spectra (*.mzXML) using ProteoWizard’s msConvert tool. msConvert was used solely for format conversion—not for centroiding—and no specific parameters or settings (e.g., centroiding filters, peak picking options, or resolution thresholds) were applied during this step, as explicitly stated: 'msConvert was not used for centroiding in this step, but only for conversion to open format.' The conversion enabled easier import into the programming environments (Python v3.9, R v4.0.4, Julia v1.6.3) where the novel centroiding algorithm was implemented.

sample_description: The research utilized seven open datasets (Dataset1 to Dataset7) encompassing both proteomics and metabolomics data, acquired under Data Dependent Acquisition (DDA) and Data Independent Acquisition (DIA)/SWATH modes. These datasets were generated on multiple mass spectrometers: TripleTOF 5600, TripleTOF 6600, Q-Exactive (Thermo QE), Agilent 6550 QTOF, and SCIEX TOF 5500. Specific datasets include: DDA-Dataset1 and DIA-Dataset1 (TripleTOF 5600, PXD021390); DDA-Dataset2 and DIA-Dataset2 (Q-Exactive, PXD018139); DDA-Dataset3 (Q-Exactive, IPX0001509000); DDA-Dataset4 (metabolomics, Q-Exactive, MTBLS2119); DDA-Dataset5 (metabolomics, TripleTOF 6600, XCMS1197236); DIA-Dataset6 (TripleTOF 5600/6600, PXD002952); and DIA-Dataset7 (Agilent 6550, PXD004712). All raw data and converted files (mzML, mgf, mz5, Aird) were uploaded to ProteomeXchange (PXD025142). The total data volume reached several terabytes, and testing was performed on a desktop computer with Intel i7-7700K CPU (4.4 GHz), 16 GB RAM, and 5900 RPM HDD.

sample_description: The research evaluated the software's cross-platform performance using numerous simple and complex sample types from four instrument platforms: AB SCIEX Triple TOF 5600 (UPS1 human, E. coli, Rat Liver), Agilent 6530/6550 QqTOF (Bovine 6, Human Serum), Thermo Scientific Q-Exactive (Bovine 6, Human Jurkat), and Waters Synapt G2/G2-S QqTOF (Bovine 6, Yeast, E. coli, Human K562). Sample complexity increases top-to-bottom per vendor in Table 1. Bovine 6 is a simple mixture of six bovine proteins; UPS1 is a human protein standard; Human Serum, Jurkat, K562 are human biological fluids/cell lines; E. coli and Rat Liver represent prokaryotic and mammalian tissue lysates; Yeast is a eukaryotic model organism. All raw datasets are publicly available via MassIVE (http://massive.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe). Sample preparation and acquisition details are provided in the Supporting Information, with explicit note that many datasets were acquired solely for software evaluation—not under optimal instrument conditions (e.g., Waters Synapt was not run with ion-mobility DIA). Inter-platform comparisons were not intended; instead, ProteoWizard’s performance was benchmarked against each platform’s native or commercial software (e.g., AB SCIEX MS Data Converter, Mascot Distiller) on the same data.
software_configuration_description: msConvert (part of ProteoWizard v3.x, binaries and source at www.proteowizard.sourceforge.net) was configured as the core execution environment for all signal processing steps. It was used to apply three custom filters: (1) CantWaiT peak-picking (applied only to MS/MS spectra by default, though applicable to MS1), (2) Turbocharger precursor charge determination, and (3) Poisson-based deisotoping. CantWaiT was invoked with default parameters: minimum SNR = 1.0 (wavelet-space signal-to-noise ratio) and minimum m/z peak spacing = 0.1 Da; however, for high-resolution Thermo Q-Exactive data, the peak spacing was adjusted to 0.01 Da. Turbocharger was applied with charge search ranges specified per dataset (e.g., +1–+5 for Agilent 6530 Bovine 6; +2–+14 for Agilent 6550 Serum), as listed in Table 1. Isolation windows were set to ±1.00 m/z (AB SCIEX), ±1.25 m/z (Thermo), and ±1.50 m/z (Agilent, Waters). For Waters data, subscan summation was first performed within msConvert using a custom algorithm that summed intensities at identical m/z values across MS/MS spectra sharing the same precursor (within 0.05 m/z and 30 s retention time tolerance, matching Mascot Distiller settings). Vendor libraries were also accessed through msConvert: Thermo and Agilent peak-picking and charge determination libraries were used for comparative analysis (though neither includes deisotoping, so ProteoWizard’s Poisson deisotoper was subsequently applied). Command-line usage examples and full parameter lists (including allowed values for all filters) are documented in Supporting Information Tables S2–S5.
