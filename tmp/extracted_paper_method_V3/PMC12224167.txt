</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Hydroxamate siderophores
are key virulence factors in multiple
pathogens, and their structures present interesting scaffolds for
potential biotechnological applications such as the design of novel
antimicrobials. However, their variable and scarce production, their
low stability, and occurrence as complex mixtures in natural samples
complicate their analysis. Herein, we present a new strategy, named
XAD-LC/MS-FBMN-IMS, which enables the analysis of the <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate siderophore composition from microbial cultures by integrating
traditional XAD resin extraction, modern Feature-Based Molecular Networking
(FBMN) tools, and a novel direct infusion ion mobility mass spectrometry
validation approach. Using ferrioxamine as a model, XAD resins demonstrated
high efficiency in extracting hydroxamate siderophore metal complexes
when compared to other sorbents. This strategy was applied for detecting
the siderophores produced by <em>Tenacibaculum maritimum</em>, a relevant pathogenic bacterium in fish aquaculture. This analysis
unveiled three families of hydroxamate siderophores, including three
known desferrioxamine derivatives along with 17 new putative acyl-desferrioxamine-like
structures. Overall, the XAD-LC/MS-FBMN-IMS strategy significantly
enhances siderophore and metallophore investigation in microbial cultures,
improving our understanding of their production and roles in bacteria
and fungi, ultimately facilitating their biotechnological applications.</p></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><hr class="headless">

<p>In response to iron deficiency, many microorganisms
produce siderophores,
low-molecular weight molecules with high affinity for Fe<sup>3+</sup>, that scavenge the surrounding iron and introduce it into the cell
through specific protein receptors. Since
iron is required for pathogenesis, siderophores are considered key
virulence factors.<em>
−


</em> Their scaffolds have been used for multiple biotechnologic applications
including the design of novel antimicrobial strategies.<em>
−

</em>
</p>
<p>Hydroxamate siderophores, predominantly produced by fungi
and some
bacterial strains, are one of the major
classes of siderophores alongside catecholates/phenolates, α-hydroxycarboxylates,
and mixed-type siderophores. Although
most of the hydroxamate siderophores are hydrophilic, marine siderophores
tend to be amphiphilic, incorporating both hydrophilic and hydrophobic
moieties in their structure. The best-known hydroxamate siderophore
is desferrioxamine B (DFO-B, <strong>1</strong>) (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S1A</a>), first reported in 1960 from <em>Streptomyces
pilosus</em>. Its mesylate salt,
marketed as Desferal, is a FDA-approved drug used as the first-line
treatment of the iron overload disease β-thalassemia.<em>
,
</em>
</p>
<p>Despite the importance of hydroxamate siderophores, their
isolation
and structural elucidation remain challenging due to their low natural
concentration, tendency to diffuse, and
their instability, particularly in their <em>apo</em> form
(unchelated). In some cases, they are produced by a single microorganism
strain as a variable complex mixture of closely related analogs which
complicates their purification and complete characterization. Their instability, low concentration, and rich
structural diversity have been proposed as part of a defense mechanism
by the producing microorganism. This strategy may help prevent their
explotation by other microbes that compete for the limited iron available
in the environment.
</p>
<p>Several procedures
have been reported to extract or preconcentrate
hydroxamate siderophores from microbial cultures prior to their separation
and identification. Direct liquid–liquid extraction from the
culture using organic solvents is commonly avoided due to the high
polar character of these compounds. Reported
extraction or prefractionation methods include the use of C-18 cartridges, hydrophobic resin CHP20P, Amberlite XAD organic polymer resins (XAD-2, XAD-4,<em>
,
</em> XAD-7, and XAD-16), Diaion SP 850, HP20 resin, immobilized
metal ion affinity chromatography (IMAC), and most recently, titanium dioxide nanoparticles. However, most of these methods target unstable <em>apo-</em>forms, which often results
in their degradation and misannotation during subsequent analysis.<em>
,
</em>
</p>
<p>This limitation can be overcome by studying the siderophores
in
their <em>holo</em>-forms (metal complexes), as complexation
stabilizes the siderophores and ensures their structural preservation.
However, the isolation and characterization of the <em>holo</em>-forms is not common, remaining largely unexplored.<em>
,
</em>
</p>
<p>While siderophores naturally chelate iron, they can also coordinate
other metal ions, broadening their analytical and biological relevance.
For instance, complexation with alternative metal ions such as Ga<sup>3+</sup> has proven effective for the isolation and full characterization
of unstable siderophores.<em>
,
</em> Gallium presents a
characteristic isotopic pattern (<em>M</em>
<sub>r</sub> =
69, 71; ratio 3:2) that facilitates the mass spectrometric detection
of gallium complexes, while it enables complete NMR elucidation, which
is not possible for Fe<sup>3+</sup> complexes.<em>
,
</em>
</p>
<p>Herein, we present a novel strategy for the investigation
of <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate siderophores by integrating conventional
extraction and isolation techniques with advanced computational tools
and a unique data validation approach. This strategy, named XAD-LC/MS-FBMN-IMS,
was applied to explore the siderophore metabolome (siderome) of the
fish pathogen <em>Tenacibaculum maritimum.</em> Our approach
not only enabled the streamlined annotation of complex metal-binding
metabolite mixtures but also uncovered new insights into the pathogen’s
iron uptake mechanisms. Overall, the analysis lead to the identification
of 20 hydroxamate siderophores across three distinct siderophore families.
Three siderophores were previously characterized and served as models
to propose putative structures of the 17 new hydroxamate compounds,
expanding the chemical diversity known for this economically relevant
pathogen.</p></section><section id="sec8"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Material and Methods</h2>
<p>In this study, we established
a novel workflow for the extraction
and analysis of <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate siderophores. Ferrioxamine
(<strong>Fe-1</strong>) was used as a model compound to benchmark the
extraction performances of the following resins: C-18, Hydrophilic
Lipophilic Balance (HLB), and Amberlite XAD. Based on the results,
an optimized workflow was developed and applied to investigate siderophores
produced by the fish pathogen <em>Tenacibaculum maritimum</em> LSP9.1 grown under iron-deficient conditions.</p>
<p>Microbial cultures
were treated with GaBr<sub>3</sub> to chelate
and stabilize the siderophores. After centrifugation and filtration,
siderophores from both the cell-free supernatant and the methanolic
extract of the cell pellet were extracted using XAD-7 resin. The extracted
metabolites were analyzed by HPLC-MS/MS, studied by feature-based
molecular networking (FBMN), and validated via direct infusion - ion
mobility spectrometry coupled to mass spectrometry (DI-IMS-MS).</p>
<p>A detailed description of materials and reagents, <strong>Fe-1</strong> synthesis and calibration curve, SPE protocols, bacterial culture
conditions, <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate siderophore extraction,
LC–MS/MS paramethers, FBMN anlaysis, and DI-IMS-MS validation
are provided in the <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supporting Information 1</a>.</p></section><section id="sec9"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results and Discussion</h2>
<section id="sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Optimization of <em>Holo-</em>Hydroxamate
Siderophore
Extraction</h3>
<p>Effective extraction of <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate
siderophores requires sorbents that fulfill two essential criteria:
(1) sufficient affinity for the analyte to ensure its retention, and
(2) a reversible interaction between the sorbent and the analyte to
obtain high recovery percentages. Therefore, we evaluated three of
the most common nonionic and nonpolar sorbents used in natural products
and siderophore studies: C-18 cartridges, HLB cartridges, and Amberlite
XAD resins using ferrioxamine (<strong>Fe-1</strong>) recoveries as a
benchmark. UV–vis spectroscopy of <strong>Fe-1</strong> showed
a characteristic maximum at 429 nm, which was used for quantification
(<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S1C</a>). The absorption maxima of the UV–vis spectra of <strong>1</strong> and the Fe­(acac)<sub>3</sub> salt, were also recorded to rule out
possible interferences in <strong>Fe-1</strong> detection (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S1C</a>). A calibration curve at 429 nm in
the concentration range of 25–500 μM of <strong>Fe-1</strong> yielded an extinction coefficient of ε = 2160 ± 5.1 L
mol<sup>–1</sup> cm<sup>–1</sup>, which was consistent
with previously reported coefficients for this complex (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S1D</a>).<em>
,
</em>
</p>
<p>C-18
and HLB sorbents are commonly used for the extraction and separation
of organic compounds from aqueous environments. To test their affinity
for <em>holo-</em>hydroxamate siderophores, 0.75 mmol of <strong>Fe-1</strong> were loaded onto each cartridge and eluted with a step-elution
of aqueous methanol (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%, <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>). Surprisingly, both sorbents
exhibited strong interaction with the analyte. <strong>Fe-1</strong> was
detected throughout all the collected fractions with maximum analyte
recoveries of 18% and 19% per fraction in C-18 and HLB cartridges,
respectively (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>A and B). C-18 cartridges also failed to
efficiently retain the analyte, with 28% of <strong>Fe-1</strong> lost
during loading (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>A). Contrarily, <strong>Fe-1</strong> was not detected in the
nonretained fraction when using HLB cartridges (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>B).</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="fig1"><h4 class="obj_head">1.</h4>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=12224167_ac5c01687_0001.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/d5f1/12224167/1c16cff9e1fa/ac5c01687_0001.jpg" loading="lazy" id="gr1" height="228" width="700" alt="1"></a></p>

<figcaption><p>Recovery percentages of <strong>Fe-1</strong> from
the different fractions
obtained by SPE using C-18 cartridge (<strong>A</strong>), HLB cartridge
(<strong>B</strong>), or nonionic Amberlite XAD resins (<strong>C</strong>).</p></figcaption></figure><p>Previous studies presented C-18
cartridges as the standard methodology
for the isolation of organic-metal complexes in aqueous samples;<em>
,−



</em> however, they consistently recovered low yields, typically ranging
between 10 and 30%.<em>
,,,
</em> The limited recoveries of the C-18 cartridges
have been attributed to the presence of free silanol groups in this
stationary phase, which strongly interact with metals.<em>
,
</em> The metallic complexes can be retained even after elution with 100%
organic mobile phase, requiring the addition of acid, e.g. 0.5% HNO<sub>3,</sub> to be eluted. Previous studies
showing the interaction between metallic complexes and C-18 sorbents
are consistent with our findings.<em>
,
</em>
</p>
<p>HLB
cartridges displayed slightly better extraction performances
than C-18 and were able to efficiently retain <strong>Fe-1</strong> (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>B). However, the
cartridges still failed to concentrate the analyte into a single fraction,
instead distributing <strong>Fe-1</strong> across multiple fractions with
yields ranging from 12 to 19% (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>B). This contrasts the effectiveness of HLB cartridges
for the extraction of catecholate/phenolate siderophores, probably
due to π-π stacking interactions facilitated by vinylpyrrolidone
(hydrophilic) and divinylbenzene (lipophilic) groups that interact
with the aromatic functionalities of catecholate/phenolate siderophores.<em>
,,
</em> In contrast, HLB cartridges appear
to be less suitable for hydroxamate-type siderophores, which lack
such aromatic functionalities.</p>
<p>Amberlite XAD resins (XAD-2,
XAD-4, XAD-7, and XAD-16) were evaluated
by incubating a 150 μM <strong>Fe-1</strong> solution with 10 mL
of resin overnight, followed by MeOH desorption. The tested Amberlite
XAD proved to be highly effective for the extraction of the <em>holo-</em>hydroxamate siderophore <strong>Fe-1</strong>, with recoveries
ranging from 65 to 74% (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>C). Among these resins, XAD-7 exhibited the highest recovery
(74%). Hydrophobic XAD resins have been widely used for the extraction
of <em>apo</em>-hydroxamate siderophores, where retention
is primarily driven by hydrophobic interactions between resins and <em>apo</em>-hydroxamic siderophores.<em>
,−


</em> However, upon complexation with the metal core, the polarity of
the siderophore increases. XAD-2, XAD-4, and XAD-16 are highly hydrophobic
styrene-divinylbenzene copolymers with varying pore sizes, whereas
XAD-7, a cross-linked acrylic ester, is the only “moderately
polar” resin. This difference in polarity may account for the
superior performance of XAD-7 in extracting <em>holo</em>-siderophores.
</p>
<p>In summary, C-18 and HLB sorbents showed
high interaction and low
recoveries during elution for the <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate
siderophore. In contrast, XAD-7 resin provided a gentle and more effective
method for extracting these compounds from aqueous samples. To the
best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic comparison of
extraction strategies specifically for <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate
siderophores.</p></section><section id="sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Detection and Identification of <em>Holo</em>-Hydroxamate
Siderophores via XAD-LC/MS-FBMN-IMS</h3>
<p>After the optimization
of the extraction method, we established a robust workflow for the
accurate detection and identification of <em>holo</em>-metallophores
in XAD-7 extracts. This workflow is divided in the following steps:</p>
<section id="sec1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Untargeted HPLC-MS/MS and Feature-Based Molecular
Networking (FBMN) Analysis</h4>
<p>Untargeted HPLC-MS/MS analysis
of the XAD-7 extracts provides a comprehensive snapshot of the bacterial
metabolome. Each compound exhibits a unique MS/MS fragmentation pattern,
facilitating partial structural characterization and identification
of structural similarities among metabolites.<em>
,
</em> After preprocessing the precursor ions based on various features
(isotope patterns, retention time, and even ion mobility separation)
in MZmine, the data is submitted to the Global Natural Product Social
(GNPS) platform for Feature-Based Molecular Networking (FBMN), which
organizes the MS/MS spectra into networks based on spectral similarity
and dereplicates ions against reported compound spectra. This approach
facilitates the identification of metabolites and provides a visual
representation of the entire metabolome within the analyzed sample.<em>
−


</em>
</p></section><section id="sec2"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2. 
<sup>69/71</sup>Ga and <sup>54/56</sup>Fe Isotopologue
Screening for Potential <em>Holo</em>-Siderophores in the
Generated Networks</h4>
<p>The distinctive isotopic patterns of gallium
(<em>M</em>
<sub>r</sub> = 69, 71; ratio 3:2) and iron (<em>M</em>
<sub>r</sub> = 54, 56, 57, 58; ratio 6:92:2:0.3) are easily
recognizable in mass spectrometry. The addition of their salts to
the sample facilitates their detection in MS by forming their corresponding
metal complexes while stabilizing the analyte. There are several open-source
tools available that can aid the isotopologues search, including the
‘isotope peak scanner’ function in MZmine, or the CAMERA
package in R, which can annotate isotopic patterns and metal-specific
adducts when combined with XCMS for peak detection and alignment.
</p></section><section id="sec3"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Prediction of Molecular Formulas
by SIRIUS</h4>
<p>The chemical formulas of the detected isotopologues
and their fragmentation
pattern are then predicted with the open-source software SIRIUS 5.8.3. This information enables the dereplication of
the data against different databases to provide putative annotations.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Manual Ion Identity (MII) Layer for Data Simplification</h4>
<p>The metal-binding properties of siderophores lead to the formation
of multiple ion species and adducts, often with distinct retention
times and MS<sup>2</sup> fragmentation patterns in HPLC-MS/MS analysis.
Consequently, adducts (e.g., [M + H]<sup>+</sup>, [M + Na]<sup>+</sup>, [M + K]<sup>+</sup>) from the same metabolite may be distributed
across multiple molecular families rather than clustering together
within a single network. This dispersion complicates comprehensive
annotation and interpretation, hindering accurate metabolome characterization.</p>
<p>Several computational tools have attempted to address this issue,
typically using retention time filtering and the correlation of feature
intensities across spectra. These methods are poorly suited for metallophore
analysis where metal-adduct forms (e.g., Fe<sup>3+</sup>- and Ga<sup>3+</sup>) may not coelute or exhibit consistent intensity correlations.<em>
−


</em> Recently, Dorrestein and co-workers introduced ion identity molecular
networking (IIMN), which relies on the presence of unbound <em>apo-</em>forms of ionophores to establish ion relationships (e.g.,
adducts, charge states, neutral losses) via MS<sup>1</sup>-based mass
differences. Since most of the siderophores
are present in their <em>holo</em>-forms after metal-chelation
of extracts, direct application of this computational tool is challenging.</p>
<p>To overcome these challenges, a manual ion identity (MII) curation
step was implemented to refine the network. Chemical formula predictions
and MS<sup>1</sup> isotopic patterns were inspected to manually identify
and group ions corresponding to different adducts of the same compound
(Supplementary data 2). As a result, otherwise disconnected nodes
across the molecular network were linked, reducing redundancy and
improving interpretability.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">5. Data Validation by Direct-Infusion
Ion Mobility
Spectrometry–Mass Spectrometry (DI-IMS-MS)</h4>
<p>HPLC-MS/MS
data used for FBMN studies generally uses reverse-phase sorbents for
sample analysis. However, we observed that <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate
siderophores strongly interact with these sorbents, and may form artifacts,
compromising the integrity of the results. To address this, we propose
the use of direct-infusion ion mobility spectrometry coupled to mass
spectrometry (DI-IMS-MS) as an orthogonal layer of validation and
annotation to FBMN.</p>
<p>IMS offers a mild, rapid (millisecond time
scale), and sensitive separation of ions that provides valuable information
about the composition of complex mixtures without the use of other
separation techniques. Interestingly, IMS can be coupled to different
detection methods such as MS that increases its value and usefulness.
Ion mobility also provides specific features of each precursor ion,
such as drifting time, arrival time or collision cross section (CCS)
that could be potentially used as features for refining the data in
FBMN. In the present study, we validated the data based on exact mass
and <sup>54/56</sup>Fe or <sup>69/71</sup>Ga isotopologues pattern.
The nodes detected via IMS were then dereplicated against different
databases based on their proposed chemical formulas. To our knowledge,
this study represents the first application of IMS-MS as a data validation
tool for FBMN analyses.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">6. Putative Siderophore Structure
Elucidation Based
on MS/MS Fragmentation</h4>
<p>Structural characterization of validated <em>holo</em>-siderophores was performed through careful interpretation
of MS/MS fragmentation spectra. Fragmentation trees generated via
SIRIUS can provide preliminary insights into substructural motifs
and diagnostic neutral losses. However, the results must be considered
with caution, as we observed that the program often fails to generate
correct fragment annotations for metal complexes. Although NMR elucidation
is usually preferred, the interaction of <em>holo</em>-hydroxamic
siderophores with reverse-phase supports and complex bacterial production
of highly similar analogs hinder its use. Thus, inferred structures
are proposed here based on recurring fragmentation patterns, metal-retaining
fragment ions, and complementary literature data.</p>
<p>Overall, this
systematic multistep approach (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>) not only serves for the identification of new siderophores
but also improves our understanding of their chemical diversity and
the underlying mechanisms of iron chelation.</p>

for the analysis of <em>holo-</em>metallophores.</p></figcaption></figure></section></section><section id="sec12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Application of the XAD-LC/MS-FBMN-IMS Strategy to Unveil the
Siderome of <em>Tenacibaculum maritimum</em>
</h3>
<p>The
XAD-LC/MS-FBMN-IMS strategy (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>) was evaluated for the detection and identification
of the siderophores from <em>Tenacibaculum maritimum</em> LSP9.1,
a strain isolated from a tenacibaculosis outbreak in cultured sole
(<em>Solea senegalensis</em>) in Spain.</p>
<p>
<em>Tenacibaculum
maritimum</em> is the causative agent of tenacibaculosis, an ulcerative
infection with lack of host specificity that severely impacts the
aquaculture industry worldwide.<em>
,
</em> Recent genomic studies
of this bacterium showed the presence of iron uptake systems mediated
by hydroxamate siderophores as putative virulence mechanisms.<em>
−

</em> Notably, the detected biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) displayed
high homology for the <em>mbs</em> locus, which has been proposed
to encode hydroxamic bisucaberin and desferrioxamine-like compounds. Despite the significance of <em>T. maritimum</em> as a fish pathogen in aquaculture, no studies have characterized
its siderome.</p>
<p>To enhance the production of siderophores, <em>T. maritimum</em> LSP9.1 strain was grown in minimal medium supplemented
with the
chelating agent EDDHA. The presence of siderophores was confirmed
by the appearance of a yellow halo around the colonies on CAS agar.
However, manipulation of the culture (e.g., centrifugation) resulted
in decreased CAS value, denoting unstable siderophores.</p>
<p>Therefore,
iron-deficient cultures of <em>T. maritimum</em> LSP9.1 were
treated with GaBr<sub>3</sub> to stabilize the siderophores.
The cell-free supernatant and cell pellet were then extracted with
XAD-7 resin to afford the CAS-active extracts TMSX7M and TMPX7M, respectively.
These extracts, along with the standard <strong>Ga-1</strong>, were subjected
to HPLC-MS/MS. Acidic conditions were strictly avoided to prevent
degradation and dechelation of the siderophores during separation.
The limit of detection (LOD) of this analysis for <strong>Fe-1</strong> used as a standard for <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate
siderophores and tested over the range of 62.5 to 1.95 μg/mL
was 7.8 μg/mL. As expected, strong interaction of <strong>Fe-1</strong> with the RP column was observed, resulting in the detection of the
complex across the entire run rather than as a distinct peak and preventing
a gradual decrease in signal intensity between dilutions.</p>
<p>The
HPLC-MS/MS data of both extracts and <strong>Ga-1</strong> were
analyzed by FBMN (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S2, Tables S1 and S2</a>). The search for <sup>69/71</sup>Ga and <sup>54/56</sup>Fe isotopologues
revealed seven subnetworks comprising a total of 122 precursor ions
putatively associated with metal-complexes (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a> and <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_001.xlsx" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supporting Information 2</a>). The MII layer, based on chemical formulas predicted with
SIRIUS, reduced the number of chelating compounds to 45 (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_001.xlsx" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supporting Information 2</a>). Interestingly, aluminum
and iron complexes were also detected, despite not being intentionally
introduced, which were annotated based on isotopic patterns and exact
masses (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_001.xlsx" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supporting Information 2</a>). DI-IMS-MS
analysis of TM<strong>S</strong>X7M and TM<strong>P</strong>X7M extracts validated
only 20 out of the 45 metal complexes detected by FBMN (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S3–S21</a>). The sensitivity of DI-IMS-MS
was also measured in terms of <strong>Fe-1</strong> concentration detecting
the LOD at 7.9 pg/mL, which denotes a high sensitivity for our compounds
and outcompetes the LOD displayed by HPLC-MS/MS.</p>

we first examined the MS/MS fragmentation
pattern of <strong>Ga-1</strong>. The MS<sup>2</sup> fragmentation of
this <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate siderophore can be rationalized
by remote hydrogen rearrangements (RHR) (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S22</a>), which confirmed the presence of the characteristic <em>N</em>-hydroxy-<em>N</em>-succinyl-cadaverine (HSC) units

(fragment <strong>d</strong>). Finally, another cadaverine unit is released
from the metal complex (fragment <strong>b</strong>), while the metal
core remains intact throughout the process (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S22</a>).</p>
<p>Analysis of the MS/MS fragmentation patterns of
the 20 metal complexes
revealed a common core structure with <strong>Ga-1,</strong> displaying <em>N</em>-hydroxy-<em>N</em>-acyl cadaverine (HAC) and HSC
units, indicative of desferrioxamine-like structures. Variations included
the presence of a hydroxamic acid functionality at one end or additional
HSC units that enabled their classification into three distinct series
(families) (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>). Within each family, differences were found in the length and degree
of unsaturation of the fatty acid tail in the HAC unit.</p>
<p>The
first family of compounds is characterized by the presence
of a second HSC unit, of which one is acetylated. This series is distributed
across subnetworks 1, 2, and 3 (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>A). Chemical formula dereplication allowed for the
annotation of desferrioxamine D1 (<strong>2</strong>) and two previously reported siderophores from two <em>Streptomyces</em> species, C4 acyl desferrioxamine-D (C4 acyl
DFO–D, <strong>3</strong>) and C5 acyl desferrioxamine-D (C5 acyl
DFO–D, <strong>4</strong>) (referred in the original report as <em>N</em>-butyryldesferrioxamine B).<em>
,
</em> Additionally,
the putative structures of five new desferrioxamine D-like siderophores,
compounds <strong>5</strong>-<strong>9</strong>, within this family were proposed

of ferrioxamine D1 chelated with Ga<sup>3+</sup> (<strong>Ga-2</strong>) (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>A). Siderophores
from this family displayed four characteristic neutral losses (<strong>a</strong>, <strong>e</strong>, <strong>d</strong>, and <strong>b</strong>) produced
by remote hydrogen rearrangement (RHR). After losing a molecule of
water (<strong>a</strong>), the characteristic terminal acetylated cadaverine
unit is cleaved observing a neutral loss of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 142.1108 (<strong>e</strong>, calc. for C<sub>7</sub>H<sub>14</sub>N<sub>2</sub>O, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 142.1106,
Δ 1.4 ppm). This cleavage is then followed by the loss of a
succinyl moiety (fragment <strong>d</strong>, calc. for C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 82.0055, Δ 2.4 ppm)
and a cadaverine unit (<strong>b</strong>, calc. for C<sub>5</sub>H<sub>12</sub>N<sub>2</sub>, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 100.1000,
Δ 1.0 ppm) (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>A). These fragmentations confirmed not only the presence of
the two HSC units but also their structural resemblance to desferrioxamine
D1 (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Figure S22 and S23</a>).</p>

These tenacibactin-like siderophores displayed the loss of a water

the compounds of the third family are characterized
by the presence of two HSC units and a succinyl group at one end,
which resemble those of tenacibactin C isolated from a bacterium belonging
to the <em>Tenacibaculum</em> genus.<em>
,
</em> Within this group, two new structures for compounds <strong>20</strong> and <strong>21</strong> were proposed. Three additional compounds <strong>17</strong>–<strong>19</strong>, related to this family, were detected
but their structures could not be predicted from the current data.
MS/MS fragmentation pattern of these complexes exhibited a diagnostic
neutral loss of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 216.1111 (<strong>g</strong>) proposed as the cleavage of an HSC unit (calc. for C<sub>9</sub>H<sub>16</sub>N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 216.1110, Δ 0.5 ppm), as depicted in <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figures

been reported from <em>T. mesophilum</em>,<em>
,
</em> a species closely related to <em>T. maritimum</em>, this
compound was not detected in this study.</p>
<p>Based on the data,
the exact structures of the fatty acid tail
in the HAC unit at one end could not be fully established, mainly
in terms of the position and configuration of the double bonds. These
fatty acid chains could either be linear, mimicking those in desferrioxamine-like
siderophores, or contain a terminal isopropyl group as seen in the
previously reported tenacibactins.<em>
,
</em> The amphiphilic
character of the metal complexes reported here resulted in their distribution
between the cell-free supernatants and the cell-pellet extracts from
the cultures of <em>T. maritimum</em> LSP9.1 (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S3</a>). Long aliphatic chains are likely to
be inserted in the bacterial membrane, avoiding diffusion in marine
environments and facilitating iron transport through membrane-embedded
receptors. The <em>N</em>-acetylated terminal siderophores
(first family) including the three known siderophores were predominantly
detected in the cell-free supernatant, while the hydroxamic acid-terminal
siderophores (second family), and <em>N</em>-succinic acid
terminal siderophores (third family) were detected in both the cell-free
supernatant and the cell-pellet extracts from the cultures of <em>T. maritimum</em> LSP9.1 (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/acs.analchem.5c01687/suppl_file/ac5c01687_si_002.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Table S3</a>). Some nodes were exclusive to the cell pellet; yet, the presence
of siderophores in cell pellets are often overlooked. This work highlights
the importance of analyzing both supernatants and cell pellets to
gain a holistic understanding of the siderophore production of pathogenic
bacteria. Semiquantitative analysis of the siderome based on node
intensities (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>) showed the hydroxamic acid-terminal tenacibactin-like siderophores
as the most abundant class, followed by those terminating in a succinic
acid moiety. The least abundant were the acetylated desferrioxamine-like
siderophores. This distribution reflects the metabolic preference
of <em>T. maritimum</em> under iron-limited conditions and
underscores the structural diversity within its siderome.</p>
<p>In
summary, we propose 17 putative structures of new <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate compounds produced by <em>Tenacibaculum maritimum</em> and detect three additional compounds whose structures remain unresolved.
From this set of metallophores, desferrioxamine D1 (<strong>2</strong>), C4 acyl desferrioxamine-D (<strong>3</strong>), and C5 acyl desferrioxamine-D
(<strong>4</strong>), were previously reported, while the remaining 17
(<strong>5</strong>-<strong>21</strong>) represent potential not previously
reported new metallophores (<a href="#fig5" class="usa-link">Figure 
</a>). To the best of our knowledge, this study represents
the first comprehensive characterization of the siderome of <em>T. maritimum</em>.</p>

Strategy</h3>
<p>This study utilizes direct-infusion ion mobility
spectrometry coupled
with mass spectrometry (DI-IMS-MS) as a novel data validation layer
within a Feature-Based Molecular Networking (FBMN) workflow. While
IMS provides distinct advantages, due to its versatility in data validation
and separation, it also has limitations when applied to complex mixtures.</p>
<p>IMS offers a mild, rapid (millisecond time scale), and sensitive
separation of ions that provides valuable information about the composition
of complex mixtures without the use of other separation techniques.<em>
,
</em> It is particularly effective for resolving complex mixtures of reverse-phase
sensitive analytes and isomeric or isobaric compounds - such as isomers
with different double bond positions, like compounds <strong>6</strong>, <strong>8</strong>, and <strong>9</strong> - that are often challenging
to distinguish using liquid chromatography alone. The additional molecular
descriptors generated by IMS (e.g., drifting time, arrival time or
collision cross section) also improve the detection and filtering
of the metabolites, leading to cleaner and more robust data in FBMN.
However, availability of databases that incorporate these parameters
are still limited.</p>
<p>DI-IMS also displays some drawbacks related
to low separation resolution
and low IMS-MS sensitivity along with the lack of metallophores standards
and software limitations. DI-IMS resolving power is lower than that
of liquid chromatography, commonly resulting in the coelution of analytes.
Therefore, ion mobility separation can sometimes negatively affect
the sensitivity of mass detection, which could in turn influence the
finding of low abundance analytes. For this reason, optimization of
IMS parameters is crucial for the analysis.</p>
<p>The lack of software
packages that incorporate DI-IMS-MS data remains
a major bottleneck of this strategy. Although ion mobility data has
recently been incorporated into open source processing tools like
MZmine, current software does not support the analysis of data obtained
via direct infusion. Similarly, new software tools are needed for
the ion identity of metal complexes within the FBMN workflow that
do not rely on the presence of <em>apo</em>-forms in the mixture.
Due to these limitations, we ultimately performed this layer of analysis
manually, highlighting the need for further software development.</p>
<p>The analysis of siderophores and other metallophores presents unique
challenges due to the absence of commercial standards and specialized
software tools for their analysis and structural elucidation. These
limitations can make reliable identification difficult, especially
when dealing with new molecules. However, advancements in computational
tools and community-driven database development hold promise for improving
the identification and characterization of these important biomolecules.</p></section></section><section id="sec14"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p>A novel, robust, and high-sensitivity strategy
is presented for
comprehensive characterization of <em>holo</em>-hydroxamate
siderophores and metallophores. By integrating XAD-based extraction
with Feature-Based Molecular Networking (FBMN) and data validation
through direct-infusion ion mobility spectrometry-mass spectrometry
(DI-IMS-MS), we overcame limitations of traditional chromatographic
methods, particularly for C-18 sensitive analytes.</p>
<p>This innovative
strategy was successfully applied to detect siderophores
produced by <em>Tenacibaculum maritimum</em>, a pathogenic
marine bacterium of significant ecological and aquacultural concern.
Using this methodology, we were able to propose the structures of
20 hydroxamate siderophores, of which 17 had not been reported. These
compounds were distributed across both the cell-free supernatant and
cell-pellet extracts. This work represents the first detailed siderome
characterization of <em>T. maritimum</em>, providing key insights
into the iron acquisition strategies of this marine pathogenic bacterium
as one of its key virulence factors.</p>
<p>While IMS does not replace
chromatographic separation in terms
of resolution, its application as a complementary validation layer
in FBMN workflows, particularly for hydrophilic, reverse-phase sensitive,
or isomeric compounds, was thoroughly demonstrated. This work deepens
the research on the extraction and structural elucidation of hydroxamate-type
siderophores. At the same time, it lays the groundwork for future
research into the development of improved computational tools that
integrate DI-IMS data into FBMN workflows, providing novel solutions
for the analysis of metal complexes. Overall, this workflow significantly
improves siderophore research by providing a versatile, reproducible,
and comprehensive strategy. Its application in the analysis of siderophores
from microorganisms will increase our understanding of microbial metal
acquisition and their role in bacterial or fungal virulence, paving
the way for potential applications in medicine, agriculture, and environmental
remediation. Furthermore, we believe the XAD-LC/MS-FBMN-IMS strategy
is also suited for the analysis of other metallophores or analytes
exhibiting strong reverse-phase interactions.</p></section><section id="sec15"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
