</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>ABSTRACT</h2>
<p>Disruption of mitochondrial function is observed in multiple drug-induced liver injuries (DILIs), a significant global health threat. However, how the mitochondrial dysfunction occurs and whether maintain mitochondrial homeostasis is beneficial for DILIs remains unclear. Here, we show that defective mitophagy by OPTN (optineurin) ablation causes disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis and aggravates hepatocytes necrosis in DILIs, while OPTN overexpression protects against DILI depending on its mitophagic function. Notably, mass spectrometry analysis identifies a new mitochondrial substrate, GCDH (glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase), which can be selectively recruited by OPTN for mitophagic degradation, and a new cofactor, VCP (valosin containing protein) that interacts with OPTN to stabilize BECN1 during phagophore assembly, thus boosting OPTN-mediated mitophagy initiation to clear damaged mitochondria and preserve mitochondrial homeostasis in DILIs. Then, the accumulation of OPTN in different DILIs is further validated with a protective effect, and pyridoxine is screened and established to alleviate DILIs by inducing OPTN-mediated mitophagy. Collectively, our findings uncover a dual role of OPTN in mitophagy initiation and implicate the preservation of mitochondrial homeostasis via inducing OPTN-mediated mitophagy as a potential therapeutic approach for DILIs.</p>

<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>KEYWORDS:</strong> Drug-induced liver injury, mitophagy, mitochondrial homeostasis, optineurin, phagophore formation</p></section></section><section id="s0001"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Drug-induced liver injury (DILI), an unpredictable event in response to the exposure of medication, is the most common cause of acute liver failure and the most frequently issued by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration with a boxed warning in the drug’s safety labeling [<a href="#cit0001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0001">1–3</a>]. Over 1000 medications, with acetaminophen (APAP) being the most common, have been linked to DILI [<a href="#cit0004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0004">4</a>]. Because of the lack of a valid diagnostic biomarker, DILI is a diagnosis of exclusion based primarily on a thorough history and judicious use of blood tests, hepatobiliary imaging, and liver biopsy [<a href="#cit0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0005">5</a>]. Currently, the mainstay of treatment for DILI is withdrawal of the offending drugs as early as possible [<a href="#cit0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0006">6</a>]. In addition to the only approved N-acetylcysteine in treatment of APAP-hepatoxicity at early-stage in adults, no definitive therapy is available for a general use in DILI with acute liver failure [<a href="#cit0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0007">7</a>].</p>
<p>Mitochondria are a hub for biosynthetic processes in hepatocytes and play a central role in energy metabolism and stress responses. Notably, altered mitochondrial functions or damaged mitochondria have been documented in multiple DILIs [<a href="#cit0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0008">8–11</a>], and mitochondria-targeted probe is even designed for visual precise diagnose is of APAP-induced DILI [<a href="#cit0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0012">12</a>], indicating an extensive and core role of mitochondria in DILI. Nevertheless, it is still not known how the disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis contributes to the onset and perpetuation of DILI.</p>
<p>Mitophagy, the primary quality control mechanism in mitochondrial maintenance, is an evolutionarily conserved self-degradative process that orchestrates the lysosomal degradation of damaged mitochondria in response to cellular stress [<a href="#cit0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0013">13–15</a>]. As expected, in the necrotic liver areas caused by hepatoxic drugs including APAP, efavirenz, and isoniazid (INH), mitophagy induction was observed to act as a scavenger as indicated by increased mitophagosomes [<a href="#cit0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0008">8</a>,<a href="#cit0016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0016">16</a>,<a href="#cit0017" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0017">17</a>]. Although impaired mitophagy by knockout of PINK1 (PTEN induced kinase 1)-PRKN (parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase) aggravates APAP-mediated liver injury [<a href="#cit0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0018">18</a>], it is not determined if inducing mitophagy can preserve mitochondrial homeostasis and then confront different DILIs.</p>
<p>OPTN (optineurin), which was first designated as an “optic neuropathy inducing” protein [<a href="#cit0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0019">19</a>,<a href="#cit0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0020">20</a>], receives considerable attention owing to its multiple roles in the autophagic process, including cargo recognition, autophagosome formation and maturation, and lysosomal quality control [<a href="#cit0021" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0021">21–24</a>]. In this study, by applying <em>optn</em> knockout (<em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup>) mice, we demonstrated that <em>Optn</em> deficiency causes mitophagy defects and impaired mitochondrial homeostasis, thus aggravating APAP-induced liver injury (AILI). On the contrary, OPTN overexpression is able to protect against AILI via inducing mitophagy. Interestingly, OPTN not only selectively recruits GCDH (glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase)-mediated damaged mitochondria for mitophagic degradation in a PINK1-PRKN-dependent way, but also interacts with VCP (valosin containing protein) to promote BECN1 stabilization during phagophore formation, thus initiating mitophagy to preserve mitochondrial homeostasis to alleviate AILI. Further, the accumulated expression of OPTN is validated to have a beneficial role in different DILIs, and pyridoxine is screened to induce OPTN-mediated mitophagy in hepatocytes and then alleviate DILIs, revealing a potential therapeutic strategy for DILIs via inducing OPTN-mediated mitophagy.</p></section><section id="s0002"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="s0002-s2001"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis in various DILIs</h3>
<p>To estimate the mitochondrial function in different DILIs, we collected the transcriptome data of human hepatocytes or mouse livers treated with different toxic drugs, like APAP, monocrotaline, INH, idelalisib, amiodarone, and diclofenac [<a href="#cit0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0025">25–28</a>]. Gene set variation analysis showed that the scores of mitochondrial energy metabolism and mitochondrial biogenesis were obviously reduced in DILIs (<a href="#f0001" class="usa-link">Figure 1A,B</a>), while the scores of mitochondrial fissions, mitophagy, mitochondrial depolarization and mitochondrial superoxide generation were significantly increased in DILIs (<a href="#f0001" class="usa-link">Figure 1C–F</a>). Consistently, the expression of the mitochondrial respiratory chain and mitochondrial biogenesis-related genes, such as <em>RPUSD3</em>, <em>NDUFAB1</em>, <em>TMEM11</em>, and <em>PPARGC1B</em>, were decreased in DILIs, while the expression of mitophagy, mitochondrial depolarization, mitochondrial superoxide generation and mitochondrial fission related genes, such as <em>SNCA</em>, <em>CALR</em>, <em>BCL2</em>, <em>BNIP3</em>, <em>FZD9</em>, <em>IER3</em>, <em>PPP2CB</em>, <em>PGAM5</em> and <em>RALBP1</em> were increased in DILIs (<a href="#f0001" class="usa-link">Figure 1G</a>). Taken together, these data indicated impaired mitochondrial homeostasis in DILIs (<a href="#f0001" class="usa-link">Figure 1H</a>), in contrast to the unchanged mitochondrial functions in chronic liver diseases like liver steatosis, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (Figure S1).

<p>In order to study the manipulation of mitochondrial homeostasis in DILI livers (<a href="#f0001" class="usa-link">Figure 1H</a>), OPTN-mediated mitophagy was taken into consideration because of the important role of OPTN as a mitophagy regulator [<a href="#cit0029" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0029">29–31</a>]. Given the significant upregulated expression of OPTN in hepatocytes of AILI, a representative model of DILI (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2A,B</a>), we intraperitoneally injected a single toxic dose of APAP (500 mg/kg) into wild-type (WT) and <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup> mice to explore the role of OPTN in mitochondrial homeostasis upon AILI (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2C</a>). Results showed that the deceased expression of MAP1LC3/LC3 (microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3)-II was observed in <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup>-AILI livers, as well as the impaired mitochondrial degradation as shown by the accumulated expression of the mitochondrial outer-membrane protein TOMM20, the mitochondrial inner-membrane proteins TIMM23 (translocase of inner mitochondrial membrane 23) and MT-CO2/COX-II (mitochondrially encoded cytochrome c oxidase II), and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA; the ratio of <em>mt-Atp6/ATPase6:Rpl13</em>) in <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup>-AILI livers (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2D–F</a>). Further, electron microscope data showed the mitochondria in <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup>-AILI livers were vacuolated and swollen without being enveloped with the autophagic membrane, revealing the decreased mitophagosomes in <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup>-AILI livers compared with WT-AILI livers (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2G</a>). These data suggested the obvious mitophagy defects in <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup>-AILI livers.

<p>In addition to those patients taking overdosed APAP, AILI with therapeutic doses of APAP also occurs because of fasting, chronic excessive drinking or genetic factors [<a href="#cit0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0032">32–35</a>]. In this case, WT and <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup> mice were intraperitoneally injected with a single nontoxic dose of APAP (150 mg/kg), followed by biochemical and histological evaluation of liver injury at indicated time points (Figure S2A). Although no obvious hepatotoxicity was found in WT mice, dramatically raised GPT/ALT and GOT1/AST levels, increased hepatic necrosis, and upregulated expression of inflammatory genes like <em>Tnf/Tnf-a</em>, <em>Il6</em>, and <em>Cd86</em> were shown in <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup> mice (Figure S2B-D). Collectively, these data indicated that mice with <em>optn</em> inactivation are more susceptible to AILI, and <em>optn</em> variation may become a risk factor for APAP treatment.</p></section><section id="s0002-s2003"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Severe mitochondrial homeostasis disruption in optn-deficient AILI</h3>
<p>Then, liver tissues were collected from WT and <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup> mice treated with APAP (500 mg/kg) and submitted to transcriptome profiling. Genes significantly upregulated and downregulated (fold change &gt; 1.5, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) in <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup>-AILI livers compared with WT-AILI livers were identified (<a href="#f0003" class="usa-link">Figure 3A</a>). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis results indicated that the significantly upregulated genes in <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup>-AILI livers were enriched in cell death regulation and immune regulation (<a href="#f0003" class="usa-link">Figure 3B</a>). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) also revealed that the genes upregulated in <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup>-AILI livers were enriched in gene signatures associated with necrotic cell death and immune response (<a href="#f0003" class="usa-link">Figure 3C</a>). Moreover, the expression of cell death-related genes such as <em>Asah1</em> and <em>Ripk3</em> and immune response-related genes such as <em>Ccr2</em> and <em>Tnf</em> were increased in <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup>-AILI livers compared with WT-AILI livers (<a href="#f0003" class="usa-link">Figure 3D</a>). qRT-PCR analyses further confirmed the upregulation for these genes evaluated (<a href="#f0003" class="usa-link">Figure 3E</a>). Taken together, these data were in line with the aggravated liver injury phenotype in <em>optn</em><sup><em>-/-</em></sup> mice upon APAP treatment.

Thus, these data indicated that <em>optn</em> deficiency aggravates mitochondrial dysfunction in hepatocytes after APAP treatment.</p></section><section id="s0002-s2004"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Mitophagy induction by OPTN overexpression protects against AILI</h3>
<p>Because of the critical role of mitophagy in AILI [<a href="#cit0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0018">18</a>], we wonder if OPTN overexpression could increase the mitophagic flux and then result in the protection of AILI. An increased protein level of OPTN and increased OPTN foci colocalized with LC3 fluorescent puncta indicated OPTN was distributed in autophagosomes after APAP treatment in L02 cells (<a href="#f0004" class="usa-link">Figure 4A,B</a>). By applying mCherry-GFP-LC3 autophagy reporter, we observed the accumulated autophagosomes and autolysosomes upon OPTN overexpression (<a href="#f0004" class="usa-link">Figure 4C,D</a>). Further, by utilizing mitoQC, a tandem GFP-mCherry-tagged mitochondria-targeting probe, we also found a significant increased number of mitolysosomes in OPTN overexpressed cells than control (<a href="#f0004" class="usa-link">Figure 4E</a>), indicating that OPTN-mediated mitophagy was induced in hepatocytes. In addition, an obvious increase of mitochondrial membrane potential and decrease of mitochondrial ROS production were observed in OPTN overexpressed hepatocytes (<a href="#f0003" class="usa-link">Figure 3I,J</a>), showing a restoration of the mitochondrial function with OPTN overexpression.

<p>Then, to investigate the underlying mechanism for the increased OPTN levels in DILI (<a href="#f0007" class="usa-link">Figure 7F</a>), L02 cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX) to block the <em>de novo</em> protein synthesis. Taken together, these data suggested that pyridoxine protects against DILI via inducing OPTN-mediated mitophagy.</p></section></section><section id="s0003"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p>DILI is a serious public health issue worldwide, hepatic mitophagy is induced and functions in response to various DILIs [<a href="#cit0044" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0044">44–47</a>], indicating manipulating mitophagy and maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis becomes a novel approach to alleviate liver injuries. In this study, we showed that mitochondrial dysfunction occurs in various DILIs, and hepatocytes with OPTN absence undergo mitophagy defects and mitochondrial disruption aggravate liver necrosis in AILI, while increased expression of OPTN induces mitophagy to confront AILI. A dual role of OPTN were established to initiate mitophagy to clear damaged mitochondria to alleviate AILI. On the one hand, APAP drives OPTN to recruit damaged mitochondria by recognizing GCDH, a new mitophagic substrate, in a PINK1-PRKN dependent way. On the other hand, OPTN interacts with VCP to stabilize BECN1 during the phagophore assembly. Finally, the accumulation of OPTN in different DILIs is further validated with a protective effect, and pyridoxine is screened to alleviate DILIs via inducing OPTN-mediated mitophagy (Figure S3). Thus, these findings revealed novel roles of OPTN in mitophagy initiation and DILI prevention, having implications on future DILI drug development.</p>
<section id="s0003-s2001"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">The clinical implication of OPTN’s regulation in DILI</h3>
<p>APAP is one of the most commonly used antipyretic analgesics as well as the leading cause of liver failure in the world [<a href="#cit0048" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0048">48</a>]. The threshold doses of APAP causing liver injury varies between individuals, and the risk factors include genetic variability, fasting, and excessive drinking [<a href="#cit0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0033">33–35</a>]. Notably, in addition to AILI with overdosed APAP, AILI with therapeutic doses of APAP is recently characterized in a prospective cohort study [<a href="#cit0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0032">32</a>]. And little is known about the nontoxic doses of APAP-induced AILI till now. In the present study, we found that <em>optn</em> knockout not only aggravated overdosed APAP-induced AILI but also resulted in obvious liver injury upon nontoxic doses of APAP characterized by the raised serum GPT/ALT and GOT1/AST levels as well as the increased necrotic areas in livers (Figure S2), indicating OPTN plays a critical role in different doses of APAP-induced DILI. Moreover, <em>optn</em> knockout also aggravated other DILI models induced by TSN or INH (<a href="#f0007" class="usa-link">Figure 7C–E</a>), implying OPTN-mediated mitophagy is a potential omnipotent regulator in DILI.</p>
<p>The clinical relevance of OPTN has been indicated by the identification of human genetic variants in different diseases, including glaucoma disease, Paget disease of bone, and ALS [<a href="#cit0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0019">19</a>,<a href="#cit0049" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0049">49</a>,<a href="#cit0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0050">50</a>]. OPTN<sup>E478G</sup>, one of the loss-of-function variants identified in ALS, showed no protective role against APAP with toxic dose (<a href="#f0004" class="usa-link">Figure 4F–I</a>), suggesting that OPTN<sup>E478G</sup> mutation is more sensitive to APAP-induced liver injury. Although we cannot find any relevant clinical case reports now, we still hold the view that patients with OPTN<sup>E478G</sup> mutation should be particularly careful when they take APAP even at therapeutic doses. Notably, we noticed that Riluzole, the only drug approved by FDA (Food and Drug Administration) for ALS, is associated with a high incidence of liver failure [<a href="#cit0051" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0051">51</a>]. Given the general protective role of OPTN in DILI, ALS patients with OPTN<sup>E478G</sup> mutation or deletion mutation should be taken special attention and monitored their liver function when they are taking Riluzole for ALS treatment.</p></section><section id="s0003-s2002"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">OPTN functions as a mitophagy receptor in hepatocytes</h3>
<p>OPTN is considered as one of the autophagy receptors to recognize and recruit the substrates to autophagic degradation [<a href="#cit0052" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0052">52</a>]. Recent studies revealed that the substrates of OPTN-mediated autophagic degradation vary in different cells and conditions. For example, OPTN physically targets GSK3B for autophagic degradation in mouse myoblasts during myogenesis [<a href="#cit0053" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0053">53</a>]. FABP3 is the target of OPTN to regulate cell fate and bone-fat balance in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells [<a href="#cit0054" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0054">54</a>]. KDM4D is the substrate of OPTN to epigenetically regulate inflammation in bone marrow derived dendritic cells [<a href="#cit0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0055">55</a>]. However, little is known about the targets of OPTN in mitophagy. In this study, we identified GCDH as a new mitophagy substrate of OPTN in APAP-treated hepatocytes by a mass spectrometry analysis (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Figure 5</a>). It is not determined that whether GCDH is a functional mitochondrial protein for hepatocyte survival or just an accessory protein to connect damaged mitochondria with OPTN, and further studies of GCDH function are needed.</p>
<p>In mitophagy, the mitochondrial outer-membrane proteins are always considered as the ubiquitinated substrates, such as ATAD1, TBCD15, and VDAC [<a href="#cit0056" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0056">56</a>]. Interestingly, GCDH, the new substrate identified here, is a mitochondrial enzyme and is mainly located in the mitochondrial matrix [<a href="#cit0057" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0057">57</a>]. And the interaction of OPTN and GCDH only existed in hepatocytes after APAP treatment and dismissed in OPTN<sup>E478G</sup> or <em>PINK1-PRKN</em> knockdown cells (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Figure 5E,N</a>). It makes sense that mitochondria are depolarized and ruptured caused by APAP [<a href="#cit0058" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0058">58</a>], so GCDH is able to be exposed in the cytosol to be ubiquitinated and then recognized by OPTN. We think that GCDH is a specific mitophagy substrate of OPTN in injured hepatocytes. Furthermore, like the mitochondrial inner-membrane proteins TIMM23 and MT-CO2/COX-II, GCDH can also be a mitophagy indicator because its location is in the mitochondrial matrix.</p>
<p>OPTN-mediated autophagy relies on the ubiquitin-binding domain to recognize and binds the ubiquitin signal of the substrate to the autophagosome [<a href="#cit0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0031">31</a>]. Our results showed that OPTN overexpression indeed degraded the GCDH expression after APAP treatment, while overexpression of OPTN<sup>E478G</sup>, a mutation in the ubiquitin-binding domain, failed to decrease the GCDH levels (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Figure 5H,I</a>). It is reasonable that OPTN<sup>E478G</sup> has a defect in recruiting the substrate, like GCDH in this study, for autophagic degradation. In addition, it is reported that OPTN<sup>E478G</sup> can interact with OPTN and form OPTN-OPTN<sup>E478G</sup> hybrid complexes to repress the function of OPTN in the clearance of inclusion bodies [<a href="#cit0037" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0037">37</a>]. Thus, it is also possible that the overexpressed OPTN<sup>E478G</sup> alternatively blocks the function of endogenous OPTN to promote mitophagic degradation of GCDH.</p>
<p>Though OPTN and CALCOCO2/NDP52 function redundantly as mitophagy receptors, the expression of these proteins in different tissues are not the same. Specifically, CALCOCO2 shows much lower expression than OPTN in livers [<a href="#cit0059" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0059">59</a>], indicating the potential role of OPTN rather than CALCOCO2 in liver homeostasis. Furthermore, OPTN knockdown is reported to have the most significant inhibitory effects on embryonic stem cells reprograming among inhibition of mitophagy receptors including CALCOCO2 [<a href="#cit0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0060">60</a>], suggesting a more severe phenotype caused by <em>optn</em> knockout than <em>calcoco2</em> knockout, and it is probably because OPTN acts as not only an autophagy regulator, but also an immune regulator or a vesicular trafficking regulator. Altogether, we can infer that it is <em>optn</em> knockout rather than <em>calcoco2</em> knockout causes a severe defect in livers. In addition to functioning in mitophagy, OPTN also participates in immune signaling like NFKB/NF-κB signaling. The upregulation of inflammatory markers after <em>optn</em> knockout (<a href="#f0002" class="usa-link">Figure 2K</a> and S2D) can be a secondary effect of aggravated AILI by <em>optn</em><sup>−/−</sup>-mediated mitophagy defects, or a direct effect of NFKB activation by <em>optn</em> knockout. Though we cannot separate the role of OPTN in mitophagy and immune signaling, our study demonstrated OPTN-induced mitophagy at least partially functions in AILI prevention.</p></section><section id="s0003-s2003"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">An unforeseen function of OPTN’s interaction with VCP in phagophore assembly and mitophagy initiation</h3>
<p>In addition to functioning as an autophagy receptor, OPTN is also shown to regulate autophagosome formation and maturation in recent studies [<a href="#cit0023" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0023">23</a>,<a href="#cit0061" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0061">61</a>]. In detail, OPTN promotes autophagosome formation by recruiting the autophagy-related ATG12–ATG5-ATG16L1 complex to phagophores and thus influence LC3-II production in mouse embryonic fibroblast with nonselective autophagy [<a href="#cit0023" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0023">23</a>]. However, <em>OPTN</em> knockout does not affect the autophagosome formation as indicated by the unchanged LC3-II expression in HeLa cells with antimycin A + oligomycin A-induced mitophagy [<a href="#cit0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0030">30</a>]. Thus, we can conclude that the precise mechanism of OPTN regulation in autophagosome formation varies in different cells. Interestingly, in APAP-treated hepatocytes, we demonstrated a tight interaction between OPTN and VCP during phagophore assembly, a step before autophagosome formation (<a href="#f0006" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a>). What is more, this interaction can also be observed in control hepatocytes, indicating OPTN-VCP interaction is not only involved in APAP-stimulated mitophagy but also involved in the basal autophagy in hepatocytes.</p></section><section id="s0003-s2004"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Targeting OPTN-mediated mitophagy in DILI</h3>
<p>Though mitophagy is always a hotspot in liver disease-related studies, how to manipulate mitophagy to treat diseases is not well characterized [<a href="#cit0062" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0062">62</a>]. Multiple compounds, like STF-083010, ginsenoside Rg1, and puerarin, are able to induce autophagy to fight against DILI [<a href="#cit0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0045">45</a>,<a href="#cit0063" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0063">63</a>,<a href="#cit0064" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0064">64</a>]. Actually, autophagy always acts partially without any specific target in the function of these compounds. Here we found decreased OPTN degradation by the ubiquitin proteasome system in DILI, and OPTN accumulation prevented against DILI (<a href="#f0007" class="usa-link">Figure 7</a>), suggesting OPTN is a potential drug target in DILI treatment. It is better to identify the specific regulator of OPTN ubiquitination and then generate a drug screen model. Alternatively, we screened the expression of OPTN in cells after different compounds treatment. Interestingly, pyridoxine is found as the top one to upregulate OPTN expression, and pyridoxine protects against DILI because of the induced OPTN-mediated mitophagy.</p>
<p>Pyridoxine, namely vitamin B6, is one of the main dietary forms of vitamins involved in developmental, metabolic, and physiological processes [<a href="#cit0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0065">65</a>]. Marginal pyridoxine deficiency is associated with the dysfunctions of the nervous, immune, and endocrine systems [<a href="#cit0066" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0066">66</a>]. Dietary pyridoxine has been found to have a potential hepatoprotective role against APAP-induced hepatotoxicity due to its antioxidant activity [<a href="#cit0067" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0067">67</a>]. Our study here not only indicated its protective role in different DILIs by inducing mitophagy but also pointed out that OPTN can be a promising target of pyridoxine (<a href="#f0008" class="usa-link">Figure 8</a>). What is more, OPTN-mediated mitophagy deficiency resulted in mitochondrial dysfunction as well as oxidative stress in hepatocytes (<a href="#f0003" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>), suggesting pyridoxine can induce OPTN-mediated mitophagy and then reduce oxidative stress. Interestingly, pyridoxine is the preferred antidote for severe standard isoniazid poisoning clinically and could improve liver injury in some clinical cases [<a href="#cit0042" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0042">42</a>,<a href="#cit0043" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0043">43</a>]. More studies are needed on pyridoxine as an omnipotent drug for different DILIs.</p></section></section><section id="s0004"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="s0004-s2001"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Animals</h3>
<p>C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Weitong Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology CO., Ltd. <em>optn</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice were gifted by Prof. Ronggui Hu (University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai 200,031, China). The animals were supplied with a standard laboratory diet and water ad libitum at a temperature of 22 ± 1°C with a 12 h light-dark cycle and 65 ± 5% humidity. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Zhejiang University (IACUC-s22–008).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2002"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">DILI mouse models</h3>
<p>APAP-induced DILI mouse model was established as follows: After fasting overnight, the male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old) were treated with 150–750 mg/kg APAP (Aladdin, A105808) by intraperitoneal injection to get the DILI mice model. To overexpression hepatic OPTN or OPTN<sup>E478G</sup>
<em>in vivo</em>, WT or <em>optn</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice were injected tail intravenously with lentiviruses carrying pCDH-OPTN or pCDH-OPTN<sup>E478G</sup> or the pCDH-vector at 0.5 × 10<sup>8</sup> transducing units/mouse for 5 days, then treated with APAP and sacrificed at indicated time-point. Serum GPT/ALT and GOT1/AST levels were measured by a fully automatic blood biochemical analyzer (Roche, Cobas c311, Switzerland). The mice were treated with 750 mg/kg APAP to record the survival rate of mice.</p>
<p>TSN-induced DILI mouse model was established as follows: WT or <em>optn</em><sup>−/−</sup> male mice (8–10 weeks old) were treated with 10–15 mg/kg TSN (TAUTO BIOTECH, E-0634) by intraperitoneal injection for 24 h before all mice were sacrificed.</p>
<p>INH-induced DILI mouse model was established as follows: WT or <em>optn</em><sup>−/−</sup> male mice (8–10 weeks old) were intragastric (i.g.) administrated with 150–200 mg/kg INH (Aladdin, I104692) or vehicle (normal saline) for 6 days before all mice were sacrificed.</p>
<p>To investigate the protective effect of pyridoxine on DILI, 20 mg/kg pyridoxine (TargetMol, T0973) was injected intraperitoneally with hepatotoxic drugs simultaneously.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2003"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Hematoxylin and eosin (H&amp;E) staining</h3>
<p>Liver tissues from the mice were fixed in 10% formalin for at least 24 h followed by paraffin embedding. Sections (3 μm) of paraffin-embedded liver tissues were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Necrotic areas indicated by H&amp;E staining were quantified by ImageJ software.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2004"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunostaining analysis</h3>

<p>Total RNA was isolated from liver tissues or L02 cells after grinding in TRIzol reagent (Takara, 9109) following the manufacturer’s instruction. <p>JC-1 (mitochondrial membrane potential probe; Sigma-Aldrich, T4069) staining assays were performed to detect the cell MMP. JC-1 existed either as a cytoplasmic JC-1 monomer or in mitochondrial J-aggregates, depending on the MMP. In healthy cells with high MMP, JC-1 spontaneously forms J-aggregates that emit red fluorescence in the mitochondria. However, in unhealthy cells, the MMP declines, and JC-1 is released from the mitochondria and exists as a monomer with green fluorescence in the cytoplasm. Thus, the MMP was indicated by the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity. After treatment with APAP for the indicated times and concentrations, cells were collected by trypsinization and subsequently stained with 5 μM JC-1 for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. The FACSCalibur cytometer (BD, USA) was used to measure the stained samples, which adjusted a 520-nm filter for the detection of the green fluorescence and 590 nm for the red fluorescence.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2013"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP)</h3>

After incubation with beads for 12 h at 4°C, the beads were washed 8 times and then eluted with sample buffer.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2014"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Proteomics analysis</h3>
<p>L02 cells were transfected with OPTN-HA or Vector and treated with 5 mM APAP for 6 h and then immunoprecipitation with HA beads. The lysates of HA beads after immunoprecipitation were lysed by lysis buffer (8 M urea [Sigma-Aldrich, V900119-500 G], 1% protease inhibitor cocktail [Merck Millipore 539,134-10 ML]) and then subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis. LC-MS/MS analysis and data processing services were undertaken by PTM Biotech.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2015"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">MitoSOX staining</h3>
<p>The L02 cells were stained with MitoSOX red mitochondrial superoxide indicator (Yeasen Biotech, 40778ES50) according to the product instruction. Briefly, the MitoSOX was dissolved in DMSO to reach a working solution of 5 mM. Next, the cells were incubated with MitoSOX for 10 min at 37°C in the dark. After washing with PBS three times, the cells were counterstained with Hoechst (Solarbio 33,343) for 10 min in a 37°C incubator. At last, stained cells were rinsed three times with PBS and then covered in warm PBS for imaging (Leica TCS-SP8, Germany).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2016"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Autophagic flux assay</h3>
<p>Cells cultured in glass-bottom cell culture dishes (Nest 801,001) were transfected with mCherry-GFP-LC3 for 24 h. After treatment with 5 mM APAP for 6 h, cells were covered in warm PBS for imaging (Leica TCS-SP8, Germany). Before being fused with the lysosome, the autophagosome can be detected as GFP (green) and mCherry (red) puncta (showed as a yellow signal after the merge). As the GFP signal is quenched in the acidic pH of autolysosomes, so once the mCherry-GFP-LC3 turns into autolysosomes, only the red signal can be detected. Similarly, under normal conditions, mitochondria fluoresce red and green when transfected with mitoQC, a tandem GFP-mCherry-tagged mitochondria-targeting probe. Upon mitophagy, mitochondria are delivered to lysosomes where the low pH quenches the GFP signal but not mCherry. The result is that a portion of mitochondria form punctate structures and fluoresce red only, with the degree of mitophagy calculated by their increase.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2017"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">GST affinity-isolation assay</h3>
<p>Recombinant protein of GST, GST-OPTN and VCP-His were expressed in <em>E. coli</em> BL21 (DE3) strain (TransGen Biotech, CD601–02), induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl-1-thio-β-d-galactopyranoside (Invitrogen 15,529,019) for 18 h at 16°C and purified following the protocol from Beyotime Biotechnology (GST-tag Protein Purification Kit, P2262; His-Tag Protein Purification Kit, P2245S). GST (150 µg) or GST-OPTN proteins were incubated with GST Mag-Beads by rocking at room temperature for 30 minutes, and the beads were then washed three times with 1 × PBS. 150 μg of VCP were added to the beads and incubated by rocking at 4°C overnight. The beads were washed eight times with 1 × PBS, and then dissolved into 1.5× SDS loading buffer and boiled 10 min at 95°C.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2018"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Microscale thermophoresis (MST) binding assay</h3>
<p>The MST binding assay using NanoTemper monolith was described previously [<a href="#cit0068" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cit0068">68</a>]. GST, GST-OPTN and VCP-6×His was purified and dialyzed. When purified VCP-6×His was used as target, protein was labeled with RED-Tris-NTA Protein Labeling Kit (MO-L018, NanoTemper Technologies). Seventy-five nM labeled VCP and 4.25 µM to 0.1297 nM GST or GST-OPTN was mixed in PBS-T (PBS with 0.05% Tween 20). The sample was loaded into the premium capillaries (NanoTemper Technologies, MO-K025), and the MST measurements were performed at 25°C, 100% excitation nano-red and medium MST-power. Kd values were calculated using the mass action equation available in MO.Affinity Analysis software.</p></section><section id="s0004-s2019"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Protein expression-based compound screening assay</h3>

<p>LysoTracker Red DND-99 (Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific, L7528) was used following the manufacturer’s instructions to assay lysosomal number and diameter. In briefly, remove the medium of L02 cells from the dish and add the prewarmed (37°C) probe-containing medium and then incubate the cells for 30 min under growth conditions. Then replace the solution with fresh medium for confocal imaging (Leica TCS-SP8, Germany).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2021"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Evaluations of livers sections from DILI patients</h3>

<p>Total RNA was isolated and purified from WT and <em>optn</em><sup>−/−</sup> livers following the manufacturer’s procedure. The RNA amount and purity were quantified using NanoDrop ND-1000 (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE, USA) and then submitted for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). The average insert size for the final cDNA library was 300 ± 50 bp and 2 × 150bp paired-end sequencing (PE150) on an Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 (LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) were performed following the vendor’s recommended protocol.</p>
<p>StringTie was used to perform expression levels for mRNAs by calculating FPKM. The differentially expressed mRNAs were selected with fold change &gt; 1.5 or fold change &lt; 0.67 and with parametric F-test comparing nested linear models (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) by R package edgeR (<a href="https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/edgeR.html</a>). The scatter diagram, volcano plot, Venn diagram, and heatmap showing differential expression genes were plotted according to <a href="https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool</a>. Gene ontology analysis of gene expression changes was performed using GSEA (<a href="http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/search.jsp" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/search.jsp</a>). The Dot plot of the GSEA summary was performed by R (v4.0.4) (<a href="https://www.r-project.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.r-project.org/</a>). The GO summary plots showing upregulated and downregulated pathways were plotted according to <a href="https://toppgene.cchmc.org/enrichment.jsp" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://toppgene.cchmc.org/enrichment.jsp</a>. Gene set variation analysis analysis was performed by “gene set variation analysis (v1.48.1)” (R package).</p></section><section id="s0004-s2023"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Quantification and statistical analysis</h3>
<p>All quantifications were done using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (San Diego, California, <a href="http://www.graphpad.com" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">www.graphpad.com</a>).
