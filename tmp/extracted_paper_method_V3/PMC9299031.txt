</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Intersystem crossing to the long‐lived metastable triplet state is often a strong limitation on fluorescence brightness of single molecules, particularly for perylene in various matrices. In this paper, we report on a strong excitation‐induced reverse intersystem crossing (rISC), a process where single perylene molecules in a dibenzothiophene matrix recover faster from the triplet state, turning into bright emitters at saturated excitation powers. With a detailed study of single‐molecule fluorescence autocorrelations, we quantify the effect of rISC. The intrinsic lifetimes found for the two effective triplet states (8.5±0.4 ms and 64±12 ms) become significantly shorter, into the sub‐millisecond range, as the excitation power increases and fluorescence brightness is ultimately enhanced at least fourfold. Our results are relevant for the understanding of triplet state manipulation of single‐molecule quantum emitters and for markedly improving their brightness.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> single-molecule spectroscopy, triplet states, reverse intersystem crossing, fluorescence blinking, intrinsic lifetimes</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><hr class="headless">
<p>
<strong>Enhanced brightness of single molecules</strong>: Single perylene molecules immobilized in a dibenzothiophene host crystal give rise to narrow optical resonances below 4 K. The single emitters show clear blinking due to long‐lived triplet states. However, the blinking times shorten considerably as the probe laser's intensity increases. The observations are explained by a power‐dependent depopulation of the triplet states via reverse intersystem crossing (rISC), which significantly improves the brightness of the molecules at saturated excitation intensities.</p>
<section class="bt xbox font-sm" id="boxed-text1"><p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/a2f8/9299031/f705b5172c2d/CPHC-23-0-g004.jpg" loading="lazy" id="jats-graphic-1" height="582" width="639" alt="graphic file with name CPHC-23-0-g004.jpg"></p></section></section><section id="cphc202100679-sec-0001"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>Triplet states are usually a bottleneck for single‐molecule fluorescence spectroscopy (SMS) at cryogenic temperatures. As the single molecule enters the triplet state, its fluorescence is cut off for the time that it remains in this metastable dark state.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0001">
<sup>[1]</sup>
</a> For some fluorophores, such as perylene, this time is relatively long – in the range of milliseconds.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0002">
<sup>[2]</sup>
</a> The result is a relatively poor brightness of single perylene molecules and this is the main reason that their study in SMS has been mostly overtaken by bright and stable emitters such as dibenzoterrylene, known to have a very low triplet yield and a triplet lifetime on the order of tens of microseconds.[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0003">
<sup>3</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0004">
<sup>4</sup>
</a>] Nevertheless, the fluorescence intermittency originating from the existence of triplet dark states can be exploited in cryogenic super‐resolution localization schemes based on stochastic blinking of single emitters.[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0005">
<sup>5</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0006">
<sup>6</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0007">
<sup>7</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0008">
<sup>8</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0009">
<sup>9</sup>
</a>] One of the applications of single molecules as bright emitters is as very sensitive probes of their local environment by means of perturbations on their optical transitions. The ideal case of an uninterrupted stream of photons, caused by a very low triplet yield and/or a very short triplet lifetime, would act as a stable information source for probing local effects. Some of these proven concepts of single molecules as probes are high sensitivity to electric fields and charges by an electrically‐ or optically‐induced Stark effect,[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0010">
<sup>10</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0011">
<sup>11</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0012">
<sup>12</sup>
</a>] or single molecules as nano‐microphones of mechanically induced strain.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0013">
<sup>[13]</sup>
</a> Recently, single‐molecule emitters have also received significant attention as stable and tunable single‐photon sources and non‐linear elements in integrated photonic devices.[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0014">
<sup>14</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0015">
<sup>15</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0016">
<sup>16</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0017" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0017">
<sup>17</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0018">
<sup>18</sup>
</a>]</p>
<p>However, single molecules are not only studied for their emissive properties originating from the singlet transition. Unlike the singlet, the triplet state has a non‐zero spin and therefore makes experiments involving static and variable magnetic fields possible. Such are the optically detected magnetic resonance (ODMR) experiments on pentacene in p‐terphenyl, which showed that the transitions between triplet sublevels can be induced by microwaves that match the (zero‐field) splitting in energy.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0019">
<sup>[19]</sup>
</a> However, one has to rely on the stochastic nature of ISC transitions to the triplet state before the spin state can be manipulated. Optical control of the switching between a single molecule's singlet ground state and triplet excited state remains to be demonstrated.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0020">
<sup>[20]</sup>
</a> Perylene, an interesting candidate for such an experiment, has both its singlet and triplet transitions conveniently placed in wavelength ranges accessible by the well‐established Ti:sapphire laser, directly for the triplet transition, and through the second harmonic of another Ti:sapphire laser in the blue region<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0021" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0021">
<sup>[21]</sup>
</a> for the singlet. However, for a direct optical excitation of the molecule to the triplet state, the corresponding wavelength of the <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="jats-math-1"><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mn>0</mn></msub><mo>→</mo><msub><mi>T</mi><mn>1</mn></msub></mrow></math></span>
transition needs to be found first. So far, the 0–0 zero‐phonon line (ZPL) for the phosphorescence of perylene‐doped matrices has been detected solely for perylene in anthracene<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0022">
<sup>[22]</sup>
</a> and was determined to be around 12844±1 cm<sup>−1</sup>, blue‐shifted by 472 cm<sup>−1</sup> compared to the origin of phosphorescence found for a pure perylene crystal.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0023" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0023">
<sup>[23]</sup>
</a> Unfortunately, fluorescence from single perylene molecules in anthracene could not be detected, probably because of intermolecular ISC.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0024" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0024">
<sup>[24]</sup>
</a> For future studies on the phosphorescence of perylene in the dibenzothiophene matrix, we chose to study deuterated perylene in this work, instead of the commonly employed hydrogenated perylene. For the deuterated analogue of perylene, it is expected that non‐radiative decay from the triplet state to the singlet ground state will be reduced, enhancing the phosphorescence quantum yield.[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0025">
<sup>25</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0026">
<sup>26</sup>
</a>]</p>
<p>Over the years, the spectroscopy of single perylene molecules has been studied in the polymer polyethylene,<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0027">
<sup>[27]</sup>
</a> the Shpol'skii matrix <em>n</em>‐nonane<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0028">
<sup>[28]</sup>
</a> and organic crystalline hosts such as biphenyl<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0022">
<sup>[22]</sup>
</a> and ortho‐dichlorobenzene (<em>o</em>‐DCB).<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0002">
<sup>[2]</sup>
</a> In almost all of the studied hosts, single‐molecule emission of perylene was accompanied with spectral diffusion,<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0022">
<sup>[22]</sup>
</a> broadened linewidths,<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0022">
<sup>[22]</sup>
</a> or low fluorescence rate due to long dark times.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0002">
<sup>[2]</sup>
</a> One way to increase a molecule's fluorescence rate is by recovering an emitter from its dark triplet state by exciting it with visible light of sufficient energy back to its excited singlet state – a phenomenon that is known as reverse intersystem crossing (rISC). Although rISC was first observed in 1969 by Keller,<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0029" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0029">
<sup>[29]</sup>
</a> it remains a scarcely studied effect at low temperature. To our knowledge, rISC has never been observed for lifetime‐limited emitters below 5 K. A typical system that exhibits lifetime‐limited emission below 5 K, namely terrylene in <em>p</em>‐terphenyl,<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0030">
<sup>[30]</sup>
</a> had negligible effects of rISC below 30 K, however rISC became increasingly strong at temperatures above 50 K.[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0031">
<sup>31</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0032">
<sup>32</sup>
</a>] In non‐lifetime‐limited systems, and especially at room temperature, rISC is a much more common effect and was for example achieved by using a secondary laser beam that was red‐shifted with respect to the probe beam to recover a molecule from the dark triplet state and to enhance the fluorescence signal.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0033">
<sup>[33]</sup>
</a> However, this required much higher light intensities (from tens of kWcm<sup>−2</sup> up to several MWcm<sup>−2</sup>, at an increased risk of photobleaching the molecule<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0033">
<sup>[33]</sup>
</a>) than typically used in cryogenic SMS (0.01–10 W cm<sup>−2</sup>).<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0034" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0034">
<sup>[34]</sup>
</a> The rISC mechanism has also been utilized for improving the efficiency of organic light‐emitting diodes.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0035">
<sup>[35]</sup>
</a> While characterizing this new system of perylene‐d12 in dibenzothiophene, we discovered that the brightness of single emitters in this system can be markedly increased and the triplet dark times significantly shortened with increasing intensity of the excitation beam. Importantly, we observed rISC mediated by the same beam that probes a single molecule. In this work, the power dependence of rISC was studied on a single perylene molecule by measuring the autocorrelations of its resonance fluorescence over a broad range of laser excitation intensities. We additionally provide an analytical framework to explain the experimental results by taking into account the process of rISC.</p></section><section id="cphc202100679-sec-0002"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Experimental Section</h2>
<section id="cphc202100679-sec-0003"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Confocal Fluorescence Spectroscopy Setup</h3>
<p>The samples were measured in a liquid helium flow cryostat (Janis, SVT‐200‐5) that can reach a working temperature of 1.3 K. With an objective (0.85 NA, Edmund Optics) immersed in liquid helium, the cryostat forms part of a home‐built confocal microscope. A Ti:sapphire laser (M‐squared SolsTiS) in combination with a frequency‐doubling module (M‐squared ECD‐X) was used as a narrow bandwidth (&lt;1 MHz) tunable excitation source, operating in the 440–460 nm range. The output wavelength of the laser was measured continuously with a wavemeter (High Finesse WS6‐200), with resolution of a few MHz. A dichroic mirror (Semrock, FF458‐Di02‐25x36) was used to separate fluorescence from the excitation light and further filtering was done by a long‐pass filter (Chroma, ET460lp). The laser beam was scanned over the sample by a scanning mirror (Newport, FSM‐300‐01) and fluorescence was detected by an avalanche photodiode (Excelitas, SPCM‐AQRH‐16). Fluorescence spectra were recorded with a Horiba iHR320 spectrometer coupled to a liquid‐nitrogen‐cooled Symphony II CCD detector. The excitation intensity was measured with a power meter (Newport 1830‐C), before the laser beam entered the cryostat.</p></section><section id="cphc202100679-sec-0004"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Preparation of Perylene‐Doped Dibenzothiophene Crystals</h3>
<p>Perylene‐d12 (Sigma‐Aldrich, 98 %) was dissolved in toluene (Acros Organics, 99.85 %) and diluted to concentrations of 10–100 ppm for bulk spectroscopy and 0.1–10 ppb for single‐molecule spectroscopy measurements. The commercially obtained dibenzothiophene (Alfa Aesar, 98 %) was heated above its melting point by a heat gun and a small quantity of the perylene in toluene solution was pipetted into the hot liquid.</p>
<p>For the bulk spectroscopy, square borosilicate capillaries (VitroTubes, 1 mm ID and 0.2 mm walls) were filled with a molten solution of dibenzothiophene doped with perylene‐d12. The capillaries were subsequently quenched in liquid nitrogen to rapidly crystallize and reduce the formation of perylene dimers producing excimer emission. Zone‐refined dibenzothiophene was used for single‐molecule spectroscopy experiments. The hot liquid solution of perylene‐d12 in dibenzothiophene was allowed to crystallize and a few crystal grains were melted and pressed in between two glass slides, yielding a thin layer of organic material. Finally, the crystals were molten again and freeze‐quenched in liquid nitrogen. All preparation steps were performed in normal atmosphere.</p></section></section><section id="cphc202100679-sec-0005"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Results and Discussion</h2>
<section id="cphc202100679-sec-0006"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Line‐Narrowing Spectroscopy</h3>
<p>In this work, we have used for the first time dibenzothiophene (DBThio) as a host matrix for perylene‐d12 (from now on simply referred to as perylene). DBThio is a sulphur‐containing planar molecule forming a monoclinic unit cell with four molecules.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0036" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0036">
<sup>[36]</sup>
</a> The origin of absorption of the singlet state is at 29923 cm<sup>−1</sup> (334.3 nm) and phosphorescence is around 24292 cm<sup>−1</sup> (411.8 nm).<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0037" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0037">
<sup>[37]</sup>
</a> Both the singlet and triplet of DBThio are higher in energy than the excited singlet state of perylene. The higher electronic energy levels of the host prevent intermolecular ISC, which is responsible for efficient quenching of single‐molecule fluorescence of perylene in anthracene<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0022">
<sup>[22]</sup>
</a> and of terrylene in anthracene.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0024" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0024">
<sup>[24]</sup>
</a>
</p>
<p>At a temperature of 1.3 K, the excitation of the strongest vibronic line of perylene in DBThio at 447.2 nm shows line‐narrowing of the 0–0 zero‐phonon line (ZPL) in the bulk spectrum of a polycrystalline sample (Figure <a href="#cphc202100679-fig-0001" class="usa-link">1</a>). The full‐width‐at‐half‐maximum (FWHM) of the inhomogeneous band of the 0–0 ZPL was measured by a narrow‐range excitation spectrum around the strong 344 cm<sup>−1</sup> vibronic line and was established to be 10±1 cm<sup>−1</sup>. The inhomogeneous broadening of the 0–0 ZPL and the vibronic lines in the bulk spectrum are comparable to those of perylene in other matrices.[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0022">
<sup>22</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0027">
<sup>27</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0028">
<sup>28</sup>
</a>] The peak of the 0–0 ZPL lies around 22017±1.3 cm<sup>−1</sup> with no obvious other sites. From the emission spectrum of a single perylene molecule, the Debye‐Waller factor, defined as the ratio of the 0–0 ZPL to the 0–0 phonon sideband, was determined to be approximately 0.40±0.05. The assignments of the specific vibrational modes of perylene‐h12[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0038" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0038">
<sup>38</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0039" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0039">
<sup>39</sup>
</a>] and perylene‐d12 can be found elsewhere.[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0038" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0038">
<sup>38</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0039" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0039">
<sup>39</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0040">
<sup>40</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0041" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0041">
<sup>41</sup>
</a>]
</p>

Single‐Molecule Fluorescence Spectroscopy</h3>
<p>The phonon states in the crystal matrix are drastically depopulated at cryogenic temperatures and single molecules of perylene can be excited individually by their purely electronic transition to the singlet excited state – the 0–0 zero‐phonon line. In order to find relatively isolated molecules in frequency space, the concentration and method of preparation had to be optimized. When the step of fast crystallization due to freeze‐quenching was skipped, the perylene molecules tended to pack at the edges of the crystals and could not be distinguished spectrally (Supporting Information S2). Furthermore, the crystal thickness and concentration of perylene were of influence on the background signal and spectral diffusion. A dopant concentration of approximately 1 ppb was optimal for finding isolated molecular resonances.</p>
<p>The single‐molecule fluorescence measurements we report on will relate to a single molecule (M1) that is representative for the perylene molecules in the DBThio matrix. In Figure <a href="#cphc202100679-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2</a>, single‐scan excitation spectra of perylene molecule M1 are shown at two different excitation intensities. Below the saturation intensity (Figure <a href="#cphc202100679-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2</a>a) the molecule behaves typically as perylene in other systems, showing many events of ISC, which lead to reduced average fluorescence brightness.[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0002">
<sup>2</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0022">
<sup>22</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0027">
<sup>27</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0028">
<sup>28</sup>
</a>] Unique to this studied system is the behaviour at excitation intensities far above the saturation intensity (Figure <a href="#cphc202100679-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2</a>b), where the fluorescence is much brighter and ISC seemingly has a negligible effect on the fluorescence rate. As the integration times are identical for both scans in Figure <a href="#cphc202100679-fig-0002" class="usa-link">2</a>, we conclude that the effective lifetime of the triplet state is significantly shortened at high excitation intensities.

Normally, the fluorescence intensity would saturate in the same sigmoid‐shape as the red curve, but the blue points clearly follow a pattern that diverges from it. At high excitation intensities the average fluorescence intensity tends to converge to the limit where the triplet state plays a negligible role on the fluorescence yield. To explain this deviation from the standard saturation model we propose a four‐level scheme including rISC.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/cphc202100679-tbl-0001/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section><p>The lifetimes of the two triplet states are longer than reported for perylene in <em>o</em>‐DCB<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0002">
<sup>[2]</sup>
</a> (3.0 ms and 19 ms) and <em>n</em>‐nonane<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0028">
<sup>[28]</sup>
</a> (1.1±0.5 ms), which might be related to the deuteration of the perylene.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0026">
<sup>[26]</sup>
</a> In general the triplet lifetimes are reported to be longer for deuterated fluorophores compared to their non‐deuterated analogues.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0026">
<sup>[26]</sup>
</a> For perylene‐d12 dissolved in PMMA, the triplet lifetime was found to increase by a factor of 2.3 (298 K) compared to perylene‐h12.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0026" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0026">
<sup>[26]</sup>
</a> The triplet yield <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="jats-math-115"><mrow><msub><mrow><mi>φ</mi><mrow></mrow></mrow><mi>T</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><msub><mi>τ</mi><mi>F</mi></msub><mfenced separators="" open="(" close=")"><msubsup><mi>k</mi><mrow><mn>23</mn></mrow><mrow><mi>x</mi><mi>y</mi></mrow></msubsup><mo>+</mo><msubsup><mi>k</mi><mrow><mn>23</mn></mrow><mi>z</mi></msubsup></mfenced></mrow></math></span>
was determined to be on the order of 3×10<sup>−6</sup>, which is comparable to the triplet yield found for perylene‐h12 in <em>o</em>‐DCB.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0002">
<sup>[2]</sup>
</a>
</p>
<p>The reason for efficient rISC taking place in this particular matrix, as opposed to other matrices that have been used for perylene, remains unknown. Assuming that the excitation beam excites the triplet state to a higher triplet level there are at least two pathways for the rISC. The most commonly proposed pathway involves an intramolecular intersystem crossing, i. e., a spin‐flip process when the perylene molecule is in a high triplet level.[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0033">
<sup>33</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0035">
<sup>35</sup>
</a>] The high density of vibrational states in both multiplicities makes it easy to achieve resonance between triplet and singlet levels, leading to relaxation to the lower singlet states. The large T<sub>1</sub>‐T<sub>N</sub> energy gap and energetic proximity of T<sub>N</sub> and S<sub>N</sub> states can favour rISC over an internal conversion process from T<sub>N</sub> to T<sub>1</sub>.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0035">
<sup>[35]</sup>
</a> The relatively heavy sulphur atom of the DBThio matrix could further help couple singlet and triplet states, particularly in the more delocalized orbitals of the higher excited states.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0047" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0047">
<sup>[47]</sup>
</a> If this triplet‐to‐singlet transition is fast enough, the rISC rate would be dominated by the optical excitation of the T<sub>xy</sub> and T<sub>z</sub> states, leading to the same rISC rate for all three triplet sublevels. A less‐likely pathway for rISC could involve a Dexter exchange with the host's triplet state, producing a triplet excitation of a neighbor DBThio molecule by Dexter energy transfer. However, this excitation might again relax by another Dexter process to the perylene triplet, bringing the perylene molecule back to the triplet multiplicity. Lastly, the efficiency of rISC does not appear to vary significantly from molecule to molecule. However, the rISC process could be of use while doing spectroscopic studies on the excited triplet states by not using the optical fluorescence‐exciting beam to induce rISC, but by using a secondary tunable beam<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0033">
<sup>[33]</sup>
</a> or a time‐gated double excitation method to determine the quantum yields of the T<sub>N</sub> to S<sub>1</sub> rISC.<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0047" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0047">
<sup>[47]</sup>
</a> The rISC process might also be harnessed for a better control of triplet blinking in view of fluorescence superresolution STORM schemes at low temperature.[<a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0005">
<sup>5</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0048" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0048">
<sup>48</sup>
</a>, <a href="#cphc202100679-bib-0049" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="cphc202100679-bib-0049">
<sup>49</sup>
</a>]</p></section></section><section id="cphc202100679-sec-0009"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Conclusion</h2>
<p>Deuterated perylene in dibenzothiophene is not the most spectrally stable system. However, the fluorescence we detected from a single perylene molecule is on par with other bright systems commonly employed as lifetime‐limited single‐molecule emitters. That is a direct consequence of rISC that allows the recovery of the molecule from the perylene's long‐lived triplet state and at least quadruples the detectable fluorescence rate. By studying the autocorrelations of resonance fluorescence time traces we were able to estimate the intrinsic triplet lifetimes of deuterated perylene to be 8.5±0.4 ms for the T<sub>xy</sub>‐state and 64±12 ms for the T<sub>z</sub>‐state. In addition, we were able to quantify the effect of rISC on the depopulation rate of the triplet states. The demonstrated effect of rISC could be used in the future quantum studies of triplet‐state manipulation of single emitters, for superresolution at cryogenic temperatures, for enhancing the fluorescence emission, and for recovering single emitters from long‐lived triplet states.</p></section><section id="cphc202100679-sec-0011"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conflict of interest</h2>
