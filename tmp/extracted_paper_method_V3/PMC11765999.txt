</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Blink detection is considered a useful indicator both for clinical conditions and drowsiness state. In this work, we propose and compare deep learning architectures for the task of detecting blinks in video frame sequences. The first step is the training and application of an eye detector that extracts the eye regions from each video frame. The cropped eye regions are organized as three-dimensional (3D) input with the third dimension spanning time of 300 ms. Two different 3D convolutional neural networks are utilized (a simple 3D CNN and 3D ResNet), as well as a 3D autoencoder combined with a classifier coupled to the latent space. Finally, we propose the usage of a frame prediction accumulator combined with morphological processing and watershed segmentation to detect blinks and determine their start and stop frame in previously unseen videos. The proposed framework was trained on ten (9) different participants and tested on five (8) different ones, with a total of 162,400 frames and 1172 blinks for each eye. The start and end frame of each blink in the dataset has been annotate by specialized ophthalmologist. Quantitative comparison with state-of-the-art blink detection methodologies provide favorable results for the proposed neural architectures coupled with the prediction accumulator, with the 3D ResNet being the best as well as the fastest performer.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> blink detection, 3D CNN, 3D autoencoder, 3D ResNet, prediction accumulator, signal analysis</p></section></section><section id="sec1-jimaging-11-00027"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>Blink detection and extraction of blinking patterns is considered an essential component in applications like human–computer interaction (HCI) [<a href="#B1-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1-jimaging-11-00027">1</a>] systems, sentiment computing, monitoring fatigue and alertness [<a href="#B2-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2-jimaging-11-00027">2</a>,<a href="#B3-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3-jimaging-11-00027">3</a>], or behavioral studies [<a href="#B4-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4-jimaging-11-00027">4</a>]. Blinking patterns can also serve as important non-invasive biomarkers for several pathologies. More specifically, altered blink rates can be indicators of neurological or ocular conditions, as well as for a series of ophthalmological and systemic diseases or even with more severe pathologies or mental diseases [<a href="#B5-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5-jimaging-11-00027">5</a>,<a href="#B6-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6-jimaging-11-00027">6</a>], such as Parkinson’s disease [<a href="#B7-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7-jimaging-11-00027">7</a>,<a href="#B8-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8-jimaging-11-00027">8</a>], Tourette syndrom [<a href="#B9-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B9-jimaging-11-00027">9</a>], hemifacial spasm [<a href="#B10-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B10-jimaging-11-00027">10</a>], dry eye syndrome [<a href="#B11-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11-jimaging-11-00027">11</a>] or even schizophrenia [<a href="#B12-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12-jimaging-11-00027">12</a>], making blink recognition a valuable diagnostic tool. In the transportation and industrial sectors [<a href="#B13-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13-jimaging-11-00027">13</a>], reduced or irregular blink patterns can help to identify fatigue or drowsiness, consequently preventing accidents and enhancing safety. Moreover, in assistive communication systems [<a href="#B14-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B14-jimaging-11-00027">14</a>], blink recognition serves as a non-invasive, intuitive way of interaction for individuals with severe physical limitations. Therefore, the automatic detection and analysis of blinks is a crucial and versatile step in many artificial intelligence pipelines.</p>
<p>Using image processing and machine learning techniques, as well as sensor-based tracking, blink detection systems capture eyelid motion with high precision. Many of these methods utilize facial landmarks [<a href="#B15-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15-jimaging-11-00027">15</a>], using deep neural networks like convolutional neural networks (CNNs) or vision transformers. Blink detection systems can improve user’s experience, ensuring safety, and enable accessibility in diverse contexts.</p>
<p>Thus, automatic blink detection and identification of blinking patterns can bridge critical gaps between human needs and the capabilities of machines, offering solutions for more accurate, on-time diagnosis and improving the interaction between human and machines.</p>
<p>In this work, we propose a complete blink detection framework based on video sequences of the participants’ upper face, comparing various architectures of neural networks combined with signal segmentation techniques applied to a prediction accumulator for each video frame. The main contributions of this work are</p>
<ul class="list" style="list-style-type:disc">
<li><p>The training and application of an object detector (YOLOX [<a href="#B16-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16-jimaging-11-00027">16</a>]) that extracts the regions around each eye from each frame of a video sequence, since the zoom factor of the images renders other methods of eye-detection not applicable (i.e., Mediapipe [<a href="#B17-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17-jimaging-11-00027">17</a>]).</p></li>
<li><p>Embedding temporal information in three-dimensional (3D) input into convolutional neural networks (3D CNN, 3D autoencoder and 3D ResNet), with the third dimension spanning a typical blink duration, combined with a simple classifier in order to classify the image sequences during training.</p></li>
<li><p>The proposed inference mode (blink detection in unseen videos) that utilizes one accumulator for each eye, aggregating predictions during a single forward pass of all <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm1" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub><mo>−</mo></mrow></mrow></math></span>subsequences in the unseen video with step = 1. The accumulators are subsequently processed using morphology and watershed segmentation in order to robustly detect blinks of any duration.</p></li>
<li><p>A novel and accurate definition of blink detection metrics that considers many-to-one predicted and actual blinks (and vice versa).</p></li>
</ul>
<p>The proposed methodologies were validated and tested in our clinical data and in one public dataset in comparison with other state-of-the-art methods.</p></section><section id="sec2-jimaging-11-00027"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and Methods</h2>
<section id="sec2dot1-jimaging-11-00027"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Related Work</h3>
<p>An early algorithm, which efficiently detects faces and isolates the eye regions was based on Adaboost method and utilized image features based on the integral image [<a href="#B18-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18-jimaging-11-00027">18</a>], has been an initial component in many blink detection methods, especially when acquired images have full-frontal faces. In [<a href="#B19-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19-jimaging-11-00027">19</a>], multi-scale and orientation Gabor filtering were utilized for blink detection, reporting precision 84.62%. In addition, a method using SVM classifier with Haar wavelets and HOG (Histogram of Oriented Gradient) features [<a href="#B20-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B20-jimaging-11-00027">20</a>] achieved blink detection accuracy 92.5% and 86% when tested on standard databases and under real world conditions, respectively. A similar method proposed in [<a href="#B21-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21-jimaging-11-00027">21</a>], where Haar-like features were utilized for face detection, reported 95% blink detection accuracy for good illumination conditions and 77% for poor illumination conditions.</p>
<p>In the study of Choi et al. [<a href="#B22-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22-jimaging-11-00027">22</a>], an AdaBoost classifier was proposed as blink detector, achieving 96% accuracy on their own dataset. Additionally, Al-gawwam and Benaissa [<a href="#B23-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23-jimaging-11-00027">23</a>] proposed a facial landmark estimator in order to calculate the vertical distance between the upper and lower eyelids and used Savitzky–Golay (SG) filter to detect blinks with 96.65% precision on standard datasets.</p>
<p>Drutarovsky and Fogelton proposed an eye blink detection algorithm based on the motion vectors of each eye region [<a href="#B24-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B24-jimaging-11-00027">24</a>]. A simple finite state machine was used to change the state of each eye and thus detect blinks. Following this approach, in [<a href="#B25-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B25-jimaging-11-00027">25</a>], Fogelton and Benesova estimated motion vectors [<a href="#B26-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B26-jimaging-11-00027">26</a>] for each pixel of the eye region, extracting the vertical component of the average motion vector and the statistical standard deviation. Those two parameters were fed to a finite state machine that classifies whether the frame can be considered blink or not. A basic constraint in this method is that the head should not be rotating significantly, and it also requires the cropping of the eye.</p>
<p>In [<a href="#B27-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27-jimaging-11-00027">27</a>], Fogelton and Benesova expanded their previous work, utilized the detected motion vectors of the eye region combined with two LSTMs (each 64 units) that accept normal and reversed time input implementing a bidirectional LSTM. The input subsequence length was set to 40 frames and care was taken to extract the subsequences with the blink of a closed eye in the middle of the frames. However, no details were provided on how an unseen video sequence is temporally segmented into 40-frame subsequences. This method also requires the cropping of the eye region, which was performed manually in the reported results.</p>
<p>In a more recent method [<a href="#B28-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28-jimaging-11-00027">28</a>], a Siamese CNN neural network was used as a 256-length feature extractor. These features were fed to a bidirectional LSTM with 256 cells, followed by a dropout layer and a fully connected layer, which performs many-to-many sequence predictions. The training of the Siamese network was performed independently from the LSTM. Although the authors noted a subsequence length of 64 frames, no details were provided on how a given video was split into subsequences, nor how the exact blink start and stop frame were determined. Cropping of eye regions is a prerequisite for this method as well.</p>
<p>In [<a href="#B29-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29-jimaging-11-00027">29</a>], a deep learning encoder–decoder (DLED) architecture, DeepLabv3+ [<a href="#B30-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30-jimaging-11-00027">30</a>], was used, and two different instances of this network were trained to segment the iris and the palpebral fissure regions from each frame independently. In a post-processing step, the distance between the eyelids and the iris diameter was calculated. The estimated fraction of the palpebral fissure height and the moving median iris diameter were used to classify blinks using adaptive thresholds to handle cases of varying patient–camera distance or the patient’s orientation. This is not an end-to-end method; thus, it may be susceptible to erroneous segmentations. In contrast, the DLEDs are trained using individual frames rather than subsequences.</p></section><section id="sec2dot2-jimaging-11-00027"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Overview of the Proposed Methodology</h3>
<p>The proposed blink detection method is established through the following basic algorithmic steps. Initially, each eye region (left and right) is identified and detected for each participant using a trained YOLOX [<a href="#B16-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16-jimaging-11-00027">16</a>] neural network detector. The detector predicts the four values of the parameters (upper left corner -x and y coordinate-, width and height) that are used in order to define the bounding boxes that isolate the two eye regions. Typical results of the application of YOLOX are shown in <a href="#jimaging-11-00027-f001" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>.</p>

Each 3D eye region is input in the trained classifier, and each one of the 12 frames is characterized as 0 or 1 no blink or blink, respectively. An accumulator <em>A</em> is defined for each eye with a non-negative integer value for each frame of the whole video sequence that sums the predicted value for each frame. Considering the accumulator as a 1D discrete signal, the blinks are identified as the peaks of <em>A</em>, using the watershed segmentation algorithm.</p></section><section id="sec2dot3-jimaging-11-00027"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Eye Region Detection and Preparation of Dataset for the Training Phase</h3>
<p>A web-camera setup was used to acquire videos from 17 different subjects during a clinical examination. In order to extract the necessary eye region that was used as input to the classifiers, a YOLOX eye detector was trained. Using the pre-trained neural network for palpebral fissure segmentation [<a href="#B29-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29-jimaging-11-00027">29</a>], the coordinates of the bounding box from 48,000 images from 7 different individuals can be calculated and can be used to train the detector. The YOLOX differs from the classic YOLO methods as it adopts an anchor-free manner and performs other advanced detection techniques like the decoupled head, achieving state-of-the-art results. Having the two bounding boxes (one for each eye, see <a href="#jimaging-11-00027-f001" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>) for each frame and knowing the blink starting and ending points (annotation of ground truth), the dataset can be formed as follows.</p>
<p>For all participants, the median blink length/duration was found to be 300 ms or 12 frames correspondingly, considering that videos are captured with frequency 25 frames per second (fps); however, the number of frames where the palpebral fissure is substantially small (i.e., the eye appears closed). It is 85 to 100 ms or 3 to 4 frames at 25 fps. Using the ground truth, we extract a subsequence of length <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm3" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>12</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span> frames, starting from the annotated beginning of the blinks. Non-blink sequences with a length of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm4" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>12</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span> frames are also selected to represent the “No blink” class. In both cases, YOLOX-generated bounding boxes are used to crop the subsequences, which obtain the size of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm5" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mn>48</mn><mo>×</mo><mn>48</mn><mo>×</mo><mn>12</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span>. A graphical overview of the data preparation procedure is shown in <a href="#jimaging-11-00027-f002" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>.</p>

Deep Learning Architectures for Blink Detection</h3>
<p>Three different architectures of deep learning neural networks are proposed and tested as blink classifiers. Since blinking is a temporal process of finite duration, we utilized 3D CNNs as proposed architectures, using the cropped subsequences <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm6" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mn>48</mn><mo>×</mo><mn>48</mn><mo>×</mo><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub></mrow></mrow></math></span> as input. Spatial cropping was generated by YOLOX, as described in the previous subsection. Except for the 3D CNN, we also experimented with an end-to-end two-phase model consisting of a 3D CNN subsequence classifier and of a 3D autoencoder, with a linear combination of the corresponding loss functions. This technique has been shown to be beneficial in many machine learning tasks [<a href="#B31-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31-jimaging-11-00027">31</a>].</p>
<p>After the inference process, the classification results of each classifier are stored in the accumulators and processed, as shown in the next subsection.</p>
<section id="sec2dot4dot1-jimaging-11-00027"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4.1. Three-Dimensional CNN Architectures for Subsequence Classification</h4>
<p>The first tested classifier is the 3D ResNet classifier. It consists of a 50-layer ResNet with 3D convolutions [<a href="#B32-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32-jimaging-11-00027">32</a>], with one (1) stack of four (4) residual blocks, where each convolutional layer of the stack has 64 filters. This architecture was selected since it was already pre-trained on the generic ImageNet dataset, enabling it to successfully capture image characteristics.</p>
<p>Furthermore, in order to assess the required complexity of the CNN architecture, a much simpler 3D CNN is also tested as a sequence classifier. This particular architecture “simple 3D CNN” consists of two components, one 3D CNN encoder for feature extraction and one classifier, which is created from repeated blocks of fully connected, ReLU and dropout layers. A graphical representation of this architecture is depicted in <a href="#jimaging-11-00027-f003" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>, where the green box is highlighting the part of the 3D CNN feature extractor and with the blue box being highlighted as the classifier.</p>

Three-Dimensional CNN Autoencoder</h4>
<p>Finally, a more complex neural architecture was used as a classifier, a 3D CNN autoencoder along with a classifier. This particular architecture is trying to isolate features in an unsupervised way, aimed at reconstructing the 3D sequence. Alternatively, using descriptive features to classify the subsequence into one of the two classes in a supervised manner can only be carried out when we are given ground-truth labels.</p>
<p>The encoder part of the autoencoder and the classifier (fully connected layers) are identical with the feature extractor and the classifier of the simple 3D CNN, respectively. The encoder part consists of four blocks; each block consists of a 3D convolutional layer, a ReLU activation and a dropout layer. The convolutional layer of the first block, “Conv_1”, with stride [2, 2, 2] and 16 filters with 5 width across the three dimensions, expects the input to be of size 48 × 48 × 12 × 1, resulting in a lower feature map of size 24 × 24 × 6 × 16. The following block, “Conv_2” with stride [2, 2, 2] and 32 filters of size [5, 5, 5], also outputs a lower feature map of size 12 × 12 × 3 × 32. The third convolutional block, “Conv_3”, preserves the same dimensions as the previous “Conv_2” by setting the stride to [1, 1, 1] and retains the same number of filters. The last convolution block, “Conv_4”, reduces the size of the first three dimensions into 6 × 6 × 3 × 32, due to stride [2, 2, 1] and preserving the same number of filters. Finally, the output of “Conv_4” block is flattened and fed to a fully connected layer, which defines the latent space, with 2048 units.</p>
<p>From each one of the “Conv_1”, “Conv_2” and “Conv_4” blocks, a skip connection is created, and the outputs of those blocks are connected and concatenated with the input of the corresponding 3D transposed convolution layer of the decoder’s three blocks.</p>
<p>The decoder part consists of three blocks of repeated 3D transposed convolutional and ReLU layers, while the last 3D convolutional layer followed by a Clipped-ReLU with ceiling 255 are the last layers that will give the predicted/reconstructed output. An example of this architecture is shown in <a href="#jimaging-11-00027-f003" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>, where the size of each output feature map is available along with the skip connections. The skip connections are used in order to minimize the information loss due to the compression and decompression of an encoder and decoder, respectively. Also, their usage will help the autoencoder and the reconstruction loss function to quickly converge in the solution. The autoencoder learns to reconstruct the given input using the characteristics of latent space, while the classifier is utilizing them in order to classify the input sequence as blink or no-blink.</p></section></section><section id="sec2dot5-jimaging-11-00027"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. <p>A common issue in time sequence analysis, such as video, is the segmentation of the sequence into subsequences and their individual classification.</p>
<p>Many of the referenced works on blink detection do not adequately clarify the process of inference in the case of video sequences. Segmenting a previously unseen video sequence into constant-length subsequences will inevitably split a blink event into two subsequences. Thus, inference in these cases may be incorrect. The proposed method consists of interference subsequences of constant length <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm18" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub></mrow></math></span> with step equal to 1. Thus, each frame will be “seen” by the deep learning model <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm19" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub></mrow></math></span> times. Specifically, we may assume the number of frames with a palpebral fissure as substantially small (i.e., the eye appears closed). i.e., for them to be <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm20" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mi>M</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>3</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span> or 4 frames (equivalently 85 to 100 ms at 25 fps). The significant frames of each blink will be split into two adjacent subsequences <em>M</em> times, whereas the blink significant frames will be contained into a single subsequence <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm21" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub><mo>−</mo><mi>M</mi></mrow></mrow></math></span> times. In our application, we selected the subsequence length of <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm22" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>12</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span> frames or an equivalent time of 300 ms. Setting <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm23" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>12</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span> ensured that, on average, each frame was expected to be correctly classified 8 to 9 time. Otherwise, it could have been wrongly classified 3 to 4 times. Therefore, accumulating the predictions for each frame and subsequent accumulator peak detection and segmentation is expected to be immune to the blink split issue.</p>
<p>It is evident that increasing <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm24" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub></mrow></math></span> would cause a small improvement in the achieved classification metrics; however, it would increase the third dimension of the input to the deep learning architectures, thus increasing the memory requirements and computational burden, with it being significant for both training and testing.</p>
<p>For any unseen video <em>V</em> of <em>N</em> frames, the following steps are applied: The YOLOX eye detector is applied to generate two cropped sequences around each one of the eyes: <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm25" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>V</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mi>R</mi></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>V</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mi>L</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mi>Y</mi><mi>O</mi><mi>L</mi><mi>O</mi><mi>X</mi><mrow><mo>(</mo><mi>V</mi><mo>)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></math></span>, where <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm26" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>V</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mi>R</mi></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>V</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mi>L</mi></msub><mo>∈</mo><msup><mi mathvariant="bold">R</mi><mrow><mn>48</mn><mo>×</mo><mn>48</mn><mo>×</mo><mi>N</mi></mrow></msup></mrow></mrow></math></span>. For both <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm27" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>V</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mi>R</mi></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>V</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mi>L</mi></msub></mrow></mrow></math></span>, the subsequences of length <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm28" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub></mrow></math></span> and overlap <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm29" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span> are fed to the blink classifier <em>C</em>, and the prediction for the <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm30" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub></mrow></math></span>-tuple frames is assigned to each frame. The blink classifier <em>C</em> may be a simple 3D CNN, a 3D autoencoder or a 3D ResNet, as described in the previous subsection.</p>
<p>The first step is to create an accumulator array <em>A</em> that holds the sum of predictions for each frame. For each <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm31" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn><mo>,</mo><mo>…</mo><mo>,</mo><mi>N</mi></mrow></mrow></math></span>, the subsequence <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm32" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>S</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>F</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>F</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>+</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></msub><mo>,</mo><mo>…</mo><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>F</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>+</mo><mn>11</mn></mrow></msub><mo>)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></math></span> is extracted. The blink classifier <em>C</em> is applied to predict the class of the subsequence (0: no blink, 1: blink). The index <em>i</em> is increased by 1, and the next subsequence is extracted and fed into the classifier. Thus, each frame <em>F</em> is being “seen” by the classifier <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm33" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>12</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span> times, with an equal number of predictions. An accumulator array <em>A</em> of size <em>N</em> adds all the predictions for each frame. The algorithmic steps for generating the accumulator for the right and left eye are given in detail in Algorithm 1.
</p>

<td class="formula"><math id="mm64" display="block" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mi>c</mi><mo>=</mo><munder><mo>∑</mo><mrow><mi>i</mi><mi>j</mi></mrow></munder><msub><mi>R</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi><mi>j</mi></mrow></msub><mo>,</mo><mspace width="4.pt"></mspace><mi>where</mi><mspace width="4.pt"></mspace><mi>j</mi><mo>:</mo><munder><mo>∑</mo><mi>i</mi></munder><mi>R</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi><mi>j</mi></mrow><mo>&gt;</mo><mn>1</mn><mspace width="4.pt"></mspace><mi>and</mi><mspace width="4.pt"></mspace><mi>i</mi><mo>:</mo><munder><mo>∑</mo><mi>j</mi></munder><msub><mi>R</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi><mi>j</mi></mrow></msub><mo>&gt;</mo><mn>1</mn><mo>.</mo></mrow></mrow></math></td>

<p>In addition, the number of columns of <em>R</em> with a sum equal to 0 is also as FP blinks. Combining all the above, the total number of FP blinks is given by</p>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="FD8-jimaging-11-00027"><tr>
<td class="formula"><math id="mm65" display="block" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mi>F</mi><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><mi>a</mi><mo>+</mo><mi>b</mi><mo>−</mo><mi>c</mi><mo>+</mo><mfenced separators="" open="(" close=")"><mspace width="4.pt"></mspace><mi>number</mi><mspace width="4.pt"></mspace><mi>of</mi><mspace width="4.pt"></mspace><mi>columns</mi><mspace width="4.pt"></mspace><mi>of</mi><mspace width="4.pt"></mspace><mi>R</mi><mspace width="4.pt"></mspace><mi>with</mi><munder><mo>∑</mo><mi>j</mi></munder><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mfenced></mrow></mrow></math></td>

<p>Finally, the number of correctly predicted blinks or true positives (TPs) is defined as</p>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="FD9-jimaging-11-00027"><tr>
<td class="formula"><math id="mm66" display="block" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mi>T</mi><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><munder><mo>∑</mo><mrow><mi>i</mi><mi>j</mi></mrow></munder><msub><mi>R</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi><mi>j</mi></mrow></msub><mo>−</mo><mi>F</mi><mi>P</mi></mrow></mrow></math></td>
<td class="label">(9)</td>

<p>A simplified example of the corresponding matrix <em>R</em> is shown in <a href="#jimaging-11-00027-f006" class="usa-link">Figure 6</a> to demonstrate the relevant definitions. Each row of the matrix represents a ground-truth blink, and each column represents a predicted blink after applying the inference process. Considering Equation (<a href="#FD4-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link">4</a>), the number of false-negative blinks (FNs) is equal to the number of rows of <em>R</em> with a sum equal to 0; thus, <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm67" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mi>F</mi><mi>N</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span> due to the 7th row. The 5th column of <em>R</em> is empty; thus, it contributes 1 blink to the FPs. Further, according to Equation (<a href="#FD5-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link">5</a>), <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm68" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mi>a</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span> (due to <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm69" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>R</mi><mrow><mn>6</mn><mo>,</mo><mn>7</mn></mrow></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>R</mi><mrow><mn>6</mn><mo>,</mo><mn>8</mn></mrow></msub></mrow></mrow></math></span>). Similarly, according to Equation (<a href="#FD6-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link">6</a>), <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm70" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mi>b</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span> (due to <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm71" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>R</mi><mrow><mn>2</mn><mo>,</mo><mn>3</mn></mrow></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>R</mi><mrow><mn>3</mn><mo>,</mo><mn>3</mn></mrow></msub></mrow></mrow></math></span>). Also, according to Equation (<a href="#FD7-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link">7</a>), <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm72" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mi>c</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span> (due to <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm73" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>R</mi><mrow><mn>2</mn><mo>,</mo><mn>3</mn></mrow></msub></mrow></mrow></math></span>). Thus, <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm74" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mi>F</mi><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>4</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span>, according to Equation (<a href="#FD8-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link">8</a>). Having calculated the number of false-positive and false-negative blinks, the number of true-positive blinks (indicated by green color) can be readily calculated using Equation (<a href="#FD9-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link">9</a>) as <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm75" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><mi>T</mi><mi>P</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>9</mn><mo>−</mo><mn>4</mn><mo>=</mo><mn>5</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span>.</p>

<p>The data were acquired through a prospective study. The protocol that was utilized adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was provided by all participants. The institutional review board of Democritus University of Thrace approved the study protocol (protocol number/date of approval: ES2/Th15/25-2-2021). This clinical study was conducted between October 2020 and March 2021. The official registration number of this study is <a href="https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT04828187" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NCT04828187</a>.</p></section></section><section id="sec3-jimaging-11-00027"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results</h2>
<section id="sec3dot1-jimaging-11-00027"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. The Available Dataset</h3>
<p>The available dataset consists of 17 patients, 5 of them wearing glasses, each with one video of their frontal face with a duration of between 5 and 10 min. The total number of frames is 162,400, containing 2376 blinks. The ground truth (starting and ending frame for each blink and each eye) was provided by specialized ophthalmologists. The data were split into training and test subsets, using nine and eight patients, respectively. The corresponding number of frames/blinks was 69,400/1204 and 93,000/1172, respectively. All methods under comparison were trained using subsequences from the trained subsets, except for the [<a href="#B29-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29-jimaging-11-00027">29</a>], where deep learning segmentation models were used as already-pretrained models using static images. All methods (including [<a href="#B29-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29-jimaging-11-00027">29</a>]) were validated using the aforementioned test subset. The publicly available dataset “Talking face” [<a href="#B34-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34-jimaging-11-00027">34</a>], provided with the manual annotations of [<a href="#B27-jimaging-11-00027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27-jimaging-11-00027">27</a>], was also used as a separate test subset to assess all methods.</p></section><section id="sec3dot2-jimaging-11-00027"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. <div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/jimaging-11-00027-t003/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section></section><section id="sec4-jimaging-11-00027"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Discussion</h2>
<p>Three different deep learning architectures for blink detection in videos acquired under clinical settings were described. A robust approach was proposed for the inference mode of the three architectures for unseen videos. More specifically, all three architectures were applied in an inference mode to cropped video subsequences with a constant length <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm77" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub></mrow></math></span> and a step set to 1 (overlap equal to <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm78" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></mrow></math></span>). Thus, every cropped subregion of each frame participated in <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm79" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub></mrow></math></span> input subsequences and therefore received <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><math id="mm80" overflow="linebreak"><mrow><msub><mi>N</mi><mi>s</mi></msub></mrow></math></span> predictions. An accumulator added the predictions for every frame, for each eye. A watershed-based segmentation of a negative accumulator segments different blinks and obtains their starting and ending frames.</p>
<p><a href="#jimaging-11-00027-t004" class="usa-link">Table 4</a> shows the achieved true-positive (TP) and false-positive (FP) blinks for three different values of the IOU threshold. It can be observed that the number of TPs and FPs shows a very slow decreasing trend, as expected, which indicates a low sensitivity of the proposed methods with respect to the threshold.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/jimaging-11-00027-t004/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section><p>Quantitative results indicate that all three different deep neural architectures combined with the prediction accumulator achieved better blink detection results than the prior state-of-the-art methods, with the 3D ResNet classifier slightly outperforming the other ones. The usage of the prediction accumulator provides us with the ability to handle video subsequences and to be more robust than seeing subsequences once. The application of a simple threshold and watershed algorithm could be used with any other blink classifier, whether it is a deep learning approach (neural network) or a simple feature-based classifier, like a support vector machine (SVM), allowing for fast signal processing while providing competitive and effective blink detection results.</p>
<p>As already stated, our dataset comprises 17 Caucasian participants from 5 different countries. Five of the participants wore glasses. Under our clinical settings, video acquisition was performed with the participants placed at approximately 10–15 cm from the camera, who were illuminated by two NIR light sources. We plan to substantially increase the number of participants in our future work; however, we do not expect the race distribution to change. Thus, the proposed system is not currently tested for different races nor different camera-to-face distances and orientations. Current experimentation has demonstrated that the proposed system, and especially the eye detection/cropping module, is relatively sensitive to camera-to-face distances. Our future work will also include modifications of the proposed system to classify blinks as complete or incomplete.</p></section><section id="notes1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Author Contributions</h2>
