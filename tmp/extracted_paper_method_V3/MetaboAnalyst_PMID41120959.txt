>Materials and methods   Study subjects   This study included 221 untreated chronic HBV-infected patients (stratified into immune-tolerance [IT,  n  = 47], immune-clearance [IC,  n  = 44], low-replicative [LR,  n  = 73], and enhanced-replication [ENH,  n  = 57] phases), 32 cirrhosis patients, 26 HCC patients, and 44 healthy controls from Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital (June 2020–March 2021). Classifications followed established HBV phase guidelines [ 2 ], cirrhosis criteria [ 10 ], and HCC diagnostic standards [ 15 ]. Exclusion criteria encompassed concurrent liver diseases, other infections, malignancies, or metabolic disorders. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Declaration of Istanbul, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Provincial People's Hospital, which granted a waiver of informed consent. Sample collection and processing   Fasting venous blood was centrifuged (4,000 rpm, 10 min), with serum stored at −80 °C. Clinical parameters (age, sex, alanine transaminase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], total bilirubin [TBIL], direct bilirubin [DBIL]) were recorded. For LC–MS, 100 μL serum was mixed with internal standard, precipitated (methanol:acetonitrile = 2:1, v/v), freeze-thawed, centrifuged (13,000 rpm, 15 min), and analyzed. Quality control (QC) samples were interleaved every six runs. LC–MS analysis   LC–MS analysis was performed using a Thermo Scientific™ system (United States). Chromatography used an Acclaim™ C18 column (40 °C, 0.2 mL/min) with 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B). Mass spectrometry utilized an electrospray ionization (ESI) source in positive ion mode. Data were acquired across an m/z range of 20–1000. Data processing   Clinical data were analyzed using SPSS 20.0. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± SD (Student’s t-test), non-normal data as median (interquartile range) (Mann–Whitney U test), and categorical variables using chi-square tests, with statistical significance set at  P  < 0.05.   Raw metabolomic data were processed using MetaboScape 3.0 for peak extraction, noise reduction, and normalization. Metabolites were annotated using the Bruker Spectral Library, HMDB, and online databases. Multivariate analyses (Principal component analysis [PCA], Partial least squares discriminant analysis [PLS-DA]) and univariate analyses (t-test, fold change [FC]) were performed in MetaboAnalyst 6.0. DMs were identified using thresholds of FC > 2 or < 0.5,  P  < 0.05, and VIP > 1.5. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted with an impact value threshold of > 0.02. Results   Clinical characteristics of patients   The clinical characteristics of the enrolled study subjects are shown in Table  1 . There were no statistically significant differences in gender between the patient cohorts in the four natural history phases of CHB and the healthy controls ( p  > 0.05). The conventional serological liver injury markers—including ALT, AST, TBil, and DBil—were elevated in the IC and ENH groups compared to the IT and LR groups. This indicated that the liver injury marker profiled of the IT and LR groups were more similar to those of healthy controls.   Table 1. The results showed that, except for the LC group and healthy controls, other groups could not be well clustered from healthy controls, with intra-group differences exceeding inter-group differences (Fig.  1  A, D, G, J, M, and P). Subsequently, the supervised PLS-DA algorithm based on partial least squares regression was employed to cluster serum metabolites between each group and healthy controls. All groups and healthy controls exhibited clear separation, with samples clustering into two distinct groups (Fig.  1  B, E, H, K, N, and Q). For the PLS-DA models of the six groups, after 100 permutation tests, the R2X(cum) values were relatively high (0.627—0.724), the R2Y(cum) values were very high (0.875—0.953), and the Q2(cum) values were also very high (0.787—0.893). The Q2 values were generally slightly lower but close to the corresponding R2Y values, and all  p -values were less than 0.05. These results collectively demonstrated that the outcomes of the PLS-DA models were highly reliable (Fig.  1  C, F, I, L, O, R and Supplementary Table 1).   Fig. 1.
