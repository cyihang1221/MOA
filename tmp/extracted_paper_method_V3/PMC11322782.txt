</ul></section><section class="abstract" id="ab015"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Melanoma accounts for more than 60% of deaths associated with skin cancer, making its early detection through dermatological screening essential for improved treatment outcomes. Early detection and successful treatment of melanoma can dramatically increase the 5-year survival rate from 14 to 98%. To support such efforts, comprehensive identification of metabolite patterns capable of describing cancer progression will help support the foundational knowledge necessary to build early detection platforms for intervention prior to metastasis. Using an UPLC-MS, as part of a cell-based, untargeted metabolomics approach, we profiled the metabolomes of WM-226-4 and WM-115 cells. Derived from the metastatic and the primary sites of the same individual, these two cell lines represent a paired melanoma cancer cell line. Progenesis and MetaboAnalyst, platforms dedicated to metabolomics data analysis, were used to establish a panel of differentially expressed metabolites across these two stages of melanoma. In addition, <em>mummichog</em> was used to identify the affected pathways. A total of 12 differentially expressed metabolites including amino acids, carnitine, acylcarnitine, and a limited set of lipids were identified. The significantly differing metabolites are components of a diverse set of metabolic pathways (e.g., glycerophospholipid metabolism, carnitine shuttle, tryptophan metabolism), that have biological implications for the survival and dissemination of metastatic melanoma cells.</p></section><section id="s0005"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0020">Melanoma, characterized as a malignant tumor originating from melanocytes, is one of the most serious types of skin cancer and is estimated to account for more than 60% of skin cancer deaths in the United States in 2018 <a href="#b0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0005">[1]</a>. Melanoma is commonly found in dermal tissue, but, in rare cases, it can occur in the oral cavity and eyes <a href="#b0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0010">[2]</a>, <a href="#b0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0015">[3]</a> According to the National Cancer Institute, melanoma has become one of the most common cancers for individuals with low levels of skin pigmentation, and, from 1991 to 2011, the annual incidence of melanoma increased by more than 60% among Caucasians <a href="#b0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0020">[4]</a>. Projection of these statistics on the demography of the United States estimates a melanoma diagnosis for one out of every 75 people.<sup>5</sup> The rapidly invasive and metastatic characteristic of melanoma leads to a high mortality rate, with 5-year survival rate of only 14% after metastatic diagnosis <a href="#b0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0030">[6]</a>. Early detection of melanoma, while still in the primary stage, followed by proper treatment (e.g., surgical resection) has been shown to boost the 5-year survival rate to 98.2% <a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">[7]</a>. However, early detection remains a challenge as characteristic lesions used for diagnosis are often masked by other skin features, such as nevi, seborrheic keratosis, and freckles <a href="#b0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0040">[8]</a>. Determination of diagnostic metabolites capable of indicating the progress of melanoma could aid in detection prior to metastasis. In addition, research on metabolite signatures of melanoma will contribute to understanding and elucidating the affected metabolic pathways and could yield mechanistic insights into its formation and progression.</p>
<p id="p0025">In contrast to highly specific targeted metabolite probes, untargeted metabolomics combined with contemporary informatics has the potential to elucidate the intricate metabolic complexities of biological systems <a href="#b0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0045">[9]</a>, <a href="#b0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0050">[10]</a>. In terms of analytical approaches, untargeted metabolomics research can be divided into two broad categories: mass spectrometry (MS)-based, which is routinely coupled with a variety of separation techniques (e.g., liquid chromatography (LC), gas chromatography (GC), ion mobility spectrometry (IMS), capillary electrophoresis (CE)) <a href="#b0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0055">[11]</a>, and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based approaches. Abaffy et al. applied the GC–MS technique to analyze the metabolite profiles of volatile organic compounds (VOC) extracted from melanoma skin biopsies in comparison with those obtained from the benign skin biopsies using headspace solid phase micro extraction (HS SPME) <a href="#b0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0060">[12]</a>. The authors found elevated levels of lauric acid and palmitic acid in the melanoma samples, which could be potential diagnostic metabolites. While the analysis method used for the study focused on VOC, non-VOC, such as phospholipids and carbohydrates were ignored. In a paper published in 2014, Wang et al. <a href="#b0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0025">[5]</a>. utilized an NMR-based untargeted metabolomics approach to investigate spleen tissues from mice with metastatic melanoma. In this study, the authors compared the metabolite pool extracted from spleen tissue samples of the experimental group (which received subcutaneous injection with tumor cells) with those of the control group (which received subcutaneous injection with phosphate buffered saline (PBS)). Orthogonal partial least squares (OPLS) analysis identified metabolites accounting for the difference observed between the experimental and control groups. Based on this data, the authors proposed that the pyrimidine, purine, pyruvate, and glutamate metabolic pathways, among others, could be associated with melanoma development. In a subsequent 2014 manuscript, Wang et al. <a href="#b0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0065">[13]</a>. analyzed stomach rather than spleen tissue using a similar methodology to further investigate the altered metabolic pathways during melanoma progression. In this case they observed a number of common metabolites, including alanine and glutamate, that aligned with their findings using spleen tissue.</p>
<p id="p0030">Cell-based metabolomic analysis, as an alternative to the tissue-based approach, has been gaining popularity in the research community <a href="#b0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0070">[14]</a>, <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>, <a href="#b0080" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0080">[16]</a>. In contrast to tissue and biofluids (e.g., blood, urine), cellular samples are generally less expensive to produce and procure. In addition, they are more likely to have readily available controls, and tend towards simplified interpretations since the factors affecting to the metabolic phenotype are minimized <a href="#b0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0085">[17]</a> Although cells may not always fully reflect the range of biological processes at the tissue and organ level, they can serve as a reasonable introductory medium to aid in understanding the fundamental metabolic mechanism(s) associated with disease progression. Moreover, cell lines offer researchers the opportunity to directly explore response mechanisms to external stimuli (e.g., drugs) and identify metabolite profiles capable of distinguishing normal and abnormal states.</p>
<p id="p0035">With the goal of complementing the previously referenced metabolomics research, this study compares the metabolic profile of two melanoma cell lines that represent the primary and metastatic stages. Experiments were conducted using an ultra performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Compared to GC techniques, the use of LC captures a different subset of metabolites that may provide additional information regarding the underlying biochemistry. In addition, the high sensitivity of mass spectrometry will enable detection of a more comprehensive metabolite profile than NMR approaches. In this study, by applying a variety of data analysis methods (i.e., partial least squares discriminant analysis, fold change analysis, ROC curve analysis, and network analysis) and comprehensive metabolomics database queries (i.e., Metlin <a href="#b0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0090">[18]</a>, MassBank <a href="#b0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0095">[19]</a>, mzCloud, LipidBlast <a href="#b9000" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b9000">[46]</a>, MetFrag <a href="#b9005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b9005">[47]</a>, and HMDB <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>), a list of metabolites were found to distinguish the different stages of melanoma for the cell lines we have chosen to analyze. Placing these observations in context, a detailed discussion of the metabolic implications for the dissemination and survival of metastatic melanoma are discussed.</p></section><section id="s0010"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and methods:</h2>
<section id="s0015"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Sample preparation</h3>
<section id="s0020"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1.1. Cell culture and harvesting</h4>
<p id="p0040">WM-115 and WM-266-4 melanoma cell lines were provided by the lab of Dr. Yinsheng Wang (University of California, Riverside). The cell pellets were obtained from aliquots of a single batch of cultured human melanoma cell lines. Cell cultures were maintained at 37˚C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO<sub>2</sub> using Eagle's Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM, Gilbco, Calrsbad, CA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and penicillin/streptomycin (100 IU/mL). The culture medium was changed every 2 to 3 days, as necessary. At 80% confluency, cells were harvested using gentle scraping and collected by centrifugation. Experiments performed on the WM-266-4 and WM-115 cell lines were conducted on the 20th and 16th passage, respectively. Prior to metabolite extraction, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and cell numbers were counted using a standard hemocytometer. Approximately 1 × 10<sup>6</sup> cells were used for metabolite extraction for each measurement.</p></section><section id="s0025"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1.2. Metabolite extraction</h4>
<p id="p0045">A simultaneous quenching and extraction method was adopted in this study with a few modifications from the previously reported protocol <a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">[21]</a>, <a href="#b0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0110">[22]</a>. Specifically, each tube of cell pellets was first quenched with 600 μL of methanol and water (80:20, v/v) which was pre-chilled on dry ice. The cell pellets were then vortexed vigorously, followed by thawing in an ice bath for 10 min. An individual tube of cell pellet was then transferred to liquid nitrogen to freeze for 10 min, and subjected to another thawing step in an ice bath for 10 min. The thawed pellet was taken out from the ice bath and briefly vortexed. The freeze-thaw cycle, as described above, was repeated to completely rupture the cells. Immediately following the lysing cycle, the processed samples were centrifuged at 8000×<em>g</em> for 10 min and the supernatant was transferred to a new, clean sample tube. The pellet left in the tube was subjected to a second round of aforementioned extraction process with 600 μL of methanol and water. The supernatant obtained in the second round of extraction was combined with that from the first round. The solution containing the cell metabolites was then dried in a vacuum concentrator. The dry residue was reconstituted with 500 uL of methanol and water (80:20, v/v) for subsequent analysis.</p></section></section><section id="s0030"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)</h3>
<p id="p0050">Total RNA was extracted from the cell pellets using the Total RNA Kit I (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA) after WM-115 and WM-266–4 cells were harvested at 80% confluency. 1 μg of total RNA was subjected to reverse-transcription using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI) and an oligo(dT)16 primer to produce the corresponding cDNA. Gene expression levels were measured by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) with iQ SYBR Green Supermix kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). 20 ng of cDNA dissolved in 20 μL reaction mixture containing SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (2X) and corresponding primers (250 nM forward and 250 nM reverse) was inserted in a Bio-Rad CFX Connect™ Real-Time PCR Detection System. The gene expression level was normalized to that of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), which was used as an internal control. Oligonucleotide sequences of primer pairs used in real-time PCR are listed in <a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Table S1</a> in <a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">supporting materials</a>. The final concentrations of all primers was 500 nM. The comparative CT (2<sup>−ΔΔct</sup>) method was used to calculate the relative gene expression level.</p></section><section id="s0035"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Liquid chromatography-quadrupole time of flight analysis</h3>
<p id="p0055">A total of 8 primary cell samples and 12 metastatic cell samples were tested using a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Waters Xevo G2, Waters, Manchester, UK) interfaced with ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (Acquity I-Class UPLC, Waters, Milford, MA). The samples were eluted through a C18 BEH UPLC column (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters, Milford, MA), which was kept at 40 °C throughout the experimental run. UPLC grade water with 0.1% formic acid (v/v) was used as mobile phase A and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (v/v) was mobile phase B. The gradient conditions were set as follows: 0–1 min: 5% B; 1–11 min: 5–100% B; 11–13 min: 100% B; 13–13.5 min: 100–5% B; 13.5–15 min: 5% B. The mobile phase flow rate was fixed at 0.3 mL/min. The sample injection sequence was randomly organized, and each sample was injected 3 times with an injection volume of 7 μL. The quality control (QC) sample was created by pooling equal volume of each test sample. Before the analytical sample injection, QC samples were injected five times to condition the column. Also, a QC sample was inserted into the injection sequence at every 10th analytical sample to ensure consistency of system performance and data quality. An evaluation of the variation of QC samples can be found in the <a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Supplementary Material</a>. For the features identified and discussed below, the coefficients of variation (CV) are also included in the <a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Supplementary Material</a>s.</p>
<p id="p0060">Mass spectra were obtained in the positive mode using MS<sup>E</sup> function (low energy: off; high energy: ramp from 20 to 50 eV) over <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> range of 100–1200. Potentials of 2.8 kV and 35 V were applied to the capillary and sample cone, respectively. The flow rate of cone gas was 50 L/h with the desolvation gas of 1000 L/h and the source temperature was 140 °C. The mass domain was calibrated online with a lockspray solution of leucine enkephalin (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 556.2771) infused into the system every 45 s. The sample data were then recorded in centroid mode with a scan time of 0.1 s.</p></section><section id="s0040"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. Data processing and reduction</h3>
<p id="p0065">The raw experimental data were first processed using Progenesis QI (Non-Linear Dynamic, Durham, NC), which aided TIC-based chromatogram alignment, peak picking, and deconvolution using the default software settings. Following these steps, a master table that included <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values, retention times, and intensities, was exported from Progenesis for further statistical analysis using Metaboanalyst <a href="#b0115" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0115">[23]</a>. The data imported into Metaboanalyst was subjected to filtering using the interquantile range (IQR) method, normalization by a pooled sample of the primary cancer cell group, and transformation with the pareto scaling method. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was applied to evaluate the differences between sample groups and to identify outliers. Fold change evaluation and t-tests were used to identify features that were differentially expressed across sample groups (metastatic vs. primary).</p></section><section id="s0045"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Metabolite identification</h3>
<p id="p0070">Features with p-values less than 0.05 and intensity fold changes greater than 1.5 across sample group comparisons were chosen as the significant features distinguishing primary and metastatic melanoma cell lines. The screened features were then ranked based on their intensities. In order to obtain reasonable MS/MS spectra, the top 70 significant features with the highest intensities were subjected to a second round of mass analysis focusing on targeted MS/MS with the collision energy ramping from 5 to 65 eV. Besides targeted MS/MS, the data dependent acquisition (DDA) mode was also employed. For DDA, the MS survey was conducted over the range of 50–1000 Da with a scan time of 0.2 s. Five precursor ions were selected for MS/MS in each MS survey scan. The low mass collision energy ramped from 5 to 15 eV while the high mass collision energy was from 20 to 45 eV. The fragmentation patterns acquired from targeted MS/MS and DDA experiments were searched against a range of existing metabolite databases including Massbank <a href="#b0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0095">[19]</a>, METLIN <a href="#b0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0090">[18]</a>, mzCloud, LipidBlast <a href="#b9000" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b9000">[46]</a>, MetFrag <a href="#b9005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b9005">[47]</a>, and HMDB <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a> for compound identification. When possible, standards of the identified compounds were also tested on the UPLC-QTOF platform to achieve higher levels of identification confidence. The identification confidence levels used in this manuscript follow the standards proposed by the metabolomics standards initiative (MSI) <a href="#b0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0120">[24]</a>. The putatively identified metabolites in this paper have confidence levels equal to or higher than 2.</p></section><section id="s0050"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Significant feature analysis</h3>
<p id="p0075">Prediction models were built from the significant features list using support vector machine (SVM) and random forest (RF) algorithms, respectively. Multivariate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis <a href="#b0125" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0125">[25]</a> integrated in Metaboanlayst was used to evaluate the classification performance of the identified features. The ROC curves reported also include a 95% confidence band. The prediction accuracy was also computed based upon 100 cross-validations.</p></section><section id="s0055"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.7. Pathway analysis</h3>
<p id="p0080">The <em>m</em>/<em>z</em>, retention time, p-value, and t-statistic information generated from Metaboanalyst were analyzed by <em>mummichog</em>
<a href="#b0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0130">[26]</a>, a pathway analysis package written in the Python programming language. <em>Mummichog</em> deduces the most probable metabolite identifications by mapping the possible compounds (based on the match of the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values) onto the range of known metabolic networks and reports a likelihood list of the top affected pathways. The default settings in <em>mummichog</em> were used when conducting the pathway analysis (cutoff p-value: 0.05, network model: human, mass measurement error: 10 ppm).</p></section></section><section id="s0060"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results and discussion</h2>
<section id="s0065"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Multivariate analysis</h3>
<p id="p0085">A description of the samples investigated in this study are outlined in <a href="#t0005" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>. In total, 8 primary cell samples and 12 metastatic cell samples were tested, with approximately 10<sup>6</sup> cells used for each sample. Pairwise comparison was conducted between the primary and metastatic melanoma cell lines using partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). Clear separation between different sample groups could be observed in the scores plots (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>). Close inspection of the raw data revealed an injector malfunction for two sample replicates (i.e., Meta #10 and Primary #5), which were excluded from the scores plot. In <a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>, approximately 30% of the variance was explained by the first two principle component axes with a R2 value of 0.979 and a Q2 value of 0.969. There are two possible reasons supporting the low explained variance percentage. The first possible reason is that a high degree of metabolic similarity is expected between the primary and metastatic tumor cells with subtle difference providing the bulk of the statistical weight used for category distinction. The second possible reason is related to assumptions of the PLS-DA technique. This approach assumes the data being analyzed has the characteristic of normality and linearity. The complexity of the data obtained from our experiments may not fully meet this criterion, and thus impedes PLS-DA from capturing the overall variability. Nevertheless, this approach serves as an informative and valid tool to examine differences, as indicated by the R2 and Q2 metrics.</p>

<p id="p0090">The significant features were chosen based on p-value (i.e., &lt;0.05) and fold changes (i.e., &lt;1.5) across sample groups. Following feature selection, significant features were identified through targeted MS/MS and fast DDA. In total, 12 significant features were putatively identified with a confidence level ≥ 2 <a href="#b0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0120">[24]</a>. Of the identified features, the feature identified by a <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> of 205.098 and retention time of 2.86 min had the highest confidence level (level 1) matching with the tryptophan standard tested on the UPLC-MS platform in our lab (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>a). <a href="#t0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a> summarizes the information of identified features including adduct type, mass error, fold change, p-value, false discovery rate (FDR), and compound identity. The metabolites identified and listed in <a href="#t0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a> include amino acids, glycerophospholipids, and acylcarnitines, to name a few classes. <a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>b–d shows three selected results of experimentally obtained tandem mass spectra matched to the archived results found in the metabolite databases. The remaining experimental MS/MS spectra and corresponding database matches can be found in the <a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Supplementary Material</a>s (<a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Fig. S1</a>). All of the identified features listed in <a href="#t0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a> had reasonable database match scores (i.e., MassBank &gt; 0.6, Metlin &gt; 50, mzCloud &gt; 75) and mass error (i.e., &lt;5 ppm). The corresponding box plots of the selected features are displayed in <a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a> and <a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Supplementary Material</a>s (<a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Fig. S2</a>). The abundances reported on the y-axis of box plots were the results scaled by Parteo-scaling method integrated into Metaboanalyst, which gives rise to the negative values. All of the identified features listed in <a href="#t0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a> showed significantly elevated levels in the metastatic melanoma cell lines, implying the corresponding metabolic pathways were altered relative to the control. Such pathways may have biological implications for the survival and dissemination of metastatic cancer cells, which will be discussed in the following section.</p>

Significant feature analysis</h3>
<p id="p0100">Support vector machine (SVM) and random forest (RF) were used to develop models based on the 12 identified metabolites that distinguish the metastatic samples from the primary cell line. ROC curve analysis was used to assess model performance, as described in the experimental section. The 12 identified metabolites collectively demonstrated the capacity to readily distinguish the primary vs. metastatic comparison set with AUC = 1 and CI = 1–1 by SVM and RF, respectively (<a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Fig. S4</a>). The confusion matrix produced from RF and SVM, respectively, through 100 cross-validations, is summarized in <a href="#t0015" class="usa-link">Table 3</a>. ‘0’ represents ‘metastatic’ while ‘1’ represents ‘primary’ in the confusion matrix. The predictive accuracy is upwards of 100% (i.e., all the primary and metastatic samples are correctly classified as ‘primary’ and ‘metastatic’, respectively). However, caution against over-interpretation is warranted given the limited sample size. Overfitting can occur, harming the reliability of the model. Future studies with larger sample sizes and additional cell lines will be needed to confirm the predictive ability of the identified metabolites.</p>

<p id="p0105">Pathway analysis was performed by <em>mummichog</em>, a metabolomic network analysis package. Different from the conventional pathway analysis program, which needs the metabolite identification prior to data input<em>, mummichog</em> leverages the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> features and metabolic network by mapping the possible metabolites on the pathways, which can integrate metabolite identification and pathway analysis into one step. <a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a> demonstrates the significantly affected metabolic pathways identified by <em>mummichog</em>. All of the significantly perturbed pathways had a p-value less than 0.05, of which the glycerophospholipid metabolism was the most affected pathway followed by the carnitine shuttle pathway. Some of the identified compounds are also involved in the significant pathways. For example, carnitine, propionyl-carnitine, and L-palmitoylcarnitine, all demonstrating remarkably higher levels in the metastatic cells, are involved in carnitine shuttle metabolism, which is prominently featured in <a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>. In addition, phosphocholine, Lyso-PAF-C-16 and Lyso-PAF-C-18 are primary participants in glycerophospholipid metabolism which gives rise to the low p-value of this pathway, as reported by <em>mummichog</em>. Differential levels of valine, leucine, and isoleucine were also observed suggesting that these pathways were also impacted. In addition, tryptophan, which serves as a precursor in the production of serotonin and melatonin, is involved into tryptophan pathway prominently featured in our analysis.</p>

<p id="p0110">The high fatality rate of melanoma is largely attributed to its rapid metastasis and the difficulties associated with early diagnosis. The aim of current effort is to provide a preliminary examination of the metabolites and their corresponding pathways that are affected as melanoma cells progress to malignancy, with the hope of contributing to improved early detection efforts and, perhaps, providing <a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">supporting data</a> for hypotheses aimed at developing novel therapeutic agents. We applied an untargeted metabolomics method to investigate metabolic differences between metastatic and primary melanoma cell lines on a UPLC-MS platform. Our work has identified 12 metabolites that displayed statistically differing levels in the tested samples and demonstrated remarkable discriminating power between cell lines. These metabolites could be used to aid understanding of underlying mechanisms of melanoma progression and be used as diagnostic metabolites to monitor melanoma cancer progression.</p>
<p id="p0115">To place our observations in context, 3 of the 4 amino acids we identified as differentially expressed diagnostic metabolites (i.e., tryptophan, valine, and leucine) were also reported in a previous study with a similar upregulated trend when comparing metastatic melanoma stomach tissue with normal tissue by NMR <a href="#b0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0065">[13]</a>. Additionally, these three amino acids are regarded as important diagnostic metabolites in various metabolomics studies on different cancers: Alberice et al. <a href="#b0140" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0140">[28]</a> reported that the level of tryptophan in urine sample was closely associated with relapse in bladder cancer patients. Additionally, Mayers et al. <a href="#b0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0145">[29]</a> found a positive association of pancreatic adenocarcinoma with branched chain amino acids, such as leucine, isoleucine, and valine, by analyzing the plasma samples from a large group of cancer patients (n = 454), against healthy controls (n = 200). As mentioned previously, tryptophan, an essential amino acid integral to the production of melatonin and serotonin, was also shown in the current study to be present at much higher concentrations in the metastatic melanoma cell line. The increased level of tryptophan, as suggested by Wang et al., <a href="#b0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0065">[13]</a> may be due to the enhanced protein breakdown, which matched with our observation on other amino acids (e.g., leucine and valine). The increased levels of tryptophan could also feed the kynurenine pathway, where tryptophan is degraded by indoleamine 2,3-dixygenase (IDO) <a href="#b0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0150">[30]</a>. Enhanced activity of IDO has been related to tumoral immunoresistance, a process whereby tumor cells escape from T-cell attack due to impeded T-cell proliferation caused by IDO and accumulated tryptophan catabolites <a href="#b0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0150">[30]</a>, <a href="#b0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0155">[31]</a>, <a href="#b0160" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0160">[32]</a>. The upregulation of tryptophan in metastatic melanoma cells may agree with the activation of IDO to avoid tryptophan depletion, putting downward pressure on T-cell proliferation, and minimizing the immunoresponse, thereby creating a greater opportunity for cellular metastasis.</p>
<p id="p0120">Tyrosine, which we found to exhibit elevated levels in the metastatic cell line in this study, plays an essential role in multiple biological processes. It not only acts as a building block for peptide synthesis, but also serves as the precursor of the pigments produced in melanocytes: melanins, eumelanins, and pheomelanins <a href="#b0165" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0165">[33]</a>. The first step of pigment production is the transformation of tyrosine to dopaquinone by the enzyme, tyrosinase. The abnormally high level of tyrosine in melanoma cancer cells implies inhibited activity of the pigment production pathway. Utilizing intravital imaging, Pinner et al. observed a similar effect, finding metastatic melanoma to be in a low-pigment state during dissemination, a process controlled by transforming growth factor (TGF)β2 <a href="#b0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0170">[34]</a>. Activated TGFβ2 can suppress pigment production while increasing cell motility, which would favor dissemination and explain why the metastatic melanoma is poorly pigmented during dispersion <a href="#b0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0170">[34]</a>. Interestingly, after colonizing a site, metastatic melanoma can switch to a high-pigment state, adopting a differentiated phenotype. It is also worthy to note the work by Pelayo et al. <a href="#b0175" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0175">[35]</a> that details the critical role of tyrosine and phenylalaine in melanoma cells. Specifically, they report that the metastatic phenotype of B16BL6 melanoma cells is inhibited in a tyrosine and phenylalanine deprived environment, which were related to the lower secretion of plasminogen activators and increased plasminogen activator inhibitors.</p>
<p id="p0125">Notably, three identified metabolites, including carnitine and its derivatives, primarily acylcarnitines, all appeared in the carnitine shuttle pathway in the analysis by <em>mummichog</em>. The uniform upregulation of carnitine and acylcarnitine has strong implications for the activation of the carnitine shuttle pathway, which is important to the survival of metastatic melanoma cells <a href="#b0180" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0180">[36]</a>. Following detachment of the primary tumor, the glucose transport of the disseminating metastatic cells is often impaired, causing an ATP deficit, which can eventually induces anoikis, a type of apoptosis <a href="#b0185" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0185">[37]</a>. In order to survive, the metastatic cancer cells are required to overcome this deficit. One way to compensate is to seek additional nutritional inputs such as fatty acids to substitute for glucose in ATP production <a href="#b0190" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0190">[38]</a>. It is the carnitine shuttle pathway that aids in the transportation of fatty acids from cytosol to the mitochondrial matrix. During this process the fatty acid is first attached to carnitine to produce fatty acylcarnitine, which can pass through the mitochondrial membrane with the help of a transferase. At the end of transport, and following release, the fatty acid enters into the β-oxidation pathway for ATP production, while the carnitine travels back to the cytosol to take part in the next round of transport <a href="#b0165" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0165">[33]</a>. The three identified metabolites in this study are postulated to be intermediates generated by the carnitine shuttle pathway. Piszcz et al<em>.</em>
<a href="#b0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0195">[39]</a> also describes increased levels of acylcarnitine in aggressive chronic lymphocytic leukaemia compared to indolent state in the serum samples <a href="#b0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0195">[39]</a>. Previous studies have reported that the expression of CPT1C, an isoform of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1), which conjugates carnitine with fatty acids to deliver them to mitochondria, can promote ATP production in cancer cells and further promote cell survival under metabolic stress, such as glucose deprivation <a href="#b0200" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0200">[40]</a>, <a href="#b0205" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0205">[41]</a>. The findings in this current study provide independent metabolite-level evidence to the enhanced carnitine shuttle pathway activity that supports metastatic cell survival.</p>
<p id="p0130">Another upregulated metabolite in <a href="#t0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>, phosphocholine, is involved in glycerophospholipid metabolism, the most perturbed metabolic pathway identified by <em>mummichog</em>. The increased level of phosphocholine, as observed in the current study, has been widely established as a hallmark of cancer malignant transformation <a href="#b0210" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0210">[42]</a>, <a href="#b0215" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0215">[43]</a>. The choline kinase (CHK) is known to play a role in the biosynthetic pathways that catalyze the production of phosphocholine. CHK is encoded by two genes including choline kinase-α (CHKA) and choline kinase-β (CHKB), and it has been reported that increases in CHKA expression contribute to enhanced CHK activity, which is essential for phosphocholine synthesis <a href="#b0215" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0215">[43]</a>, <a href="#b0220" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0220">[44]</a>. Our RT-qPCR experiment demonstrated that the expression level of CHKA in metastatic melanoma cells is more than 7-fold higher than in a primary cell line, which is consistent with the elevated levels of phosphocholine observed in the present metabolomics portion of this study (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>). When correlating with cancer patients’ gene expression data in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), our supposition that phosphocholine can be used as a diagnostic metabolite to monitor melanoma progression is further supported. As shown in <a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Fig. S5</a>, the survival rate of melanoma patients inversely correlates with the level of gene expression (p = 0.057 for CHKA), with low expression of CHKA, implying lower phosphocholine levels, related to higher survival rate.</p>

The expression levels of CHKA and PEMT were significantly higher in the metastatic cells studied compared to the primary cells.</p></figcaption></figure><p id="p0135">In a recent research on breast cancer tissue samples, Hilvo et al<em>.</em> found that the elevated phospholipid composition, including phosphatidylcholines in the cell membrane, is associated with increased tumor grade and poor patient survival rate <a href="#b0225" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0225">[45]</a>, which is in agreement with our observations of a significantly upregulated trend for the 12:0/13:0 form of phosphatidylcholine (PC) in the metastatic melanoma samples (<a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>). Phosphatidylethanolamine N-Methyltransferase (PEMT) is crucial for phosphatidylcholine biosynthesis, where it catalyzes three sequential steps to generate phosphatidylcholine. In agreement with significantly increased concentration of phosphatidylcholine from the metabolomics results (<a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>), the level of the PEMT gene was also found to be markedly elevated in the metastatic cells (<a href="#f0025" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a>) and was statistically correlated with patient survival rate with a p-value of 0.04 in the TCGA database (<a href="#s0110" class="usa-link">Fig. S5</a>). The gene expression data presented is one possible explanation for the experimental observations in this study. Although the p-value for the CHKA trend was slightly above the standard threshold of 0.05, the combined information suggests a link between the experimentally observed trend and the gene expression data. Subsequent investigation will be necessary to confirm any such relationship.</p>
<p id="p0140">Taking advantage of a state-of-the-art, cell-based untargeted metabolomics methodology, this study identified 12 metabolites that were significantly different between metastatic and primary melanoma cells. These identified metabolites may contribute to a metabolic biomarker profile for monitoring melanoma progression. Although these results are promising, it is important to note that this study is preliminary and does have limitations. The limited sample size weakens the statistical power of our data analysis. The candidate metabolites will require validation in a larger group of cell samples and patients with melanoma. Considering the complexity of cell metabolite profiles, it needs to be acknowledged that no single available extraction approach and analytical method is suitable for all of the metabolites. It is entirely possible that the extraction system (methanol and water, 80:20, v/v) and analytical column (C18) used in this study may have included a chemical bias and failed to capture a portion of the non-polar metabolites. Experiments using non-polar solvents (e.g., chloroform) coupled to hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) could provide additional diagnostic metabolite candidates for consideration, which would complement the results of the current study.</p></section><section id="s0090"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">5. Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0145">Using a UPLC-MS as part of a cell-based, untargeted metabolomics approach we investigated the association of altered metabolite profiles with progression of melanoma (i.e., primary vs. metastatic). A total of 12 diagnostic metabolites that include amino acids, carnitine and its derivatives, and lipids were identified. In attempt to align these identified metabolite markers with meaning in the context of the metastatic progression being investigated we noted that (1) the change in tryptophan levels may be related to tumoral immunoresistance, (2) tyrosine levels are known to impact pigment production and may be related to cellular behavior (e.g., dissemination), (3) the levels of carnitine and acylcarnitine can indicate the activation of the carnitine shuttle, a process vital to the survival of metastatic cancer cells, and (4) the phosphatidylcholine level can be indicative of cancer malignance, as implied by the gene expression data in TCGA database. The identification of altered metabolites and their related pathways can broaden our understanding of melanoma progression at the molecular level, and ultimately contribute to the improvement of early diagnosis and the identification of potential therapeutic targets.</p></section><section id="ak005" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
