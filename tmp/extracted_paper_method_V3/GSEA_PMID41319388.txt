>Material and methods   Patients and study design   The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Shanghai Chest Hospital and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. A total of 164 patients with histologically confirmed TETs who underwent surgical resection were included. Eligibility criteria required complete clinical data and signed informed consent. Patients with a history of other malignancies or concurrent systemic diseases were excluded. Among the enrolled patients, a subset of 40 fresh-frozen tumor samples (17 low-risk and 23 high-risk) was selected for proteomic analysis based on sample availability and RNA/protein integrity. None of the patients in this proteomic cohort had received preoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Additionally, six non-TET patients undergoing cardiac surgery were included as controls to establish baseline protein expression profiles. Tumors were classified according to the 2021 WHO classification[ 19 ] into low-risk (types A, AB, and B1) and high-risk (types B2, B3, and thymic squamous cell carcinoma [TSCC]) groups. Demographic and clinical variables were collected, including age, sex, histological subtype, TNM stage according to the 8th edition of the AJCC classification, preoperative treatment history, and resection margin status. Protein extraction, digestion, and desalting   Fresh tumor and adjacent normal tissues were collected during surgery. Samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for proteomic and RNA analyses. For histological evaluation, tissues were fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin and paraffin-embedded.   For proteomic analysis, frozen tissues were lysed in 20 mM HEPES buffer (pH 8.0) containing 0.2 % acid-labile surfactant and protease inhibitors. Tissue homogenization was performed using ceramic beads under cold conditions, followed by 30 min of incubation on ice. Lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 ×  g  for 30 min at 4 °C, and protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay.   Proteins were denatured with 6 M urea, reduced with 5 mM TCEP, and alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide. After dilution to reduce urea concentration below 1 M, sequence-grade trypsin (Promega) was added at a 1:100 enzyme-to-protein ratio. Digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were then desalted using C18 spin columns (Thermo Fisher) and vacuum-dried for subsequent analysis. Mass spectrometry and spectral library construction   Peptides were dissolved in 0.1 % formic acid and analyzed using a Thermo Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer coupled with a Dionex UltiMate 3000 nano-LC system. Peptide separation was performed on a self-packed C18 column (75 µm × 25 cm) using a binary gradient of 0.1 % formic acid in water and 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile, with a 3–30 % Buffer B gradient over 120 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Data-independent acquisition (DIA) was used for quantitative proteomic analysis across a mass range of 400–1200 m /z , applying variable isolation windows to enhance precursor ion coverage. Raw MS files were collected for all samples for spectral matching and quantification.   To construct a spectral library, pooled samples from representative groups were analyzed via data-dependent acquisition (DDA). Peptide identifications were performed against the UniProt human database using Proteome Discoverer, with a 1 % false discovery rate (FDR). The resulting spectra were imported into Spectronaut for spectral library generation, enabling robust peptide identification and quantification from DIA data. Data processing and functional annotation   DIA-MS raw data were processed in Spectronaut for peptide identification and quantification. Protein intensities were normalized across samples, and differential expression analysis was conducted with thresholds of |log₂ fold-change| > 1 and FDR < 0.05. Protein-level quality control was assessed using principal component analysis (PCA) and correlation heatmaps to ensure data consistency. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R. Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were analyzed based on over-representation analysis (ORA), with significance defined by adjusted p-values < 0.05. The background set included all proteins quantified in the DIA dataset. Pathway enrichment results were used to explore biological processes and signaling pathways associated with different TET subtypes. Data visualization was conducted using R packages including ggplot2 and heatmap. Visualization outputs included volcano plots, hierarchical clustering heatmaps, and PCA plots to highlight molecular distinctions between sample groups. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) validation and immunofluorescence   IHC staining was conducted on 5 µm-thick paraffin sections mounted on adhesive slides. Sections were baked at 65 °C for 2 h, deparaffinized with xylene, and rehydrated through graded ethanol to distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed via microwave treatment in citrate-EDTA buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 25 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3 % hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. After blocking with 5 % goat serum, slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary anti-Galectin-7 antibody (Abcam, ab206435). The following day, slides were treated with a secondary antibody (Dako) for 1 h and visualized using DAB chromogen. Counterstaining with hematoxylin was followed by dehydration, clearing, and mounting for evaluation. Whole-slide images were scanned using a Pannoramic MIDI scanner (3DHISTECH). Staining intensity (0–3) and the percentage of positive cells (0–100 %) were scored to calculate a composite IHC score (range: 0–12). Samples were categorized as low (0–4), moderate (5–8), or high (9–12) expressers.   For immunofluorescence, similar antigen retrieval and blocking steps were followed. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Galectin-7 (Abcam, ab206435) and CK19 (Abcam, ab76539). After washing, slides were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 594, Abcam). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Stained sections were visualized using a fluorescence microscope, and images were captured for co-localization analysis. Functional assays   Cell lines and culture conditions   The human thymic epithelial tumor cell line Tc1889 was kindly provided by Professor Alexander Marx from University Hospital Heidelberg. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2. Cell identity was authenticated using short tandem repeat profiling. Generation of galectin-7 overexpression cell line   Lentiviral vectors for Galectin-7 overexpression and corresponding empty controls were obtained from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Tc1889 cells were infected with lentiviral particles in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene, followed by selection with 2 μg/mL puromycin for 7 days to establish stable cell lines. Overexpression efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis, with empty vector-transduced cells serving as controls. Proliferation assay   Tc1889 cell lines overexpressing Galectin-7 and empty vectors were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well. Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (HY-K0301, MCE, China) at 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a BioTek microplate reader. Migration and invasion assays   Confluent Tc1889 cells were cultured in 6-well plates. A sterile pipette tip was used to create a wound, and debris was removed with PBS. Cells were incubated in serum-free medium, and wound closure was observed under a phase-contrast microscope at 0 and 24 h. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software to calculate the percentage of wound closure. Empty and Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers (8-μm pore size, Corning) were used to evaluate invasion. Cells (5 × 10^4) in serum-free medium were placed in the upper chamber, while 10 % FBS medium served as a chemoattractant in the lower chamber. After 48 h of incubation, cells that invaded the membrane were fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a light microscope in five random fields. RNA-Seq and bioinformatics analysis   RNA extraction and quality control   Total RNA was extracted from Tc1889 thymic epithelial tumor cell lines using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were lysed in RLT buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol, and RNA was isolated by column purification. The RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), while RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only samples with RNA Integrity Numbers (RIN) ≥ 7.0 were used for subsequent library preparation. Library preparation and sequencing   RNA-Seq libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Messenger RNA was enriched from total RNA using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation into short sequences. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase, followed by second-strand synthesis to generate double-stranded cDNA. Libraries were indexed, amplified by PCR, and purified to remove adapter dimers. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were subjected to quality control using FastQC (version 0.11.9) to check for sequencing quality, and Trim Galore! (version 0.6.6) was used to remove low-quality bases and adapter contamination. Bioinformatics analysis   Cleaned RNA-Seq reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2, and transcript abundance was quantified with StringTie. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log2 fold-change| > 1 and FDR < 0.05. GO and KEGG pathway analyses were performed to identify significantly enriched biological processes and pathways, with adjusted p-values < 0.05. Visualizations, including volcano plots and heatmaps, were generated using R to highlight DEGs and their functional associations. Results   Patients   This study included 164 patients with TETs and 6 non-TET control patients who underwent cardiac surgery. Among 164 patients with TETs, 40 patients (17 low-risk and 23 high-risk) with available frozen tumor tissues were selected for proteomic analyses. TET cases were classified into low-risk (A/AB/B1 thymomas,  n  = 60) and high-risk groups (B2/B3 thymomas and TSCC,  n  = 104) based on the WHO histologic classification. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort are summarized in  Table 1 . No significant differences were observed in the median age at diagnosis (52 vs. 51 years,  p  = 0.973) or sex distribution between the low-risk and high-risk groups ( p  = 0.105). High-risk TETs were also associated with advanced IASLC/ITMIG T stages (T3/T4: 47.1 % vs. 3.3 %,  p  < 0.001) and a higher incidence of incomplete resections (R1/R2: 22.1 % vs. 3.4 %,  p  = 0.001). Preoperative treatment, including chemotherapy or radiation, was administered more frequently in high-risk patients (16.3 % vs. 1.6 %,  p  = 0.003). Galectin-7 expression levels, assessed by immunohistochemistry, were markedly elevated in high-risk TETs, with 58.7 % of cases showing moderate to strong expression (scores 5–12) compared to 3.4 % in the low-risk group ( p  < 0.001). These findings highlight significant clinical and molecular differences between low-risk and high-risk TETs, emphasizing the distinct biological and pathological characteristics of these subgroups. Galectin-7 as a proteomic biomarker for high-risk TETs   Proteomic profiling was performed on tumor and adjacent normal tissues from 40 TET patients, including TSCC, high-risk TETs (B2/B3 thymomas), low-risk TETs (A/AB/B1 thymomas), and normal thymus tissues. The experimental workflow, including tissue processing, protein extraction, DIA-MS analysis, and data validation, is outlined in  Fig. 1 a . Heatmap analysis demonstrated distinct expression patterns across the sample groups, with Galectin-7 being prominently upregulated in high-risk TETs compared to low-risk TETs and adjacent normal tissues ( Fig. 1 b ). PCA analysis further confirmed clear molecular separation between high-risk and low-risk TETs, with minimal overlap between the two groups ( Fig. 1 c ). Volcano plot analysis highlighted Galectin-7 as one of the most significantly upregulated proteins in high-risk TETs, with a log2 fold-change of 1.5 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 ( Fig. 1 d ). Functional pathway analysis using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that high-risk TETs were significantly enriched in pathways associated with mitochondrion and extracellular matrix constituent. Cell-cell junction and adhesion were significantly downregulated in high-risk TETs ( Fig. 1 e–f ). Galectin-7 expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues ( Fig. 1 g ), and its levels in high-risk TETs were markedly higher than those in low-risk TETs ( Fig. 1 h ). In contrast, low-risk TETs exhibited negligible or no Galectin-7 expression. These differences underscore the potential of Galectin-7 as a biomarker for distinguishing high-risk TETs. These results collectively establish Galectin-7 as a robust biomarker for high-risk TETs, with potential applications in molecular classification and risk stratification. Immunohistochemical validation of galectin-7   Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on thymic epithelial tumor (TET) tissues to validate the differential expression of Galectin-7 observed in proteomic analyses. Paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues revealed significantly higher Galectin-7 expression in tumor samples, with TSCC demonstrating the strongest staining intensity ( Fig. 2 a-b ). Quantitative IHC scoring showed a consistent pattern: high-risk TETs (B2/B3/TSCC) exhibited significantly stronger Galectin-7 expression compared to low-risk TETs (A/AB/B1) and normal thymus tissues ( p  = 0.0097,  Fig. 2 b ). Galectin-7 staining was predominantly localized to the cytoplasm and nucleus of tumor epithelial cells in high-risk TETs, whereas low-risk TETs showed negligible expression ( Fig. 2 c). Notably, TSCC tissues exhibited the most robust and diffuse Galectin-7 staining, correlating with their aggressive clinical behavior ( Fig. 2 c ). Quantitative analysis of IHC scores ( Fig. 2 d ) revealed that Galectin-7 expression in TSCC was significantly higher than in adjacent normal tissues, as well as in A, AB and B1 thymomas ( p  < 0.001). To further quantify Galectin-7 expression across TET subtypes,  Table 2  summarizes the distribution of Galectin-7 IHC scores (0–12), showing significantly higher expression levels in high-risk subtypes compared to low-risk subtypes. Further quantitative comparison of Galectin-7 expression between low-risk (A/AB/B1) and high-risk (B2/B3/TSCC) TETs showed a dramatic increase in expression in high-risk subtypes, consistent with their aggressive biological behavior ( p  < 0.0001,  Fig. 2 e ). In addition to differential staining intensity, co-localization studies revealed specific patterns of Galectin-7 distribution. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that Galectin-7 was strongly co-localized with CK19 in TSCC tumor cells, confirming its epithelial origin and subcellular localization within the nucleus, cytoplasm, and extracellular space ( Fig. 2 f ). In contrast, Galectin-7 expression was notably minimal in type A thymomas, consistent with its negligible IHC staining observed in low-risk TETs. These results confirm that Galectin-7 is significantly overexpressed in high-risk TETs and underscore its potential as a biomarker for tumor aggressiveness and subtype differentiation.   Fig. 2.
