>METHODS   Study Design and Population   We performed HRM on plasma samples from a cross-sectional study of persons with leprosy (n = 39), asymptomatic household contacts (n = 17), and healthy community controls (n = 8). Participants were recruited from Belo Horizonte and Governador Valadares, 2 urban centers in the southeastern state of Minas Gerais, Brazil, from 2 state leprosy reference clinics, Hospital Eduardo de Menezes in Belo Horizonte and CREDEN-PES (Centro de Referência em Doenças Endêmicas e Programs Especiais) in Governador Valadares. Governador Valadares has a high annual new case detection rate of leprosy, found to be 28.4/100 000 in 2019 [ 20 ]. Data Collection   Between June 2018 and December 2019, participants aged 18 years and older were recruited at the 2 dermatologic clinics. One 16 year old, an individual with PB leprosy, was also recruited at CREDEN-PES. A case of leprosy was defined as an individual with newly diagnosed leprosy by a dermatologist, either by clinical diagnosis, skin biopsy, skin smear/bacillary index, or combination of these. Bacillary index was determined by microscopic examination of skin scrapings from the ears and elbows of all cases. Those with bacteria detected were positive and those without bacteria were deemed bacillary negative. Ridley-Jopling criteria on histopathology was used to differentiate the different clinical forms of leprosy in those who received a biopsy: indeterminate, tuberculoid, borderline tuberculoid, borderline borderline, borderline lepromatous, and lepromatous [ 21 ]. If a biopsy was not done, then the dermatologist classified the disease as indeterminate, tuberculoid, borderline, or lepromatous per the Madrid classification [ 22 ]. MB was defined as per World Health Organization guidelines as ≥ 5 lesions and as PB for less than 5 lesions and negative bacillary index [ 23 ]. Participants were recruited at their dermatologist visit and were eligible if they had not started multidrug therapy. Controls were recruited from the Hospital Eduardo de Menezes Dermatology clinic and were classified as household contacts (healthy individuals who accompanied cases) or healthy controls. The healthy controls were recruited from the dermatologic clinic and were eligible if they presented with a noninfectious, nonautoimmune, minor skin ailment. Household contacts were examined for leprosy before enrollment. Pregnant women were excluded.   After informed consent, participants were administered a questionnaire on clinical, demographic, and household information. Blood samples were drawn and centrifuged to separate plasma. Plasma samples were then immediately frozen and stored at −80°C until they were shipped on dry ice to the Clinical Biomarkers Laboratory at Emory University, Atlanta, GA. High-Resolution Metabolomics   Deidentified plasma samples were randomized by laboratory personnel who were blinded to the study data. Thawed plasma (65 μL) was treated with 130 μL acetonitrile (2:1, v/v) containing an internal isotopic standard mixture (3.5 μL/sample), as previously described [ 5 ]. The internal standard mix for quality control consisted of 14 stable isotopic chemicals covering a broad range of small molecules [ 5 ]. Samples were mixed and placed on ice for 30 minutes before centrifugation to remove protein and then the supernatant was transferred to low-volume autosampler vials maintained at 4°C. They were then analyzed in triplicate using a Q Exactive HF Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Dual hydrophilic interaction and C18 chromatography was used (TARGA 2.1 × 10 cm; Higgins Analytical) with a formic acid/acetonitrile gradient in positive and negative electrospray ionization mode respectively over a scan range of 85 to 1275 m/z and stored as Raw files [ 24 ]. Data were extracted and aligned using apLCMS [ 25 ] and xMSanalyzer [ 26 ] with each feature defined by specific m/z value, retention time, and integrated ion intensity [ 24 ]. Three technical replicates were performed for each plasma sample and median summarized [ 27 ]. Liquid chromatography was performed in triplicates of each sample for rigor of measurement. Data were filtered before downstream analysis to exclude m/z features having a median coefficient of variation within technical replicates ≥75%, and only samples with Pearson correlation within technical replicates ≥ 0.7 were used for downstream analysis [ 28 ].   Selected metabolites were then identified and quantified using ion dissociation methods (MS/MS) relative to authentic reference standards [ 29 ]. These metabolites were chosen from the pathway enrichment analyses to compare specific metabolites of interests within the significant pathways. In addition, given the influence of tryptophan metabolism on immunity for both tuberculosis and leprosy, median concentrations of tryptophan were determined and compared across cases (MB vs PB) [ 10 ,  12 ]. Statistical Analysis   Metabolite intensity values and concentrations were compared using R statistical software (R Foundation, version 3.5.0). Metabolite intensity values and concentrations were log 2 -transformed and compared between groups using a linear regression model controlling for age and sex. In the untargeted analysis, a false discovery rate (q < 0.2) was used to correct for multiple hypothesis testing and select metabolites for downstream analysis [ 30 ]. Row-normalized heat maps with 2-way hierarchical clustering were constructed with these intensity results to demonstrate clustering across individual samples and metabolites. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was performed using  mummichog , a Python-based informatics tool that leverages the organization of metabolic networks to predict functional changes in metabolic pathway activity [ 28 ,  31 ,  32 ].  P  values less than .05 were considered statistically significance for targeted metabolites and in the metabolic pathway analysis. Study Approval   This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Emory University (Atlanta, GA), Universidade Federal de Juiz de Fora (Juiz de Fora, Brazil), Hospital Eduardo de Menezes (Belo Horizonte, Brazil), and Faculdade da Saúde e Écologia Humana (Vespasiano, Brazil). All subjects provided written informed consent, and in the case of the 16 year old, a parent provided written informed consent and the participant assented. RESULTS   Clinical and Demographic Characteristics   Sixty-four individuals from Minas Gerais, Brazil, were enrolled in the study with 39 newly diagnosed cases of leprosy and 25 healthy controls. The majority of participants were adults, with one 16.5-year-old individual. About half of cases were female while 84% of controls were female ( Table 1 ). Of the cases, 24 (62%) were MB and of the asymptomatic controls, 17 (68%) were household contacts of leprosy cases. Leprosy Cases Versus Controls   With  mummichog  pathway analyses, multiple metabolic pathways were found to be statistically significantly different between leprosy cases and controls. Metabolic pathways associated with prostaglandins, linoleate, arachidonic acid (AA), and glycophospholipid metabolism, as well as fatty acid synthesis and activation were statistically significantly different ( P  < .05) in cases compared to healthy controls ( Figure 2 ) as were several micronutrients: vitamin A (retinol), vitamin C, vitamin D3, and vitamin E. Within the retinol pathway, retinol, 9-retinal, and 4-hydroxy-retinyl acetate were decreased in cases versus controls ( Figure 3 ). Several AA downstream metabolites were lower in cases compared to controls, including prostaglandin G2 (PGG2), leukotriene A4, thromboxane A2, and 8,9-dihydroxyeicosatrienoic acid (8,9-DHET) ( Figure 4 ). Lastly, carnitine shuttle metabolism differed between cases and controls ( Figure 2 ).   Figure 2. DISCUSSION   This exploratory HRM study is the first to simultaneously show differences across a host of metabolic pathways in leprosy, from fatty acids to energy metabolism, micronutrients, and tryptophan, all pathways at the intersection of metabolism and immunity. We found that multiple metabolic pathways differentiated leprosy cases from controls, indicating leprosy disease state may be a driver of metabolic differences relevant to disease pathophysiology.   Differences in fatty acid and eicosanoid metabolism figured prominently between controls and cases as well as between PB and MB disease ( Figure 2  and  Figure 5 ). Intracellular accumulation of lipids like AA in infected cells is a hallmark of MB disease, with foamy macrophages noted in histopathological samples [ 7 ,  8 ,  34 ]. Prior studies have shown the increase in exogenous lipid uptake as well pathways producing de novo fatty acids and phospholipids in infected cells, especially those at the lepromatous end of the spectrum [ 9 ,  35 ,  36 ]. We found that cases had lower median concentrations of several AA metabolites than controls when compared directly to each other ( Figure 4 ). PGG2, as the direct byproduct of oxidation of AA, is a precursor of PGE2 and thromboxane A2 [ 37 ] while 8,9-DHET is a product of AA metabolism through a different mechanism, the CYP450 epoyxgenase pathway [ 38 ].
