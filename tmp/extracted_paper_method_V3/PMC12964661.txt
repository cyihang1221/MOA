</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="Abs1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Background</h3>
<p id="Par1"><em>Dysosma versipellis (DV)</em> has pharmacological activity but causes toxicity, especially in nontargeted tissues such as the colon. Understanding its underlying mechanism is crucial for safety.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p id="Par2">UPLC–MS/MS was used to identify <em>DV</em> extract components. Active compounds (247) and targets (1901) were screened from databases; colon toxicity targets (1872) were obtained from GeneCards. The core targets were identified via network pharmacology and validated by molecular docking. The rats received <em>DV</em> extract for 5 days; the colon tissues were subjected to histopathology, transcriptomics, and metabolomics. Integrated omics was performed using KEGG/HMDB enrichment.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p id="Par3">Ten hub targets (GAPDH, AKT1, TP53, ACTB, TNF, IL6, ALB, EGFR, INS, and STAT3) were identified. 4′-Demethylpodophyllotoxin bound strongly to GAPDH/AKT1. KEGG revealed PI3K–Akt, lipid/atherosclerosis, and MAPK as the top enriched pathways. In vivo, <em>DV</em> damaged the colonic epithelium (shedding, bleeding). Transcriptomic analysis confirmed that PI3K–Akt/MAPK enrichment mediated cell death. Metabolomics revealed 414 differentially abundant metabolites, implicating amino sugar/nucleotide sugar metabolism. Integrated analysis highlighted shared disruptions in pancreatic secretion, protein digestion/absorption, and bile secretion.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p id="Par4">Oral <em>DV</em> metabolism releases 4′-demethylpodophyllotoxin, activating the PI3K–Akt/MAPK pathway in the colon epithelium and causing transcriptomic–metabolic dysregulation and epithelial necrosis. These findings inform clinical safety and detoxification strategies.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h3>
<p>The online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03789-5.</p></section><section id="kwd-group1" lang="en" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> <em>Dysosma versipellis</em> ethanol extract, 4′-Demethylpodophyllotoxin, PI3K–Akt, MAPK, Safe medication, Colon epithelial cell toxicity</p></section></section><section id="Sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="Par5"><em>Dysosma versipellis (DV)</em> is an important medicinal plant with anticancer activity [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>]; however, although it inhibits cancer cells, it is strongly toxic to normal tissues, which limits its clinical application. Its toxic side effects mainly stem from the cytotoxicity of lignans [<a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>] and hepatotoxicity [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>] and the inhibitory activity of flavonoids on neuraminidase [<a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>]. Following oral administration, <em>DV</em> components are metabolized in the intestine, allowing absorption of toxic substances that cause tissue damage, which is crucial for clinical safety. However, the mechanism underlying its toxicity is not yet clear. <em>DV</em> has a complex composition that can be separated into various active substances [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>], and its effects on the intestinal epithelium are multifaceted. This study aimed to elucidate the toxicity mechanism and biological processes of <em>DV</em> in the intestinal epithelium.</p>
<p id="Par6">Ultraperformance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC–MS/MS) technology can extensively identify the chemical components of <em>DV</em>, which is beneficial for subsequent network bioinformatics research. We predicted the main targets, regulated signalling pathways, and biological processes associated with the toxicity of <em>DV</em> and verified their reliability through experiments in rats. Transcriptomic technology can elucidate the mechanisms of action of certain toxic substances on organisms [<a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>]. In this study, we performed high-throughput sequencing on colon tissue samples from each group of rats and then conducted statistical analysis to filter out differentially expressed genes. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was further used to identify the main affected signalling pathways, thereby validating the results of the network bioinformatics analysis. Metabolomics technology is used to explain the relationships between detected metabolites and biological processes or biological states [<a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>]. We screened differentially abundant metabolites based on the metabolite detection data of colon tissues from different groups of rats and then used the KEGG database and HMDB for source tracing analysis, ultimately enriching significantly affected metabolic pathways. By mapping transcriptomic and metabolomic data to the KEGG pathway, further enrichment analysis revealed common pathways, and a gene–pathway–metabolite interaction network diagram was constructed. This study comprehensively analysed the toxicological mechanisms of <em>DV</em> on the colonic epithelium, identified key action targets and signalling pathways, and screened critical regulatory genes and metabolic pathways, providing a basis for the safe clinical use of <em>DV</em> and the development of detoxification drugs in the future.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Composition analysis of <em>DV</em>
</h3>
<p id="Par7">The components of the 70% ethanol extract of <em>DV</em> were profiled by UPLC–MS/MS. The data collection instrument system used was an ExionLC™ AD (<a href="https://sciex.com.cn/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://sciex.com.cn/</a>). The liquid phase conditions were as follows: chromatography column: Agilent SB-C18 (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm); mobile phase A: ultrapure water with 0.1% formic acid; and mobile phase B: acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient programme was as follows: 0.00 min, 5% B; from 0.00 to 9.00 min, linear increase to 95% B; maintained at 95% B until 10.00 min; from 10.00 to 11.10 min, linear decrease to 5% B; and maintained at 5% B until 14.00 min. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, the column temperature was 40 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The mass spectrometry conditions were as follows: electrospray ionization (ESI) source temperature: 500 °C; ion spray voltage (IS): 5500 V (positive ion mode)/− 4500 V (negative ion mode); ion source gas I (GSI): 50 psi; ion source gas II (GSII): 60 psi; curtain gas (CUR): 25 psi; collision-induced dissociation (CAD) gas pressure set to high; and QQQ scanning used MRM mode with the collision gas (nitrogen) set to medium pressure. Through optimization of the declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE), the optimal DP and CE values for each MRM transition were determined. Based on the retention times of the metabolites, specific MRM transitions were monitored in scheduled time windows.</p></section><section id="Sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Animal experiments and sample processing</h3>
<p id="Par8">SPF-grade female Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (56 days old) were purchased from the Hubei Provincial Experimental Animal Research Center (Licence No.: SCXK (Hubei) 2020-0018). The experimental protocol was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Review Committee of Hubei Minzu University (Approval No.: [2024]088). The rats were housed in the animal centre of Hubei Minzu University under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 22–24 °C; relative humidity: 50–60%). The 70% ethanol extract of <em>DV</em> was dissolved in water to a concentration of 33.75 mg/mL. After 5 days of acclimatization, the rats were randomly assigned to a control group (<em>n</em> = 6) or an experimental group (<em>n</em> = 6). Based on the adult human equivalent dose of <em>DV</em> and its extraction yield in 70% ethanol, the experimental group rats received a daily gavage dose of 0.5 mL/100 g body weight for 5 consecutive days, whereas the control group received an equivalent volume of water. Daily measurements of body weight, faecal occult blood, and stool consistency were recorded for each group, and the Disease Activity Index (DAI) was calculated using the formula DAI = (weight loss score + stool consistency score + bloody stool score)/3 [<a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>]. One hour after the final gavage administration, the rats were euthanized. Colon tissues were excised, rinsed with saline solution, and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for subsequent pathological examination. Colon tissues from three randomly selected rats per group were collected for transcriptomic analysis. Additionally, colon tissues from all the rats were subjected to metabolomic analysis.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Network bioinformatics analysis</h3>
<p id="Par9"><strong>Target prediction:</strong> For substances detected in the <em>DV</em> component analysis, oral bioavailability (OB) and drug likeness (DL) were assessed using TCMSP (<a href="https://old.tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.ph" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://old.tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.ph</a>). Active substances meeting the screening criteria (OB &gt; 30 and DL &gt; 0.18) were selected, and their corresponding target information was obtained [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>]. SWISSADME (<a href="http://www.swissadme.ch/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.swissadme.ch/</a>) was used for further substance screening. The pharmacokinetics analysis focused on GI absorption, and the druglikeness analysis evaluated the Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge criteria. Substances showing high GI absorption and meeting at least two of the five druglikeness criteria were retained. Subsequently, SwissTargetPrediction (<a href="http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/</a>) was used to predict the targets of these screened substances. Additionally, the SymMap database (<a href="https://www.symmap.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.symmap.org/</a>) provided target information for the detected substances. Target information identified by the three databases was used to obtain the target profile for each DV component. Using the keyword “colon epithelial toxicity”, we identified disease-related targets from GeneCards (<a href="https://www.genecards.org/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.genecards.org/</a>), with a score of 10 as a screening criterion [<a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>].</p>
<p id="Par10"><strong>Construction of intersection genes and core targets:</strong> Venn diagrams identified intersecting targets between <em>DV</em> and colon epithelial toxicity. These targets were analysed using the STRING database (<a href="https://string-db.org" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://string-db.org</a>) to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network, with the top 10 highest degree nodes defined as core targets [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>].</p>
<p id="Par11"><strong>GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses:</strong> Using intersecting genes from STRING, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses in R 4.3.3 (with DOSE, clusterProfiler, and pathview packages) [<a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>]. Significantly enriched terms (adjusted <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) were visualized via bubble plots.</p>
<p id="Par12"><strong>Molecular docking:</strong> Protein crystal structures were retrieved from the PDB, and the 3D coordinates of podophyllotoxin were downloaded from PubChem (CAS number). Docking was performed with AutoDock Vina (v1.2.3) and visualized in PyMOL (v2.5.5). The lower the binding energy is, the stronger the affinity between the small molecule and the protein. A binding energy value less than − 5 kcal/mol indicates strong affinity [<a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>].</p></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Colon tissue transcriptome analysis</h3>
<p id="Par13">Transcriptome analysis was performed on three rat colon tissue samples per group (control and experimental). The RNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer, and integrity was assessed using a Qsep400 bioanalyzer. PolyA-tailed mRNA was enriched with oligo(dT) magnetic beads. RNA fragmentation buffer was added to generate short fragments. First-strand cDNA synthesis uses random hexamers, followed by second-strand synthesis with buffer, dNTPs, and DNA polymerase. Double-stranded cDNA was purified with magnetic beads and then processed for end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. Size-selected libraries were PCR-amplified and quantified by Qubit. The library insert size was verified by a fragment analyser. Qualified libraries were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina platform using the <em>Rattus norvegicus</em> reference genome (Ensembl: mRatBN7.2.109; <a href="https://asia.ensembl.org/Rattus_norvegicus" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://asia.ensembl.org/Rattus_norvegicus</a>).</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Untargeted metabolomics analysis of colon tissue</h3>
<p id="Par14">Colon metabolites were profiled by LC–MS/MS (Vanquish UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X). Separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY HSS T3 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm) at 40 °C with 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B) at 0.4 mL min⁻<sup>1</sup> (4 µL injection). MS parameters: ESI + 3.5 kV; capillary, 320 °C; probe, 300 °C; stepped CE, 30, 40, 50 eV; intensity threshold, 1 × 10<sup>5</sup>.</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical analysis</h3>
<p id="Par15">Group comparisons were performed using SPSS 20.0 (Wilcoxon rank sum; <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05). Differential genes were identified with DESeq2 (v1.38.3) (|log2FC|≥ 1, FDR &lt; 0.05, Benjamini–Hochberg) and visualized by PCA and clustering [<a href="#CR14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR14">14</a>, <a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. Enrichment was performed using clusterProfiler 4.6.0. Metabolites were analysed by PCA and OPLS-DA (MetaboAnalystR: VIP &gt; 1, <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05) [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>]; annotation was performed using KEGG, HMDB, and MSEA. The correlations were |r|&gt; 0.8 and <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05. O2PLS modelling (OmicsPLS 2.0.2) was used to integrate all differentially abundant metabolites and genes, with R<sup>2</sup> and loading plots used to identify key variables.</p></section></section><section id="Sec9"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>

<p id="Par17">UPLC–MS/MS analysis of the <em>DV</em> ethanol extract revealed 2068 compounds. By integrating three databases (TCMSP, SwissDrugDesign, and SymMap), we screened 247 bioactive substances, primarily including podophyllotoxin and its derivatives (podophyllotoxin, 4′-demethylpodophyllotoxin, picropodophyllin, podophyllotoxone, and 4′-demethylepipodophyllotoxin) and flavones (6-hydroxyluteolin, kaempferol, and morin), and identified 1901 associated targets. GeneCards identified 1872 “colon epithelial toxicity” targets. Venn analysis yielded 658 overlapping targets (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>a). These genes were input into STRING to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>b). The top 30 target edges were counted based on the PPI network (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>c). The top 10 targets—GAPDH, AKT1, TP53, ACTB, TNF, IL6, ALB, EGFR, INS, and STAT3—represent core action targets, with their interactions visualized in Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>d. GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment primarily in molecular functions such as protein serine/threonine kinase activity, DNA-binding transcription factor binding, and cytokine receptor binding (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>e). KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in the PI3K–Akt signalling pathway, lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis, and MAPK signalling pathway (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>f).</p>

<p id="Par19">The normal group of rats had good physiological conditions, whereas the experimental group exhibited lethargy, bloody stools, and loose stools. The DAI of the rats in the normal group (rank mean = 3.5; rank sum = 21) was significantly lower than that of the experimental group (rank mean = 9.5; rank sum = 57) (<em>z</em> =  − 2.966; <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05). The experimental process for the rats in the two groups is shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>A. Pathology revealed that the colonic tissue of the rats in the normal group had an intact basic structure, normal mucosa, prominent mucosal folds, no sloughed cells in the intestinal lumen, no inflammatory cell infiltration in the submucosa, and a normal morphology of goblet cells (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>b). The structure of the colon tissue of the rats in the experimental group was damaged, with no obvious folds in the mucosal layer, the mucosal barrier destroyed, and the mucosal layer thickened (Fig. <a href="#Fig4" class="usa-link">4</a>c). The mucosal epithelium was composed of a single layer of columnar epithelium and goblet cells, with many necrotic and shed mucosal epithelial cells visible in the intestinal lumen. The lamina propria contained many intestinal glands, which were densely arranged, with few goblet cells and multiple haemorrhagic spots visible. Mild dilation of intestinal glands, occasional shedding of epithelial cells in the lumen, and focal aggregation of lymphocytes were observed. The muscularis mucosa separates the lamina propria from the submucosa. The submucosa is composed of loose connective tissue. The muscular layer structure is incomplete and uneven in thickness.</p>

<p id="Par20">Transcriptomic profiling of rat colon tissues was performed on the Illumina platform, generating 62.67 Gb of clean reads (8 Gb/sample; Q30 ≥ 92%). More than 70% of the reads were uniquely mapped to the reference genome. Total gene expression (FPKM) in rat colon tissues is displayed as boxplots in Figure S1a and as violin plots illustrating distribution and probability density in Figure S1b. The principal component analysis (PCA) of the gene expression levels for each group is shown in Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>a. DESeq2 analysis revealed 2187 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 968 upregulated genes and 1219 downregulated genes. Among the 658 bioinformatically predicted common gene targets, 139 exhibited significant differential expression. The MA plot illustrates global gene expression fold changes (Figure S1c), and the volcano plot visualizes DEG distributions between groups (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>b). The 15 most strongly altered DEGs were visualized with a radar chart (Figure S1d), and Z score-normalized heatmaps clustered samples within each comparison group (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>d).</p>

<p id="Par24">Sample selection for integration was based on transcriptomic (Fig. <a href="#Fig5" class="usa-link">5</a>a) and metabolomic PCA (Fig. <a href="#Fig8" class="usa-link">8</a>a). Joint KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of both omics datasets generated a bar chart (Fig. <a href="#Fig8" class="usa-link">8</a>b) showing the top 50 pathways by <em>P</em> value. Key shared pathways included pancreatic secretion (metabolite: acetylcholine), protein digestion and absorption (metabolites: asparagine, tryptophan, L-serine, and L-histidine), and bile secretion (metabolites: L-carnitine, carnitine, acetylcholine, prostaglandin F2α, cGMP, and salicylic acid). A bubble chart (Fig. <a href="#Fig8" class="usa-link">8</a>c) displays enrichment factors (Diff/Background), <em>P</em> values, and pathway counts for the top 25 jointly enriched pathways, primarily cysteine and methionine metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and pyrimidine metabolism. O2PLS modelling revealed transcriptome-associated metabolites (Fig. <a href="#Fig8" class="usa-link">8</a>d), notably sarsasapogenin (MW0063446), clavulanate (MW0106172), and AICA ribonucleotide (MW0103476). Integration of the KGML files produced a pathway–gene–metabolite interaction network (Figure S4), where red/green denote up-/downregulation, respectively. The highest degree nodes included Mapk11 (down), Fos (up), and Enpp3 (up).</p>

<p id="Par25"><em>Dysosma versipellis (DV)</em> is an herb. Modern pharmacological research has shown that <em>DV</em> possesses a wide range of biological activities, including antitumour, antiviral, and antibacterial effects [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>, <a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>]. It has been found to play significant roles in the treatment of various diseases, such as epidemic haemorrhagic fever, oesophageal cancer, and cervical cancer [<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>]. However, in practical applications, <em>DV</em> can induce toxic reactions in multiple systems, including nervous system depression, gastrointestinal irritation, toxic shock, cardiovascular system irritation injury, multiorgan failure, and blood system disorders [<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>]. Despite these observations, the underlying toxicity mechanisms remain unclear. This study employs a multidimensional approach that integrates network toxicology, transcriptomics, metabolomics, molecular docking technology, and experimental validation to systematically elucidate, for the first time, the toxicological mechanisms of <em>DV</em>.</p>
<p id="Par26">In this study, UPLC–MS/MS technology was used to determine the components of the 70% ethanol extract of <em>DV</em>, and basic information on 2068 substances was obtained. Through network bioinformatics analysis techniques, 247 active substances were screened from the network database, and 1,901 related targets were identified. An intersection analysis with the 1,872 targets related to colon epithelial toxicity yielded 658 common gene targets. Using the STRING database, the core target genes, namely, GAPDH, AKT1, TP53, ACTB, TNF, IL6, ALB, EGFR, INS, and STAT3, which are related to glucose metabolism [<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>], DNA repair, cell cycle arrest, apoptosis regulation [<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>], cell proliferation and differentiation [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>], and immune and inflammation regulation [<a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>], were identified. The results of the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed three main signalling pathways. The downstream Bcl-2 pathway of the PI3K–Akt signalling pathway is related to apoptosis [<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a>], whereas the downstream mTOR pathway is related to autophagy [<a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>]. Lipids and atherosclerosis are related to inflammation, lipids, and vascular damage caused by atherosclerosis [<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>]. The MAPK signalling pathway involves the cascade of key proteins, such as ERK, p38, and JNK, to release downstream proinflammatory mediators, which participate in inflammatory damage [<a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>]. The results of the GO enrichment analysis revealed three biological processes, with protein serine/threonine kinase activity related to DNA damage repair [<a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a>], DNA-binding transcription factor binding closely related to gene expression and regulation [<a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>], and cytokine receptor binding regulating the expression levels of cytokines and participating in the modulation of immunity and inflammation [<a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a>]. Using molecular docking technology, 4'-demethylpodophyllotoxin was screened and found to have the best affinity for GAPDH (− 8.23 kcal/mol) and AKT1 (− 8.68 kcal/mol), which may constitute a key regulatory mechanism for the PI3K–Akt signalling pathway and metabolic pathways.</p>
<p id="Par27">In this study, rat experiments were used to validate the aforementioned network bioinformatics analysis results. The DAI scores and pathological examination results of the colonic tissue of the rats indicated that the colonic epithelial tissue was significantly damaged and destroyed after the administration of the 70% ethanol extract of <em>DV</em>. The results of the network bioinformatics analysis revealed that multiple bleeding points were due to vascular damage during the inflammatory response, whereas the death and shedding of epithelial cells were caused mainly by the regulation of the PI3K–Akt and MAPK signalling pathways, which interfered with gene expression and led to the inability to synthesize essential proteins normally. Through comparative analysis of the results of transcriptomic sequencing of colon tissues from the two groups of rats, 2187 differentially expressed genes were identified. KEGG enrichment analysis of these genes revealed that the most annotated pathways were the PI3K–Akt and MAPK signalling pathways. These results are consistent with the predictions from network bioinformatics, validating the accuracy of the results at the gene expression level. Notably, a series of genes in the PI3K–Akt signalling pathway were significantly upregulated/downregulated. Based on the differentially expressed genes, the results of the GO enrichment analysis revealed enrichment of genes related mainly to hypoxia, the extracellular matrix, and transmembrane transporter activity, and the outcome of these biological effects was the induction of damage to the colonic epithelial cells and the mucosal layer.</p>
<p id="Par28">Combined analysis of transcriptomics and metabolomics data is beneficial for further elucidating the profile of metabolites and the annotation of metabolic pathways after <em>DV</em> regulates gene expression in colonic epithelial cells. Using the detection of 3,131 metabolites, 414 differentially abundant metabolites were screened. The differentially abundant metabolites identified by the OPLS-DA model include mainly endomorphin 2, which has an immunomodulatory effect [<a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>]. 2,7-Dihydroxy-5-methyl-1-naphthoic acid can be metabolized into some cytotoxic antitumour substances [<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a>]. 1,4-Naphthoquinone is a substance with antitumour activity related to apoptosis [<a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a>], and the upregulation of these substances may be associated with damage to colonic epithelial cells. The metabolic pathways obtained through KEGG and HMDB enrichment analysis included mainly amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism and nucleotide sugar biosynthesis. Among these sugar metabolic pathways, amino sugar metabolism is associated with the structure of cell membranes through N-glycolyl groups [<a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a>]. Nucleotide sugar metabolism can regulate protein glycosylation [<a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">39</a>]. Nucleotide sugars, as activated sugars, regulate cell growth and proliferation [<a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">40</a>]. Moreover, the biosynthesis of nucleotide sugars, as activated monosaccharides, is a key intermediate in carbohydrate metabolism in all organisms and participates in nucleic acid biosynthesis [<a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">41</a>]. Combined analysis of the transcriptome and metabolome data revealed that the enriched KEGG pathways included pancreatic secretion, protein digestion and absorption, and bile secretion. The metabolic pathways in which the two groups were predominantly enriched were cysteine and methionine metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and pyrimidine metabolism. The metabolites most affected by gene transcription are mainly sarsasapogenin (down), clavulanate (down), and AICA ribonucleotide (down). In terms of pathway–genes (gene products)–related effects of metabolites, the core of the network consists of the genes Mapk11, Fos, and Enpp3. The downregulation of Mapk11 prevents the phosphorylation of many proteins, including transcription factors, impacting gene transcription and protein expression [<a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">42</a>], and plant extracts can induce cytotoxicity by upregulating the expression of Fos [<a href="#CR43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR43">43</a>]. Enpp3 hydrolyses uridine diphosphate N-acetylglucosamine, which is involved in carbohydrate metabolism and is related to the metabolomics of nucleotide sugar metabolism [<a href="#CR44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR44">44</a>]. The combined analysis results indicate that the 70% ethanol extract of <em>DV</em> reprograms metabolic pathways by regulating gene expression levels in colonic epithelial cells. Our study has some limitations. The conclusions are primarily based on bioinformatics database analysis and partial animal experiments, without validation or in-depth investigations through wet-lab experiments at the cellular or animal model level. These predictive findings provide direction and a theoretical basis for subsequent functional experimental studies; however, their ultimate biological function and clinical significance require further experimental confirmation. In the future, we will verify these findings at the cellular level and in animal models.</p></section><section id="Sec17"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="Par29">The results of this study reveal that the 70% ethanol extract of <em>DV</em> can identify 247 active substances that exhibit significant toxic effects on colonic epithelial cells. After oral administration and digestion, it reaches the colon tissue, where it is absorbed by epithelial cells and regulates the expression of a series of genes. Notably, the key component 4’-demethylpodophyllotoxin binds to GAPDH and AKT1, upregulating multiple genes in the PI3K–Akt signalling pathway and inhibiting the entire pathway. It interferes with key metabolic pathways such as amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, inhibits gene transcription and protein synthesis, disrupts the basic structure of cells, and ultimately induces the death and shedding of colonic epithelial cells. These results indicate that <em>DV</em> not only inhibits tumour cell growth and induces apoptosis [<a href="#CR45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR45">45</a>] but also induces the death of normal cells. In the future, it will be necessary to optimize drug design, enhance the targeting of tumour tissues, reduce toxicity to normal organ tissues, and potentially design drugs targeting the main active sites of <em>DV</em> to address its significant intestinal tissue toxicity. These findings provide new insights for the safe use of <em>DV</em>.</p></section><section id="Sec18"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Research involving animals</h2>
