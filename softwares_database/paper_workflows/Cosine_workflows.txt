========== paper_workflow entry 2 ==========
related_tool: Cosine
sample_description: The sample used in the research consists of electron ionization (EI) mass spectra of small molecules, where each spectrum contains peaks characterized by m/z values and intensities. The model operates on spectral data interpreted as assemblies of rAEs (reconstructed Atom Environments)—disconnected substructures representing atom-type compositions of fragment ions. Molecular structures are derived from these rAEs without requiring the presence of an observed molecular ion peak; instead, the molecular rAEs are either inferred from contextual spectral evidence or directly derived from the known molecular structure when ground-truth information is available. The training data includes EI-MS spectra paired with corresponding molecular structures, enabling learning of peak-to-fragment and fragment-to-molecule relationships.
complete_workflow_description: The method employs a custom transformer-based deep learning model implemented using PyTorch. Input tokens consist of m/z values, intensity indicators ('i'), and logarithmic intensity ranks (logRank), which range from 1 to 7 and are adapted from Cao and Guler (2021) to reduce parameter count and prevent overfitting. The transformer decoder architecture processes these inputs using self-attention mechanisms with positional encodings—applied despite permutation invariance of rAE groupings—to improve training stability and performance. The target output is tokenized as sequences of rAE blocks (each block representing atom-type groupings from individual fragment ions), followed by a special <mol> token demarcating the transition to molecular-level rAEs; all rAE groupings are sorted alphabetically before input to ensure consistency. The model learns contextual relationships across the entire spectrum rather than enforcing one-to-one peak-to-fragment mappings, enabling inference of molecular atom-type content through aggregated, decomposed, and organized rAE predictions. Post-processing converts predicted rAE sequences into the final atom-type composition of the molecule. Model training setup and hyperparameters are detailed in the Supplementary Methods, and the source code and trained models are publicly available at https://github.com/snu-lcbc/MassSpec-RefAIner and Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.17075492).

sample_description: The dataset used in this study consists of 3728 Japanese-language radiology reports from plain head CT examinations conducted at Osaka University Hospital. These reports were categorized into five Report Importance Categories (RIC) by neuroradiologists based on clinical significance: Category 0 (no findings, 15.0%), Category 1 (minor findings, 26.7%), Category 2 (routine follow-up, 44.2%), Category 3 (careful follow-up, 7.7%), and Category 4 (examination or therapy, 6.4%). The dataset was split into training (2385 reports, 64%), validation (597 reports, 16%), and test (746 reports, 20%) sets using random sampling, preserving the original class imbalance across splits.
complete_workflow_description: The active learning (AL) process followed a standardized iterative workflow: (1) Initially, all 2385 training reports were placed in the unlabeled data pool (UDP), and the labeled dataset (LD) was empty; (2) A seed set of N=25 reports was randomly sampled from UDP and annotated with RIC labels to initialize LD; (3) The UTH-BERT-based NLP model—pre-trained on Japanese medical reports from Tokyo University Hospital—was fine-tuned on LD for 10 epochs using a learning rate of 2e-5 and batch size of 4; the model weights achieving minimum validation loss were saved; (4) The trained model inferred predictions on all remaining UDP reports; (5) An informative score φ was computed for each UDP report using one of seven AL algorithms (Random Sampling, Least Confidence, Marginal Sampling, Ratio of Confidence, Entropy Sampling, Euclidean Distance, or Cosine Distance); (6) The top-N=25 reports with highest φ were moved from UDP to LD with expert annotation; (7) Steps (3)–(6) were repeated until UDP was exhausted (96 iterations total, with final iteration adding remaining <25 reports). Each AL algorithm was evaluated across 10 independent trials with different random initializations of the seed set. Statistical evaluation used Mann–Whitney U tests (p<0.05) at each step, aggregated via Superiority Score (SS) over quartiles of 24 steps each. Additionally, postponed case (PC) experiments were conducted for US methods only, where PC=0–150 reports were excluded from selection priority during early AL phases to assess robustness.

complete_workflow_description: The SIMILE algorithm is a software tool implemented in Python (available at https://github.com/biorack/simile, release 'v1_manuscript-submission') that computes spectral similarity by: (1) constructing an m/z difference matrix D from concatenated ordered m/z lists of two fragmentation spectra X (length m) and Y (length n), partitioned into four quadrants (X−X, Y−Y, X−Y, Y−X); (2) computing an m/z difference frequency matrix C by counting occurrences of m/z differences within a user-defined tolerance window across all quadrants of D; (3) deriving an m/z transition matrix T by row-normalizing C, representing a Markov chain over m/z values; (4) computing the stationary probability distribution p as the normalized principal eigenvector of T; (5) calculating the directed graph Laplacian L = p^(1/2)(I − T)p^(−1/2); (6) obtaining the m/z similarity matrix S as the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of (L + L^T)/2; (7) extracting interspectral submatrices S_XY and S_YX; (8) performing dynamic programming alignment (Needleman–Wunsch style) with zero gap penalty using recurrence A(i,j) = max{A(i−1,j−1)+S_ij, A(i−1,j), A(i,j−1)} and initial conditions A(i,0)=A(0,j)=0, yielding the alignment score as the terminal value A(m,n); (9) assessing statistical significance via a Monte Carlo permutation test: generating random permutations of the concatenated m/z list that preserve m/z ordering within each spectrum (i.e., first m entries and last n entries remain sorted), symmetrically permuting rows/columns of S accordingly, recomputing alignment scores, and estimating the p-value as the fraction of permuted scores exceeding the observed score (with early stopping when p-value improvement falls below twofold across iterations, defaulting to 10/100/1000 iterations); (10) applying final filtering thresholds: SIMILE score ≥ 0.7, p-value ≤ 0.05, and matching ion count ≥ 10 (except for the Napsamycin molecular network). Key computational optimizations include vectorized alignment scoring (replacing O(nm) DP with single-loop greedy computation using alternating vectorized maximum and rolling maximum operations), broadcast-based construction of k-permuted similarity matrix stacks to minimize data copying, and vectorized batch alignment across all k stacks. Runtime benchmarks show average compute time of ~20 ms per comparison on NIST20 without parallelization at p=0.0001; worst-case times reach ~10 seconds for extremely low p-values, mitigated by early stopping (e.g., limiting to p=0.01 reduces time ~100-fold).
