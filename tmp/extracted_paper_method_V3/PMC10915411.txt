<section id="sec1_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h3>
<p><em>Helichrysum leucocephalum</em> Boiss. (Asteraceae) is an endemic plant to Iran. No reports have studied the cytotoxicity of the plant. The current study aimed to evaluate the cytotoxicity of <em>H. leucocephalum</em> collected from Fars province (Iran) against MCF-7 and HDF cell lines using HPLC-based activity profiling and to annotate the active constituents by LC-ESIQTOF-MS/MS.</p></section><section id="sec2_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p><em>H. leucocephalum</em> was collected from three locations in Fars province. The dried flowers and leaves were separately extracted by percolation using methanol. The crude extracts were fractionated by liquid-liquid partitioning with dichloromethane (DCM) and aqueous methanol. The cytotoxicity of the fractions was evaluated against MCF-7 and HDF cells by Alamarblue assay. HPLC-based activity profiling was used to track the active constituents. LC-MS dereplication strategy was used for the annotation of the compounds in the active time window. LC-MS data were preprocessed by MZmine 3.3.0 and submitted to multivariate analysis to compare the differences and similarities in the metabolites of the samples.</p></section><section id="sec3_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>The DCM fractions showed a dose-dependent cytotoxicity against the cancerous cells (IC50s, 9.8–105.1 μg/ml). In general, the metabolites of the flowers and their cytotoxicity were higher than the leaves. LCESIMS/MS analyses revealed that prenylated and geranylated α,β-unsaturated spiroketal phloroglucinols were among the active constituents.</p></section><section id="sec4_pmc"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p>It can be concluded that <em>H. leucocephalum</em> is a rich source of phloroglucinol derivatives with cytotoxic activities. Further phytochemical analysis is needed to characterize the bioactive components.</p></section><section id="kwrds0010" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> <em>Helichrysum leucocephalum</em>, Asteraceae, HPLC, LC-ESIQTOF-MS/MS, Cytotoxicity</p></section></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0010"><em>Helichrysum</em> L., belonging to the Asteraceae family, consists of about 600 species worldwide, abundantly in Eurasia, Africa, and Australia [<a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1">1</a>,<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">2</a>]. <em>Helichrysum</em> species are herbaceous, perennials, or shrubs with dense, rectangular lanceolate, and simple to entire leaves and white or colored bracts [<a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">3</a>]. Some species of the genus, such as <em>H. italicum</em>, are well-known as medicinal plants. Many <em>Helichrysum</em> species have been used traditionally to treat ailments, particularly infections, and as a wound healing agent in traditional and folk medicine worldwide [<a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4">4</a>]. Different compounds, including flavonoids, chalcones, α-pyrone derivatives, phloroglucinols, terpenoids, essential oils, and phenolic acids, have been reported from species of the <em>Helichrysum</em> genus [<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">2</a>,<a href="#bib5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib5">5</a>].</p>
<p id="p0015">According to the flora of Iran, 19 <em>Helichrysum</em> species, including eight endemic ones, have been reported from Iran [<a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6">6</a>]. <em>Helichrysum leucocephalum</em> Boiss. Is one of the endemic species of the genus in Iran, distributed in Yazd and Fars provinces [<a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7">7</a>]. To the best of our knowledge, only two studies have investigated the pharmacological activities of the plant. Both studies have reported the antimicrobial activities for this species. In one study, the essential oil of the leaves of <em>H. leucocephalum</em> showed antimicrobial effects against <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em> and <em>Escherichia coli</em> with a MIC (Minimum Inhibitory Concentration) value of 16 μg/ml [<a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8">8</a>]. In another study, a hydroalcoholic extract of <em>H. leucocephalum</em> was evaluated against <em>Streptococcus mutans</em> growth with MIC and MBC (Minimum Bactericidal Concentration) values of 5.6 ± 6.25, 21.6 ± 6.25 mg/ml, respectively [<a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9">9</a>]. From a phytochemical point of view, no reports have investigated the chemical constituents of the extracts from <em>H. leucocephalum</em>. However, in a study, the major volatile components were carvacrol (20.36%), acetophenone (11.17%), and azulene (7.09%) [<a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8">8</a>]. According to the literature review, there are no scientific reports investigating the cytotoxicity and safety of this valuable medicinal plant. Thus, in the present study, we aimed to evaluate the cytotoxicity of <em>H. leucocephalum</em>, track the active components with the aid of HPLC activity profiling, and identify the active constituents by liquid chromatography–electrospray ionization-quadrupole time of flight-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-ESI-QTOF-MS/MS) technique.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="sec2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Plant materials</h3>
<p id="p0020"><em>Helichrysum leucocephalum</em> was collected at the flowering stage in June 2020 from three different locations, Neyriz, Ashraf, and Estahban in Fars province (Iran), where are its habitats. Dr. Narjes Azizi (botanist) identified the samples. A voucher specimen for each sample was deposited at the Herbarium of Shahid Beheshti University, Tehran, Iran. After harvesting, the leaves and flowers were dried separately at room temperature in the shade and stored in paper bags until extraction (<a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a> shows the assigned code and the herbarium number for each sample.</p>

<p id="p0025">For extraction and preparative separation, technical grade solvents were purchased from Dr. Mojallali Company (Iran) and used after distillation. Extracts and microfractions were monitored by silica gel 60 F254-coated aluminum TLC plates (Merck, Germany), and the spots were visualized by heating the plates sprayed with vanillin sulfuric acid reagent. HPLC-grade methanol and acetonitrile and analytical-grade trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were obtained from Merck Millipore (Germany). Deionized water was prepared using a TKA water deionizer (Germany).</p></section><section id="sec2.3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Extraction and fractionation</h3>
<p id="p0030">After the plant materials (500 g) were powdered with an electric mill, they were extracted separately by percolation with methanol (5L, 48h). The extracts were filtered and dried under reduced pressure to afford solid residues. In the next stage, the methanol (MeOH) extracts were suspended in water and partitioned with dichloromethane (DCM). The fractions were dried under reduced pressure by a rotary apparatus to afford DCM and aqueous MeOH residues for HLA, HLAF, HLN, HLNF, and HLE (<a href="#tbl2" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). The extracts and fractions were stored in sealed glass vials at −20 °C.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/tbl2/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section id="sec2.4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.4. Cultivation of cancerous and non-cancerous cells</h3>
<p id="p0035">The MCF-7 and HDF cell lines were purchased from Pasture Institute, Tehran, Iran. Cancerous MCF-7 and normal HDF cells were cultured in optimal conditions (RPMI 1640 + 10% FBS; antibiotic-free) and kept at 37 °C in a CO<sub>2</sub> incubator. When at least 70% of the cells in the culture flask reached the confluent state, the cells were tested for cytotoxicity tests.</p></section><section id="sec2.5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.5. Cell treatment and alamar blue assay</h3>
<p id="p0040">The cytotoxicity of the fractions and HPLC microfractions were carried out according to the procedures described elsewhere [<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">10</a>]. For this purpose, serial drug dilutions covering a range from 100 to 2.5 μg/ml were prepared. The IC<sub>50</sub> values were calculated by linear regression. The cancerous (MCF-7) and normal (HDF) cells were seeded in 96-well plates with a density of 5 × 10<sup>4</sup> cells/well and incubated at 5% CO<sub>2</sub> at a temperature of 37 °C for 24 h. A stock solution (50 mg/ml) of the DCM and aq. MeOH samples were prepared in DMSO and diluted (100, 50, 20, 10, 5, and 2.5 μg/ml) with the culture medium to ensure that the concentration of DMSO was less than 1% in all samples. Then, the cells were treated with different concentrations of the fractions: 100, 50, 20, 10, 5, and 2.5 μg/ml. The untreated cells served as a negative control, whereas doxorubicin-treated cells were considered as positive controls (doxorubicin with a purity of &gt;95%; 20, 10, 5, and 2.5 mg/ml) [<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">10</a>]. Assays were run singly and repeated at least two times. The selectivity index was calculated as IC<sub>50</sub> for HDF cells/IC<sub>50</sub> for MCF-7 cells.</p></section><section id="sec2.6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.6. Microfractionation for activity profiling</h3>
<p id="p0045">HPLC-based activity profiling of the HLNFD sample was performed according to a previously established protocol [<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>] with some modifications. The extract (50 mg/ml in DMSO) was submitted to HPLC-PDA in ten portions of 10 mg (200 μL). The mobile phase was H<sub>2</sub>O (A) and MeOH (B), both containing 0.05% TFA, and the following gradient profile was used: 40% B isocratic (0−2 min), 40% → 55% B (2−10 min), 55% → 75% B (10−15 min), 75% → 90% B (15−50 min), and 90% → 100% B (50−55 min), 100% B (55−75 min). The flow rate was 2 ml/min, and 5 min microfractions (between min 5 and 65) were collected into test tubes. Corresponding microfractions from the ten separations were combined. After drying, microfractions were tested for cytotoxic activity. The microfractionation was performed using a semi-preparative HPLC instrument. Isolation was performed at 22 °C on a Knauer HPLC instrument (Germany), equipped with a vacuum degasser, Smartline pump 1000, Smartline photodiode-array (PDA) detector 2800 with a UV light source, and a manual injector with a 2 ml sample loop. A Eurospher II 100-5 C<sub>18</sub> column (250 mm × 8 mm, 5 μm) (Knauer, Germany) was used for reversed-phase separations. The chromatograms were obtained at 285 nm. Data processing was done by EZChrome Elite software (Germany).</p></section><section id="sec2.7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.7. LC-ESIMS instrument analysis conditions</h3>
<p id="p0050">A liquid chromatograph UHPLC Nexera XR (Shimadzu, Japan) connected with a Compact QTOF Bruker mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) was used. The gradient elution with binary mobile phase of methanol and 0.05% TFA was used. The Purospher® STAR RP-18 encapped (250 × 4.6 mm i.d., particle size 5 μm), tempered at 25 °C, was used with the following gradient: 40% B isocratic (0−1 min), 40% → 55% B (1−10 min), 55% → 75% B (10−15 min), 75% → 90% B (15−50 min), and 90% → 100% B (50−55 min), 100% B (55−75 min). The flow rate was 0.4 cm<sup>3</sup>min<sup>−1</sup>. The volume of the injected samples was 3 mm<sup>3</sup>.</p>
<p id="p0055">ESI-MS detection was performed on a Brucker QqTOF (Quadropole-quadropole-Time Of Flight) compact instrument operated using Compass otofControl 4.0 (Brucker Daltonics, Germany) software. Compass DataAnalysis 4.4 (Build 200.55.2969) (Brucker Daltonics, Germany) software was used for data processing. The mass spectrometer worked in the scan range of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> = 50–1000. The ionization of the analytes was performed in both positive and negative ionization modes at a capillary voltage of 2.8 kV. The pressure of the nitrogen (nebulizing gas) was set to 0.50 bar. Nitrogen (3.0 dm<sup>3</sup>min <sup>−1</sup>) also served as drying gas at 220 °C. Sodium acetate served as a standard for calibration of the mass spectrometer.</p></section><section id="sec2.8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.8. MZmine data preprocessing</h3>
<p id="p0060">Raw MS data were converted into MzXML files using MassConvert software. Then, MzXML files were processed using Mzmine 3.3.0 [<a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12">12</a>]. Mass detection was carried out with a centroid mass detector. The noise level was set for both MS<sup>1</sup> and MS<sup>2</sup> levels. The ADAP chromatogram builder parameters including minimum group size of scans, minimum group intensity threshold, minimum highest intensity, and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> (mass to charge ratio) tolerance were set. The wavelet algorithm was used for the chromatogram deconvolution and its settings including S/N (Signal to Noise ratio) threshold, intensity window SN, minimum feature height, coefficient area threshold, peak duration range, and RT (Retention Time) wavelet range were optimized. Chromatograms were deisotoped using the isotopic peaks grouper algorithm with an optimized <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance, RT tolerance, maximum charge of 2 and the representative isotope used was the most intense.</p></section><section id="sec2.9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.9. Multivariate analysis</h3>
<p id="p0065">The normalized matrix extracted from MZmine was imported to SIMCA 14.0 (Umetrics, Sweden). The matrix was pretreated with Pareto variance scaling and a PCA (Principal Component Analysis) model was generated with the data matrix. Heat maps were created using the FreeClust platform [<a href="#bib13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib13">13</a>,<a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">14</a>]. Hierarchical clustering analysis was performed based on Euclidean distance.</p></section></section><section id="sec3"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results &amp; discussion</h2>
<section id="sec3.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Cytotoxic activity</h3>
<p id="p0070">In this study, the cytotoxic effects of the DCM and aq. MeOH fractions obtained from the flower and leaves of <em>H. leucocephalum</em> collected from three regions, Neyriz, Estahban, and Ashraf of Fars province (Iran) were evaluated against HDF and MCF-7 cells. No cytotoxicity was observed for the aq. MeOH fractions. All DCM fractions showed a dose-dependent cytotoxic activity (2.5, 5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 μg/ml) to some extent against the cancerous and normal cells (<a href="#tbl3" class="usa-link">Table 3</a>, <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>, <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>, <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>, <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>). The plant samples from Neyriz, including the leaves (HLND) and flowers (HLNFD), showed the highest cytotoxic effects against MCF-7 cells compared to samples from Ashraf and Estahban regions (<a href="#tbl3" class="usa-link">Table 3</a>). Among all samples, HLNFD exhibited the highest selectivity index. Thus, sample HLNFD was submitted for further phytochemical analysis to track active components. In addition, the lowest cytotoxic activity against MCF-7 cells was observed for the leave samples from Ashraf (HLAD). However, the flower sample from this region showed a high cytotoxicity compared to the other samples. Moreover, it can be concluded that the flowers may contain more cytotoxic compounds than the leaves.</p>

<p id="p0080">According to the best of our knowledge, there is no previous report investigating the cytotoxicity of <em>H. leucocephalum</em>. Therefore, the current study evaluated the cytotoxic activity of <em>H. leucocephalum</em> for the first time. However, based on a literature survey, other <em>Helichrysum</em> species showed cytotoxicity. For example, Sagbo et al. have evaluated the anti-proliferative and genotoxic activities of the MeOH extract from <em>Helichrysum petiolare</em> Hilliard &amp; B.L. Burtt against mouse melanoma cells (B16F10) and human melanoma cells (MeWo) at concentrations 12.5–200 μg/ml [<a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a>]. The extract showed cytotoxicity against both cancerous cell lines in a dose-dependent manner. Further analysis showed that the extract could increase apoptosis in both cell lines by inducing cell cycle arrest at the S phase and the early M phase in B16F10 and MeWo cells, respectively. Moreover, at the tested concentrations, the extract exhibited genotoxic effects [<a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a>]. In a study reported by Lourens et al. [<a href="#bib16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib16">16</a>], chloroform: MeOH (1:1) extract of <em>H. aureum</em> displayed cytotoxic effects against transformed human kidney epithelial (Graham) cells, breast adenocarcinoma (MCF-7), and glioblastoma (SF-268) cells at concentration 0.1 mg/ml with inhibition of 5%, 7%, and 35%, respectively [<a href="#bib17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib17">17</a>].</p>
<p id="p0085">Interestingly, studies show that the non-polar fractions like DCM and EtOAc might be more cytotoxic than polar fractions like MeOH. For instance, Matic et al. have studied the cytotoxic activities of different fractions of <em>H. zivojinii</em> Cernjavski &amp; Soska, including <em>n</em>-hexane, DCM, EtOAc, <em>n</em>-butanol (BuOH), and MeOH fractions against human cervix adenocarcinoma HeLa, human melanoma Fem-x, human myelogenous leukemia K562, and human breast adeno-carcinoma MDA-MB-361 cells. All fractions exerted dose-dependent cytotoxic activities on the cancerous cells to some extent. However, <em>n</em>-hexane and DCM fractions exhibited the highest cytotoxic effects compared to cisplatin as a positive control. EtOAc and BuOH fractions displayed less cytotoxicity than <em>n</em>-hexane and DCM fractions, and the MeOH fraction showed the lowest activity [<a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18">18</a>]. Likewise, ethanol:EtOAc (1:9, 5:5, 9:1, and 0:100) extracts from <em>H. plicatum</em> showed cytotoxic activity against PC3, HeLa and K562 human cancer cell lines <em>in vitro</em>. All tested extracts exhibited moderate activity against HeLa cells (41.9–42.1 μg/ml) whereas the extract obtained with 100% EtOAc was the most active against K562 and PC3 cell lines with IC<sub>50</sub>s 25.9 and 39.2 μg/ml, respectively [<a href="#bib19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib19">19</a>]. In addition, there are several investigations reporting the cytotoxic activities of the essential oil from different <em>Helichrysum</em> species. For instance, the volatile oil of <em>H. odoratissimum</em> (L) Sweet. Exhibited moderate cytotoxicity against brine shrimp larvae with LC<sub>50</sub> of 31.16 μg/ml [<a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20">20</a>]. Afoulous et al. reported that the essential oil of <em>H. gymnocephalum</em> was active against MCF-7 tumor cell lines with IC<sub>50</sub> = 16 ± 2 mg/L [<a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21">21</a>].</p></section><section id="sec3.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. <p id="p0105">Compound <strong>2</strong> produces the [M+H]<sup>+</sup> ion at <em>m/z</em> 293 in the mass spectrum. Data from tandem mass spectrometry (MS<sup><em>n</em></sup>) showed that ion 293 breaks into ion 153, 141, and 125 (<a href="#sch1" class="usa-link">Scheme 1</a>). According to literature survey [<a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24">[24]</a>, <a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25">[25]</a>, <a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26">[26]</a>], this fragmentation pattern can be attributed to a homopyrone derivative namely helipyrone C (<a href="#fig10" class="usa-link">Fig. 10</a>A &amp; B). Homodimer pyrones, including helipyrone A, B, and C, have been previously isolated from other species of the <em>Helichrysum</em> genus [<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">2</a>,<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>].</p>

<p id="p0110">Compound <strong>11</strong> produced [M+H]<sup>+</sup> and [M − H]<sup>−</sup> ions at <em>m/z</em> 401 and 399 in the positive and negative ion modes, respectively. Data from tandem mass spectrometry (MS<sup><em>n</em></sup>) in the negative ion mode showed that ion 399 breaks mainly into ion 287 resulted from a 112 Da loss. However, less abundant ions such as 330, 275, 262, 219, 194, and 152 were also observed (<a href="#fig11" class="usa-link">Fig. 11</a>). By comparing the observed MS<sup>1</sup> and MS<sup>2</sup> data with our in-house library made of phloroglucinol derivatives from the genus <em>Helichrysum</em>, the molecular ion at 399 <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> can be attributed to an α,β-unsaturated spiroketal phloroglucinol which was reported for the first time from <em>H. oocephalum</em> in our previous work [<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>]. <a href="#sch2" class="usa-link">Scheme 2</a>, <a href="#fig11" class="usa-link">Figs. 11</a>A and <a href="#fig11" class="usa-link">11B</a> show the plausible fragmentation pattern for <strong>11</strong> based on previous similar studies reporting the fragmentation pathways of phloroglucinol derivatives [<a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24">[24]</a>, <a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25">[25]</a>, <a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26">[26]</a>]. Therefore, compound <strong>11</strong> was identified tentatively as helispiroketal C [<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>].</p>

Geranylated α,β-unsaturated spiroketal phloroglucinols</h5>
<p id="p0120">Compound <strong>16</strong> produced [M+H]<sup>+</sup> and [M − H]<sup>−</sup> ions at <em>m/z</em> 469 and 467 in the positive and negative modes, respectively. The fragmentation pattern of this compound was different from that of <strong>9</strong>, <strong>11</strong>, and <strong>12</strong> but with some similarities. In the MS/MS<sup>‒</sup> of compound <strong>16</strong>, ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 423, 398, 355, 329, 315, 302, 287, 275, 262, 245, 219, 207, and 194 were detected. Of these, ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 329 and 287 with intensities of 100.0 and 86.6 were the most abundant. Similarly, in the MS/MS<sup>‒</sup> of compound <strong>11</strong>, ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 355, 330, 315, 302, 287, 275, 262, 245, 219, 207, and 194 were detected, of which ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 287 was the most intense peak. As it obvious, the observed ions from the ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 355 to the ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 194 were identical in both compounds. Interestingly, the compound <strong>16</strong> differed from <strong>11</strong> by 69 amu, which can show the existence of an extra isoprenyl moiety in <strong>16</strong>. In addition, in our previous study, we could isolate geranylated α,β-spiroketal phloroglucinol 15E- and 15Z-helispiroketals G and H with a molecular mass of 454 [<a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>]. In the current study, compound <strong>15</strong> displayed [M+H]<sup>+</sup> and [M − H]<sup>−</sup> ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 455 and 453 in the positive and negative modes, respectively, with an estimated mass 454, similar to the molecular mass of helispiroketal G. Compound <strong>15</strong> exhibited a very similar fragmentation pattern to compound <strong>16</strong> with a 14 amu mass difference. Thus, according to the MS and MS/MS data and similarity search with our in-house library, compound <strong>15</strong> and <strong>16</strong> were tentatively identified as geranylated homologues of helispiroketal C (<a href="#tbl4" class="usa-link">Table 4</a>, <a href="#fig12" class="usa-link">Fig. 12</a>, and <a href="#sch3" class="usa-link">Scheme 3</a>) [<a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24">[24]</a>, <a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25">[25]</a>, <a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26">[26]</a>].</p>

Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0145">In this study, <em>H. leucocephalum</em>, especially the DCM fraction of the flowers (IC<sub>50</sub> for HLNFD: 9.8 μg/ml), showed a dose-dependent cytotoxicity against MCF-7 cancerous cells. The HPLC-based activity profiling and LC-MS analysis revealed that compounds belonging to homodimer pyrones and prenylated and geranylated heterodimer phloroglucinols bearing an α,β-unsaturated spiroketal group might be the active constituents. Plausible fragmentation pathways were predicted for each category of the annotated compounds. However, supplementary phytochemical studies are necessary to complete the characterization of the tentative new compounds using other spectroscopical data, including NMR, in order to confirm the proposed structures.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Funding</h2>
