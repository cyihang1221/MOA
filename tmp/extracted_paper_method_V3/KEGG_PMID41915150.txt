>Materials and methods   Cell culture and reagents   Human glioblastoma cell lines LN229 and T98G were obtained from the Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in a high glucose Dulbecco’s Mpdified Eagle Medium (Gibco BRL, Waltham, MA, U.S.A.) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco BRL) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco BRL) in a humidified 5% CO 2  incubator at 37℃. Corilagin was purchased from Yuanye Tech. Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). We used water purified from the Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) for our experiments. All other chemicals used in the experiments were of analytical grade. All experiments were performed in accordance with institutional biosafety guidelines. Cell proliferation assay   We evaluated the proliferative effect of corilagin on glioblastoma cells by CCK8 assay (Dojindo, Japan) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 × 10 4  of LN229 and T98G cells/mL were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated in 5% CO 2  for 12 h at 37℃. Subsequently, they were treated with varying concentrations of corilagin (0, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 µg/mL) for 24 h. Thereafter, we incubated the cells with 10 µL/well of CCK8 for 2 h at 37℃. The absorbance values of each well were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All experiments were performed in triplicates. LN229 and T98G cells (1 × 10 6 ) were seeded in 6 well plates and incubated in 5% CO 2  for 12 h at 37℃. Post corilagin (0, 100, and 300 µg/mL) treatment for 24 h, the supernatant was removed, digested with trypsin, and the cell pellt was collected by centrifugation at 300 g 4 ℃ for 5 min. The pellet was then washed twice with pre cooled PBS to collect the pellet. Then, 1 × Binding Buffer was added to resuspend the cells, and 100 µL of cell suspension was taken and added to 5 µL Annexin V-FITC. After mixing well, the cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 5 min. Finally, 10 µL of 7-AAD and 400 µLof PBS were added and mixed well. The apoptosis analysis was performed after 1 h using flow cytometry. Cell migration assay   We used transwell assay to evaluate the effect of corilagin on glioblastoma cells migration (Corning, USA). LN229 and T98G cells were seeded 6 well plates and incubated in 5% CO 2  for 12 h at 37℃. Then, cells were subjected to treatment by corilagin (0, 100, and 300 µg/mL) for 24 h. The cells were digested with trypsin, centrifuged, and cell precipitates were collected and resuspended. Serum-free cell suspension (100 µL, 1 × 10 4 ) was added to each migration chamber, and serum containing culture medium (600 µL) was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h of cell migration, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 10–15 min, washed 2–3 times with PBS, and finally photographed under an optical microscope. Metabolomics sample preparation   We collected LN229 cells (1 × 10 7 ) treated with corilagin (0, and 256.8 µg/mL) for 6 h, with six replicates per group, named as Control group and Corilagin group, respectively. After rapid freezing with liquid nitrogen, we added 400 µL of L-2-chlorophenylalanine to the powder and vortexed the mixture for 30 s and subjected it to ultrasonication at 40 kHz for 30 min at 5℃. Thereafter, we kept the samples at -20℃ for 30 min to precipitate cell proteins. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 13,000 g at 4 ℃ for 15 min, and the supernatant was carefully transferred to sample vials for LC-MS analysis. Additionally, we designated 20 µL of the supernatant from each sample as the quality control sample. LC-MS analysis   We performed the chromatographic separation of the metabolites using a UHPLC-TripleTOF system (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA). We injected 10 µL per sample into the analytical column at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The column temperature was maintained at 40℃. The mobile phase consisted of water (A) and acetonitrile (B: containing 0.1% formic acid). The mobile phase gradients were as follows: from 0 to 3 min, 95%(A): 5%(B) to 80%(A): 20%(B); from 3 to 9 min, 80%(A): 20%(B) to 5%(A): 95%(B); from 9 to 13 min, 5%(A): 95%(B) to 5%(A): 95%(B); from 13 to 13.1 min, 5%(A): 95%(B) to 95%(A): 5%(B); from 13.1 to 16 min, 95%(A): 5%(B) to 95%(A): 5%(B).   The sample quality spectrum was collected in the positive and negative modes for a full-scan mass range of  m/z  50-1000. The heating temperature of the ion source was set at 500℃, the capillary voltage was set at 4000 V in the negative mode and at 5000 V in the positive mode, and the normalized collision energy was set at 20–60 V. Data acquisition was performed in the Data Dependent Acquisition mode. Data processing and multivariate data analysis   We analyzed the raw data files obtained from LC-MS using Progenesis QI 2.3 (Nonlinear Dynamics, Waters, USA). Initially, we pretreated the data by performing procedures such as peak detection, denoising, normalization, alignment, and deconvolution. Additionally, we obtained a list of mass and retention times corresponding to all the detected peaks from each data file in the data set. We used the ropls package (R packages, Version 1.6.2) for pattern recognition multivariate analysis, including principal component analysis (PCA) to display the distribution of comparable groups. Moreover, we also used it to perform partial least squares-discriminate analysis (PLS-DA) to check the validity of model’s, and orthogonal partial least squares discriminate analysis (OPLS-DA) to determine the global metabolic changes between the comparable groups. We also calculated the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores in the OPLS-DA model. We performed a one-dimensional statistical analysis; we analyzed the data by the Student’s t-test and obtained the corresponding p values. Firstly, we selected metabolites with VIP scores > 1.0 and  P -value < 0.05 as altered metabolites. The fold change of each metabolite was calculated by comparing mean value of the peak area of the control group with that of the Corilagin group. We then summarized the metabolites that differed among the two groups, and mapped their biochemical pathways through metabolic enrichment and pathway analysis using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), respectively. Each differential metabolite was correlated with the pathways in the KEGG database. We identified the altered pathways and eventually constructed in the light of their potential function. Statistical analysis   All data are presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical significance analysis was performed using SPSS 17.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Significant values were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and  P -value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. Results   Corilagin inhibits glioblastoma cell proliferation, migration and induces apoptosis   We treated LN229 and T98G glioblastoma cells with varying concentrations of corilagin (0, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 µg/mL) for 24 h and determine the effect of corilagin on cell proliferation by CCK8 assay. As shown in Fig.  1 A, corilagin significantly inhibited LN229 and T98G cell proliferation, with IC50 values of 256.8 µg/mL and 312.8 µg/mL, respectively. Therefore, the concentration at 50% proliferation rate was selected for subsequent mebabolomic and proteomic experiments. Then, we used flow cytometry technology to evaluate the apoptosis of cells by corilagin (0, 100, 300 µg/mL) for 24 h, and the results showed that when the concentration of corilagin was 300 µg/mL, the apoptosis rates of LN229 and T98G cells reached 22.95% and 31.21%, respectively (Fig.  1 B). Finally, we evaluated the effect of corilgin on cell migration and found that corilagin can inhibit LN229 and T98G cell migration in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig.  1 C). These results indicate that corilagin can significantly inhibit the proliferation and migration, and induce cell apoptosis in LN229 and T98G glioblastoma cells.   Fig. 1. Due to the high density of detected features, retention times and chemical classifications for the annotated peaks are detailed in Supplementary Table  S1 Metabolic profiles   Multivariate statistical analyses were performed to visualize the metabolic profiling. We observed a clear distinction between the PCA values of the Corilagin group (300 µg/mL) and the Control group in both the positive and negative ion modes (Fig.  3 A, Fig.  S1 A). This suggested that corilagin significantly altered the cell metabolome after 24 h of treatment. To identify the sample groups, we performed PLS-DA and OPLS-DA to establish the cell metabolome of each sample. Notably, the positive ionization mode yielded robust PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA models with PC1 variance values below 80%, indicating a well-distributed multidimensional separation between groups (Fig.  3 ). In constrast, the negative ionization mode PLS-DA model exhibited a dominant first component (PC1 = 80.3%) (Fig.  S1 ), suggesting that the positive mode provides a more balanced representation of the metabolic variance. The PLS-DA (Fig.  3 B) and OPLS-DA (Fig.  3 C) score plot revealed that the metabolic profiles of the Corilagin group deviated from those of the Control groups, indicating that corilagin induced significant biochemical changes in the LN229 cells. The R 2 X, and R 2 Y values ranged from 0.6 to 1.0, and Q 2  values > 0.5, indicating that the mathematical model we established in this study have good reliability and high quality of data to identify differences among the two groups. These values are listed in Table  1  and Table  S2 , demonstrating good prediction.   Fig. 3.
