sample_description: The study utilized data from phases I and II of the OPTiMiSE trial, a multicenter European (14 countries) and Israeli clinical trial involving individuals with first-episode psychosis (FEP). In phase I, 446 participants aged 18–40 meeting DSM-IV criteria for schizophrenia, schizophreniform disorder, or schizoaffective disorder were enrolled after screening (n=481 assessed); exclusion criteria included >2 years since psychosis onset, prior antipsychotic use exceeding 2 weeks in the past year or 6 weeks lifetime, known intolerance or contraindications to study medications, compulsory treatment/legal guardianship, or pregnancy/breastfeeding. Participants received open-label amisulpride (200–800 mg/day) for 4 weeks; 250 achieved symptomatic remission (remitters) and 196 did not (non-remitters) by week 4. For phase II, 92 participants (85 included in analyses) were randomized in a double-blind design to continue amisulpride or switch to olanzapine (5–20 mg/day) for 6 weeks. PANSS assessments were conducted at baseline, week 1, 2, and 4 (phase I) and at weeks 6, 8, and 10 (phase II). The analysis employed a 21-item version of the PANSS (PANSS-21), derived by excluding 9 items with low stability (<0.75) in bootstrap-based network community consistency (per Dal Santo et al.), retaining items distributed across four domains: Positive, Cognitive/Disorganized, Excited/Aggressive, and Negative. All participants provided written informed consent; the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethics approval in all participating countries.
complete_workflow_description: All analyses were conducted in R statistical software version 4.3.2. Cross-sectional network estimation used bootnet (v1.6) with the EBICglasso estimator (a LASSO-regularized Gaussian graphical model) on Spearman correlations due to ordinal PANSS item scaling; regularization hyperparameter γ was set to the default 0.5. Visualization and centrality (strength and one-step Expected Influence) were performed using qgraph (v1.9.8); centrality stability was assessed via case-dropping bootstrap (1000 samples) in bootnet to compute the CS-coefficient (threshold ≥0.25 for stability). Group comparisons used the NetworkComparisonTest (v2.2.2) with 1000 permutations to test differences in global strength, network structure, and centrality, followed by Holm-Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc edge tests. For sensitivity matching on baseline severity, MatchIt (v4.7.0) with the 'nearest neighbor' method was applied. Temporal networks (CLPN) were estimated using glmnet (v4.1.8) for regularized autoregressive and cross-lagged regressions with LASSO penalty selected via 10-fold cross-validation; a two-step debiasing procedure (Rhemtulla et al.) was then applied: zero paths from step one were fixed to zero, and remaining non-zero paths were re-estimated using lavaan (v0.6.17) within a structural equation modeling framework. Model comparison between unconstrained and time-constrained (T0→T1 = T1→T2) CLPNs used chi-square difference testing and RMSEAD. Stability of temporal networks was evaluated via non-parametric bootstrap (1000 iterations) using bootstrapLavaan (integrated in lavaan). For Network Intervention Analysis (NIA) in Phase II, the mgm package (v1.2.14) was used to estimate Mixed Graphical Models (MGMs) including all 21 PANSS items (continuous) and treatment assignment (binary), with LASSO regularization and CV-selected tuning parameter; predictability (R²) per symptom and bootstrapped CIs (1000 samples) for treatment-symptom edges were computed. Visualization across all network types used qgraph; bootnet was used for stability analyses of MGMs.

complete_workflow_description: The chemical similarity analysis was performed using Paleontological Statistics (PAST) software version 4.15. Input data consisted of a binary presence/absence matrix (rows = three P. noxium isolates; columns = 199 identified compounds), where '1' indicated detection of a compound in a given isolate and '0' indicated absence. Within PAST v4.15, the Jaccard Similarity Index was selected under the 'Similarity' or 'Cluster Analysis' module (exact menu path not specified, but standard for binary data). Default settings were applied: no data standardization, no transformation, no clustering algorithm beyond similarity computation, and no bootstrapping or significance testing. This procedure followed standard chemotaxonomic practice for comparing qualitative metabolite profiles, as cited in Survarachakan et al. (2022), and was explicitly used to corroborate findings from morphological and phylogenetic analyses.

complete_workflow_description: The segmentation pipeline consists of three core methodological components implemented in PyTorch (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA): (1) PVT (Pyramid Vision Transformer) encoder: a hierarchical transformer-based backbone incorporating multi-head self-attention (MHA) with query (Q), key (K), and value (V) matrices computed via Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ/√dₖ)V (Eq. [1]) and MultiHead(Q,K,V) = Concat(head₁,…,headₕ)Wᴼ (Eq. [2]), operating on progressively downsampled feature maps (h×w = 8×8 for group Transformer units) to capture both global context and multi-scale features; (2) Edge enhance module: a dual-train framework wherein a traditional vessel segmentation model is first trained independently, then its outputs serve as inputs to train a separate edge enhancement model that emphasizes OCT vessel boundaries; the two models are jointly leveraged to generate the final segmentation; (3) FFM (Feature Fusion Module): fuses global features (F_Global) and edge features (F_Edge) via concatenation and weighted recalibration inspired by Squeeze-and-Excitation, computed as FFusion = Conv(F_Global) ⊕ (Conv(F_Global) ⊙ F_Edge) (Eq. [3]). Training employed Adam optimizer with initial learning rate 2×10⁻⁴ and momentum 0.5, batch size 4, 200 epochs, and data augmentation including random center crop, random rotation, and axial flipping. Model complexity was evaluated via parameter count and forward inference time on four RTX 3060 Ti GPUs. Performance was assessed using ACC, SE, SP, DICE, JS (IoU), and AUC metrics under 3-fold cross-validation.

complete_workflow_description: The segmentation process involved comparative evaluation of six deep learning architectures—two CNNs (3D U-Net and V-Net), two Vision Transformers (VT-UNet and D-Former), and two hybrid networks (Swin UNETR and CoTr)—implemented in PyTorch and trained on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 (48GB GPU). All models performed multi-class segmentation of background, FS, ES, SS, and MS using a combined loss function comprising equal-weighted Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) loss and cross-entropy loss. Training employed default hyperparameters from official GitHub repositories (e.g., https://github.com/faustomilletari/VNet, https://github.com/himashi92/vt-unet, https://github.com/kkk55596/D-Former, https://github.com/Project-MONAI/research-contributions/tree/main/SwinUNETR, https://github.com/YtongXie/CoTr) and original papers. All models were trained for 200 epochs with batch size 1. Data augmentation used TorchIO and included RandomBiasField, RandomGamma (log-gamma range: –0.3 to 0.3), and RescaleIntensity (to [0,1]); spatial augmentations (e.g., rotation, flipping) were intentionally omitted to preserve anatomical orientation and inter-slice consistency. related_tool: Jaccard
sample_description: The study used two types of sEMG signal datasets: (1) a simulated dataset comprising 10,800 realizations generated by superimposing a physiological muscle activity component (s) modeled as a zero-mean Gaussian process with variance σ_s² = (10^(SNR/20)) × 1 μV and truncated via a Gaussian window (with standard deviations σ = 50, 100, or 150 ms; time supports 2ασ where α = 1, 1.5, 2, or 2.4), and background noise (n) modeled as a zero-mean Gaussian process with fixed variance σ_n² = (1 μV)²; nine SNR levels (3–30 dB in 3-dB increments) were tested; each (σ, α, SNR) combination yielded 100 realizations; all signals were sampled at 1 kHz with 1-s duration (gait cycle length) and band-pass filtered using a 4th-order Butterworth filter (10–450 Hz); binary ground-truth masks (y_Sim) were derived from the non-zero support of the truncated Gaussian function; (2) a real dataset comprising 400 sEMG signals acquired from 20 subjects (8 healthy adults, 6 post-THA patients, 6 NPH patients) during gait, with 5 gait cycles per subject and 4 lower-limb muscles (rectus femoris, lateral hamstring, lateral gastrocnemius, tibialis anterior) recorded using active differential probes (STEP32 system, 10–450 Hz Butterworth filtering); ground-truth masks (y_Real) were manually annotated by three expert operators via a custom MATLAB® GUI, with consensus defined as ≥2 out of 3 raters agreeing on muscle activation at each time-instant.
complete_workflow_description: The study evaluated three muscle activity detectors—TKEO (standard), Stat (statistical double-threshold), and LSTM-MAD (deep learning)—on both simulated and real sEMG data. For TKEO: the Teager–Kaiser Energy Operator ψ(x(n)) = x(n)² − x(n+1)x(n−1) was computed; a single threshold Th = μ_n + 7×σ_n was applied, where μ_n and σ_n were estimated from noise-only segments (simulated or expert-identified); output y^_TKEO was binary (1 if ψ ≥ Th, else 0). For Stat: an auxiliary sequence z_i = x_i² + x_{i+1}² was computed; a sliding detection window of m = 5 consecutive samples was used; muscle activation was declared if at least r₀ = 1 sample in the window exceeded amplitude threshold ζ; output y^_Stat was binary accordingly. For LSTM-MAD: a custom LSTM architecture was implemented in MATLAB® R2020b Deep Learning Toolbox; models had a sequence input layer (1 unit), variable numbers of LSTM hidden layers (n = 1 or 2), variable hidden units per layer (100–300 in 25-unit increments), learning rates (0.01–0.03 in 0.005 steps), and dropout rates (10%–45% in 5% steps); 720 configurations were trained using ADAM optimization; datasets were split into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets; early stopping was applied based on validation accuracy; outputs were classified into two classes (muscle activity vs. noise) via softmax and converted to binary mask y^_LSTM-MAD; all detector outputs underwent post-processing to remove activations shorter than 30 ms. Performance was assessed using classification accuracy on test sets, with ground truth y_Sim (simulated) or y_Real (real).

complete_workflow_description: The segmentation process consisted of five sequential stages: (1) Preprocessing: performed using MIPAV (v7.4.0); included brain extraction, noise reduction, background removal, and slice registration; FLAIR images were intensity-normalized by dividing each pixel’s intensity (C_ij) by the global average intensity (A_T), computed as A_T = Σ_i=1^N_i Σ_j=1^N_j C_ij / (N_i × N_j), yielding normalized intensities C'_ij = C_ij / A_T. (2) Feature extraction: 11 features were extracted from normalized FLAIR images using sliding windows: histogram-based features (mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis) computed over 3×3 neighborhoods; gradient-based features (mean and variance of absolute gradient) computed over 5×5 neighborhoods; and three composite features—InVa (intensity × histogram variance), IPM (intensity × local histogram mean over 19×19 window), and VIPM (gradient variance + IPM). (3) Feature selection: Sequential Forward Selection (SFS) wrapper method with KNN classifier was employed to select the optimal subset of features; ultimately, four features were retained: FLAIR pixel intensity, histogram-based local mean, variance of absolute gradient, and InVa. (4) Classification and segmentation: An Ensemble SVM approach was implemented using Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel; training data was bootstrapped into 5 disjoint subsets, each used to train an individual SVM classifier; Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) was applied to enhance robustness and accuracy. Two approaches were evaluated: Approach A applied voting directly on the 5 SVM outputs; Approach B applied morphological erosion (a binary image thinning operation using a structuring element, as illustrated in Figure 9) as post-processing before voting. (5) Voting procedure: For each pixel location across the 5 binary output matrices, a majority vote was performed; a pixel was classified as lesion if ≥3 of the 5 classifiers assigned it a value of 1 (binary-weighted thresholding), producing a final binary segmentation mask matching the original image dimensions (181×217). All algorithmic implementation details—including normalization equations (Eqs. 6–7), feature definitions (Eqs. 8–10), and voting logic—were executed in custom code; no specific commercial or open-source software beyond MIPAV (for preprocessing/manual segmentation) and unspecified SVM libraries (e.g., MATLAB libsvm or Python scikit-learn implied by context) was named, and no parameter values for SVM (e.g., C, gamma) or erosion kernel size were explicitly reported.
