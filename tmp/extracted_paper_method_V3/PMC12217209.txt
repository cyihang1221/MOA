<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objective</h3>
<p id="Par1">The present study aimed to investigate the potential role and molecular mechanism of procyanidin B2 (PCB2) in the treatment of multiple sclerosis (MS), with the hope of providing novel strategies for the precision treatment of MS.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p id="Par2">The target genes of PCB2 and the disease-related target genes of MS were gathered from databases like GEO, GeneCards, OMIM, and DisGeNET. R language was employed for the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), unsupervised clustering analysis, immune infiltration analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Eight machine learning algorithms were employed to screen key genes, and nomograms and ROC curves were constructed to assess the value of the screened biomarker genes in MS diagnosis. Through these analyses, four key genes closely associated with the pathogenesis of MS were selected, namely potassium ion channel subfamily H member 2 (KCNH2), prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 1 (PTGS1), estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), and vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA). These genes were significantly enriched in biological processes related to oxidative stress and served as potential targets for PCB2 in treating MS. Moreover, a key gene-transcription factor (TFs)-microRNA (miRNA) regulatory network was established to preliminarily explore the upstream regulatory mechanisms of these genes. Meanwhile, molecular docking and single-gene GSEA enrichment analysis were carried out to verify the interaction between PCB2 and these key genes. Finally, a CPZ (cuprizone)-induced mouse model of MS was set up, and the effects of PCB2 in MS treatment were verified by means of behavioral tests, pathological staining, immunofluorescence staining, ELISA, RT-PCR, and Western blot detection methods.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p id="Par3">PCB2 significantly improved behavioral performance in CPZ-induced MS mice, including enhanced motor coordination, reduced anxiety and hyperactivity, and improved spatial learning and memory. Additionally, PCB2 could promote myelin repair and decrease the levels of inflammatory response and oxidative stress in the mouse brain. Specifically, the expression levels of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 decreased significantly, while the expression level of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 increased, and the activities of antioxidant enzymes like CAT, SOD, and GSH-Px increased notably. More importantly, PCB2 could upregulate the expression levels of the KCNH2, PTGS1, ESR1, and VEGFA genes and their related proteins, which may played significant roles in biological processes related to oxidative stress.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p id="Par4">This study disclosed the potential role of PCB2 in MS treatment by regulating the expression of key genes, providing a theoretical basis and potential therapeutic targets for personalized treatment of MS. PCB2 demonstrates good application prospects in MS treatment. Future research will further validate the clinical application value of these key genes and deeply explore the specific mechanism of PCB2 in MS treatment, aiming to develop more effective treatment regimens for MS.</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Graphical Abstract</h3>

<p id="Par6">This study focuses on Multiple Sclerosis (MS), an autoimmune disease characterized by inflammatory demyelination in the central nervous system (CNS). The intricate pathological mechanisms and heterogeneous manifestations of MS pose enduring challenges in neuroscience research [<a href="#CR1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR1">1</a>, <a href="#CR2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR2">2</a>]. Although the exact cause of MS has not been fully clarified, increasing evidence suggests that it is an inflammatory immune-mediated disease mainly involving autoreactive lymphocytes, and in the later stage of the disease, microglial activation and chronic neurodegeneration become predominant [<a href="#CR3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR3">3</a>, <a href="#CR4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR4">4</a>]. Given the huge challenges that MS brings to patients and medical teams, it is particularly important to find effective preventive and treatment measures.</p>
<p id="Par7">In recent years, with the in-depth research on the pathological mechanisms of MS, more and more scholars have begun to pay attention to the potential of nutritional components in food in preventing or treating such diseases. Procyanidin B2 (PCB2), as a natural plant polyphenolic compound, has shown remarkable biological activities in aspects such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and lipid metabolism regulation [<a href="#CR5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR5">5</a>, <a href="#CR6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR6">6</a>]. Previous studies have already indicated that the extracted mixtures of procyanidins can play a role in preventing atherosclerosis by inhibiting inflammation, regulating lipid metabolism, and reducing macrophage foaming [<a href="#CR7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR7">7</a>, <a href="#CR8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR8">8</a>]. However, the specific role and mechanism of PCB2 in MS remain underexplored. Notably, preliminary studies have shown that procyanidins ameliorate neurodegenerative symptoms by regulating immune responses and reducing inflammation [<a href="#CR9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR9">9</a>, <a href="#CR10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR10">10</a>], which provides a theoretical basis for exploring the potential role of PCB2 in MS. In addition, animal model studies have shown that polyphenolic compounds may have a neuroprotective effect and can slow down the progression of the disease [<a href="#CR11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR11">11</a>]. Nevertheless, further preclinical and clinical studies are still needed to confirm the specific mechanism of PCB2 in MS.</p>
<p id="Par8">Therefore, this study utilized the resources of multiple databases such as GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus), GeneCards, OMIM (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man), and DisGeNET, and through bioinformatics and network pharmacology analyses, four key genes were deduced—Potassium Voltage-Gated Channel Subfamily H Member 2 (KCNH2), Prostaglandin Endoperoxide Synthase 1 (PTGS1), Estrogen Receptor 1 (ESR1), and Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor A (VEGFA). These genes, as key molecules involved in inflammation regulation and cellular stress responses, may play important roles in the pathogenesis of MS [<a href="#CR12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR12">12</a>, <a href="#CR13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR13">13</a>].</p>
<p id="Par9">In summary, for the first time, this study integrated the resources of public databases and combined techniques such as bioinformatics and network pharmacology to deduce that PCB2 may affect the disease process of MS by regulating the expression and functions of these key genes, namely KCNH2, PTGS1, ESR1, and VEGFA, and then verified it in animal experiments. We hope that through this study, the potential therapeutic role and molecular mechanism of PCB2 in MS can be revealed, providing new ideas and strategies for the treatment of MS. Meanwhile, this study will also further expand the application range of PCB2 in the biomedical field and provide more scientific evidence for its potential in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases.</p></section><section id="Sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="Sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data collection and preparation, and experimental animals</h3>
<section id="Sec4"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Data collection and preparation</h4>
<p id="Par10">The SMILE number corresponding to the PCB2 component name or CAS number was found in the PubChem database. Then, the SMILE number was used to predict the target genes in the SwissTargetPrediction database. The target genes with a Probability value of 0 were removed, and 49 PCB2 drug target genes were obtained. These were then corrected to the official gene names using the UniProt database.</p>
<p id="Par11">Data collection was carried out using the GeneCards database, OMIM database, and DisGeNET database respectively. 753 target genes were obtained from the GeneCards database, 165 target genes were found from the OMIM database, and 929 target genes were obtained from the DisGeNET database. The targets obtained from the three databases were combined, duplicate items were removed, and they were corrected to the standard gene names using the UniProt database. Eventually, 1812 MS disease target genes were derived.</p>
<p id="Par12">The dataset <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE131282" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE131282</a> of MS was acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The samples were brain tissues from normal controls or MS patients.</p></section><section id="Sec5"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Experimental animals</h4>
<p id="Par13">A total of 40 male C57BL/6 mice (aged 7—8 weeks, weighing 20-22 g) of SPF grade were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (License number: SCXK (Beijing) 2016—0006). The mice were fed in the SPF-grade animal room of Shanxi University of Chinese Medicine. After one week of adaptive feeding, relevant experiments were conducted. All experimental operations were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanxi University of Chinese Medicine.</p></section></section><section id="Sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and Unsupervised Clustering</h3>
<p id="Par14">After the data was prepared, the “limma” package in R language was used to analyze the gene expression data between the MS group and the HC group to screen for DEGs. The specific parameter settings were a P value &lt; 0.05 and |log2FC|&gt; 1. A total of 857 DEGs were identified in this step, including 3 up-regulated genes and 854 down-regulated genes. Subsequently, a volcano plot representation was used to visually display the differential analysis results of each group.</p>
<p id="Par15">The “ConsensusClusterPlus” package in R language was utilized to implement the Consensus Clustering algorithm to conduct an in-depth analysis of the transcriptome data of patients in the disease group. Through consensus clustering, 142 cases were divided into two optimal subtypes, named Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 respectively.</p></section><section id="Sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immune infiltration analysis</h3>
<p id="Par16">The appropriate R package "pheatmap" was used to draw a heatmap to visually display the expression differences of DEGs between different subtypes. Meanwhile, relevant R packages were used to conduct immune infiltration analysis, and CD274 gene expression levels were used to divide high and low groups. calculate the infiltration numbers of different immune cell types in the samples, and reveal the biological characteristics of the two disease subtypes.</p></section><section id="Sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)</h3>
<p id="Par17">To conduct an in-depth analysis of the gene expression data in <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE131282" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE131282</a>, the "WGCNA" package in R language was used to perform WGCNA on the gene expression data in <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE131282" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE131282</a>. Firstly, by calculating the network connectivity and scale independence under different soft thresholds, an appropriate soft threshold was determined. Then, the hierarchical clustering method was used to observe the clustering trend of the gene expression data, and a correlation heatmap was drawn to show the co-expression relationships among genes. Finally, by calculating the correlations between modules and phenotypes, the co-expression modules significantly related to the disease phenotype were determined, and a network heatmap was drawn to visually display the co-expression patterns and relationships of the selected genes in the weighted gene co-expression network.</p></section><section id="Sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Gene ontology (GO) analysis, GSEA enrichment analysis, and single-gene GSEA enrichment analysis</h3>
<p id="Par18">We used the MS disease genes, DEGs genes, genes in the blue module of WGCNA, and the predicted target genes of PCB2 to draw a Venn diagram and take the intersection, obtaining 25 intersection genes. The “c5.go.v2024.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” data and the “clusterProfiler”, “ggplot2”, and “enrichplot” packages in R language were used to conduct GO analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on the screened key genes to reveal the biological processes significantly enriched by these genes. Similarly, single-gene GSEA was conducted on KCNH2, PTGS1, ESR1, and VEGFA using R language to verify the previous results.</p></section><section id="Sec10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Screening of key genes by 8 machine learning algorithms</h3>
<p id="Par19">The machine learning packages in R language (such as “glmnet” for the LASSO algorithm, “glm” for the generalized linear model, etc.) were used to screen the predicted targets of PCB2. The inverse cumulative distribution residuals were calculated and box plots were drawn to evaluate the prediction performance of the models, and the feature importance was calculated to assess the contribution of different genes to the model prediction. We used the 33 genes screened by 8 machine learning algorithms and the 25 intersection genes to draw a Venn diagram and take the intersection again, obtaining 21 intersection genes. Then, according to the ranking of the frequency of appearance in the top ten of the feature importance ranking of the 8 machine learning algorithms, the top 8 genes were screened out.</p></section><section id="Sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Construction of nomograms and diagnostic models and evaluation of their effects</h3>
<p id="Par20">To comprehensively understand the diagnostic effects and significance of these biomarker genes, relevant packages in R language (such as “rms” or “pROC”, etc.) were used to construct nomograms for the 8 screened key genes. We constructed nomograms and used the ROC curve to evaluate their diagnostic value. The results showed that these genes had relatively high prediction and discrimination abilities. In addition, individual ROC analysis and nomogram effect evaluation for each gene were also carried out, and a Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network was constructed to reveal the interaction relationships among genes.</p></section><section id="Sec12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Construction of the hub Genes—transcription factors (TFs)—microRNA (miRNA) regulatory network</h3>
<p id="Par21">The miRNAs of the 4 key genes were predicted through the miRWalk database (<a href="https://mirwalk.umm.uniheidelberg.de/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://mirwalk.umm.uniheidelberg.de/</a>). The screening parameters were set as Score ≥ 0.95, the target site was limited to the 3UTR region, and the miRDB database was selected for verification (parameter setting was 1). Subsequently, the iRegulon plugin in the Cytoscape software was used to predict the transcription factors (TFs). The normalized enrichment score (NES) &gt; 3 was set as the screening threshold to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the results. Finally, the prediction results were integrated, and the TF-miRNA-target gene regulatory network was constructed through the Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1) to visually display the regulatory relationships and further analyze the potential regulatory mechanisms.</p></section><section id="Sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Molecular docking</h3>
<p id="Par22">In this study, PCB2 was selected as the small molecule drug component. The 3D structure of the active ingredient (ligand) of the drug was downloaded from the PubChem database. The 3D structures of the receptor proteins KCNH2, PTGS1, ESR1, and VEGFA were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database. Then, the molecular docking was carried out using AutoDock 1.5.6 software, and the optimal result docking diagrams were drawn using PyMOL 2.5.0 software.</p></section><section id="Sec14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Establishment of the CPZ animal model</h3>
<p id="Par23">Forty mice were randomly and evenly divided into four groups according to body weight, namely the Normal group, the Normal + PCB2 group, the CPZ + NS group, and the CPZ + PCB2 group. The Normal group and the Normal + PCB2 group were fed with normal feed, while the CPZ + NS group and the CPZ + PCB2 group were fed with feed containing 0.2% CPZ (Sigma, USA, CAS number: 370-81-0) for 10 weeks to construct a chronic demyelinating mouse model. At the end of the 10th week, the CPZ feeding was stopped and replaced with normal feed. Starting from the 11th week, the Normal group and the CPZ + NS group were intraperitoneally injected with normal saline (NS), and the Normal + PCB2 group and the CPZ + PCB2 group were intraperitoneally injected with PCB2 (Chengdu Rufens Biological Technology Co., Ltd., purity ≥ 98%, at a dose of 60 mg/kg/day) for a total of 14 days. During the entire experiment, routine cage maintenance was carried out no less than three times a week, and the daily status of the mice was observed and recorded.</p></section><section id="Sec15"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Behavioral tests</h3>
<p id="Par24">The mice needed to adapt to the surrounding environment of the behavioral experiments in advance to avoid affecting the experimental results.</p>
<ol class="list" style="list-style-type:decimal">
<li><p id="Par25">Open Field Test (OFT): The mice were placed in a quiet and clean open field facing the wall of the box. The behavioral software was used to record the activity of the mice in the open field for 10 min. Four mice could be detected simultaneously each time. After the test, the open field box was cleaned with 75% alcohol to exclude interference from other factors. After drying, the next group of experiments was continued.</p></li>
<li><p id="Par26">Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) Test: EPM consisted of two open arms and two closed arms of equal size that crossed each other. The mice were placed in the central area facing the open arm and with their backs to the experimenter. The behavior of the mice was recorded, and the maze was cleaned with 75% alcohol before the next group of experiments was carried out.</p></li>
<li><p id="Par27">T Maze Test: First, the mice were trained to be familiar with the left and right arms and to identify the position of the bait arm. Then, after fasting for 24 h, the mice were placed in the main arm of the T maze and allowed to move freely. The number of times the mice entered the bait arm and the distance were recorded to observe their working memory ability.</p></li>
<li><p id="Par28">Mouse Climbing Pole Test (CPT): A metal pole with a diameter of 2 cm and a height of 70 cm was selected. A dome with a diameter of 4.5 cm was made at the upper end of the metal pole using cloth strips, and the top and body of the pole were wrapped with gauze. Meanwhile, equipment for recording the behavior of the mice, such as a video camera or a stopwatch, was prepared. First, adaptive training was carried out to make the mice familiar with the process of climbing from the top of the pole to the ground. In the testing stage, the time required for the mice to climb from the top of the pole to the ground and the number of mistakes (falling, slipping, etc.) were recorded. Three records were taken, and the median value was selected to obtain reliable results.</p></li>
</ol></section><section id="Sec16"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample collection and specimen preparation</h3>
<p id="Par29">After the behavioral experiments, 5 mice were randomly selected from each group and anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg). The chest was opened to expose the heart, the right auricle was cut open, and the heart was perfused with normal saline until the liver turned white. Then, the heart was perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde. The brain tissues were dissected out, fixed with paraformaldehyde, and then placed in 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose solutions for gradient dehydration. After that, they were embedded with OTC, quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored in a -80 °C refrigerator for standby use. When in use, a cryostat was used to cut the brain tissue into 10 μm slices for pathological and immunofluorescence staining. Another 5 mice in each group were anesthetized in the same way as above and then only perfused with normal saline. The brains were taken out, the corpus callosum (CC) and the surrounding brain tissues were separated, weighed, and placed in sterile EP tubes. Part of the tissues was taken out, and lysis buffer was added according to the weight. The tissues were ground until a homogeneous state was achieved. The samples were lysed on ice for 30 min, and vortexed every 5 min. Then, they were transferred to a centrifuge at 4 °C, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm/min for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new sterile EP tube using a pipette. After the protein concentration was measured, the samples were aliquoted, which was the brain tissue protein solution. To reduce protein degradation, the aliquots were placed in a -80 °C refrigerator together with the remaining brain tissues for subsequent detection.</p></section><section id="Sec17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Luxol Fast Blue (LFB) and TrueGold staining detection</h3>
<p id="Par30">For Luxol Fast Blue staining (Solarbio, G3245, China), mouse brain sections were initially warmed and soaked in 70% ethanol for 15 min before being incubated in the staining solution at 56–60 °C overnight. The next day, the sections were thoroughly rinsed with 95% ethanol and deionized water to remove the excess stain, and then treated with a mixture of 75% ethanol, deionized water, and 0.05% lithium carbonate to clearly differentiate the gray and white matter. After a series of ethanol dehydration steps and xylene clearing, the sections were mounted with neutral resin for microscopic examination.</p>
<p id="Par31">For TrueGold staining (Ousaisi Biotechnology Co., Ltd, BK-AC001, China), brain slices were warmed and dried at 37 °C for 30 min before diluting the stain and terminator in double-distilled water. The slices were then stained in the dark at 45 °C for 30 min, rinsed with (Phosphate Buffered Saline) PBS, and treated with the stain terminator at 45 °C for 2–3 min. After three washes with PBS, the slices were dried, mounted, and imaged using a microscope.</p></section><section id="Sec18"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunofluorescence staining</h3>
<p id="Par32">The prepared frozen brain tissue slices were washed with PBS three times, 5 min each time. The primary antibody was added to the PBS solution containing 0.3% Triton X-100 and 1% (bovine serum albumin) BSA, suspended, and then dropped onto the slices. The slices were incubated in a humid box at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the slices were rinsed with PBS three times, 5 min each time, and the secondary antibody corresponding to the species was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The slices were washed with PBS three times again, and then mounted with an anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium containing DAPI. After 10 min, the results could be observed under a fluorescence microscope to detect the expression of Myelin basic protein (MBP, IF 1:200 dilution, Bio-Rad, aa82–87), Adenomatous Polyposis Coli Protein (APC, IF 1:200 dilution, Bioworld, MB62467, USA), and Neuron-Glial Antigen 2 Antibody (NG2, IF 1:500 dilution, Millipore, 05-710, USA) in the CC region. Finally, the fluorescence density was analyzed using Image J fiji 2.14.0.</p></section><section id="Sec19"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and enzyme assay detection</h3>
<p id="Par33">Take the above-mentioned brain tissue protein solutions from each group and follow the instructions of ELISA and micro enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits. Read the optical density (OD) values using an ELISA reader to detect the content or activity of Interleukin-1 Beta (IL-1β, Invitrogen, 88-7013A-88, USA), Interleukin-6 (IL-6, Invitrogen, 88-7064-22, USA), Interleukin-10 (IL-10, Invitrogen, 88-7105-22, USA), Tumor Necrosis Factor-Alpha (TNF-α, Invitrogen, 88-7324-88, USA), Catalase (CAT, Pulilai Gene Technology Co., Ltd., E2030, China), Superoxide dismutase (SOD, Nanjing Jiancheng, A003-4-1, China), and Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, Nanjing Jiancheng, A005-1-2, China) in brain homogenate.</p></section><section id="Sec20"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Reverse transcription—polymerase chain reaction (RT—PCR) detection</h3>
<p id="Par34">An appropriate amount of brain tissue was placed in a mortar pre-cooled with liquid nitrogen. Liquid nitrogen was slowly added, and the tissue was continuously ground into powder with a pestle and then placed in an EP tube. Total RNA was extracted using the M5 Universal RNA Mini Kit. After measuring the concentration, cDNA was synthesized using the TaKaRa reverse transcription kit. The 2 × M5 HiPer SYBR Premix EsTaq kit was selected for real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR amplification. Finally, the relative expression levels of genes were calculated through the <span xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="IEq1"><math id="d33e420"><msup><mrow><mn>2</mn></mrow><mrow><mo>-</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">Δ</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">Δ</mi><msub><mtext>C</mtext><mtext>t</mtext></msub></mrow></msup></math></span> formula to detect the gene expression levels of KCNH2, PTGS1, ESR1, and VEGFA in brain tissues. The primers used in this experiment were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and the primer sequences are shown in Table <a href="#Tab1" class="usa-link">1</a>.</p>

<p id="Par35">The brain homogenate proteins from each group mentioned above were used. The concentration of the extracted protein samples was determined by the BCA method, and 30 μg of samples were quantified for loading. The protein samples were added to the Loading Buffer, boiled for 10 min for denaturation, and then reserved for use. After preparing the gel according to the protein molecular weight and loading the samples, the proteins were wet-transferred onto the activated PVDF membrane through SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis. The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA blocking solution at room temperature for 2 h. After rinsing with TBST, the primary antibodies diluted according to the corresponding dilution ratios were added, and the membrane was incubated in a 4 °C refrigerator overnight. On the next day, the membrane was rinsed with TBST three times to wash off the excess primary antibodies. Then, the corresponding secondary antibodies were added according to the species of the primary antibodies, and the membrane was slowly incubated on a shaker at room temperature for 2 h. <p id="Par63">MS, a chronic CNS disorder, severely impairs neurological function and quality of life. Accumulating evidence highlights aberrant immune activation and dysregulated immune cell infiltration in the CNS as key drivers of MS pathogenesis [<a href="#CR15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR15">15</a>]. These findings emphasize the importance of in-depth exploration of the immunopathological mechanism of MS. With the rise of the concept of precision medicine, it has become increasingly crucial to conduct an in-depth analysis of the heterogeneity of the MS patient population [<a href="#CR16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR16">16</a>]. In this study, unsupervised clustering methods were employed to subdivide disease subtypes and thoroughly investigate the immune infiltration patterns of each subtype, aiming to reveal the potential pathological mechanisms and provide a theoretical basis for personalized treatment [<a href="#CR17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR17">17</a>].</p>
<p id="Par64">Under normal circumstances, the CNS is immune-privileged, with theblood-brain barrier (BBB) strictly restricting immune cell entry [<a href="#CR18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR18">18</a>]. However, in MS patients, the BBB is damaged and its permeability increases, allowing peripheral immune cells to infiltrate [<a href="#CR19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR19">19</a>]. T lymphocytes, especially CD4⁺ helper T cells, show an imbalance in differentiation. Pro-inflammatory subsets like Th1 and Th17 are over-activated, releasing inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-17 [<a href="#CR20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR20">20</a>, <a href="#CR21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR21">21</a>]. These cytokines attack myelin and trigger demyelination [<a href="#CR22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR22">22</a>].</p>
<p id="Par65">B lymphocytes also abnormally accumulate in the brain, producing pathogenic antibodies that further damage the myelin structure and neuronal integrity [<a href="#CR23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR23">23</a>]. Macrophages exhibit a complex “dual nature” in MS [<a href="#CR24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR24">24</a>]. In the acute phase, pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages flood into the lesion site, releasing mediators such as nitric oxide and TNF-α, expanding the scope of inflammation [<a href="#CR25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR25">25</a>]. Anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages should aid tissue repair and inflammation resolution by secreting cytokines like IL-10 and TGF-β. However, the complex MS microenvironment often interferes with M2 macrophages’ function, obstructing the repair process [<a href="#CR26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR26">26</a>, <a href="#CR27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR27">27</a>]. Immune cells such as natural killer cells and dendritic cells also contribute to the immune imbalance [<a href="#CR28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR28">28</a>, <a href="#CR29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR29">29</a>]. These studies enhance our understanding of MS pathology and offer valuable clues for developing new treatments.</p>
<p id="Par66">In this study, an unsupervised clustering algorithm was innovatively used. Based on gene expression data, the MS disease group was successfully divided into two subtypes, Cluster 1 and Cluster 2. This clustering method does not rely on existing disease classification knowledge, but purely relies on the similarity of gene expression within samples to explore the latent characteristics of patient subgroups [<a href="#CR30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR30">30</a>]. It is worth noting that from Fig. <a href="#Fig2" class="usa-link">2</a>A and B, it can be seen that the gene expression pattern of cluster 2 is relatively closer to that of healthy individuals, indicating that patients with this subtype may be in a relatively mild disease stage or have relatively strong compensation and repair mechanisms themselves [<a href="#CR31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR31">31</a>].</p>
<p id="Par67">When deeply exploring the immune infiltration of the two subtypes, some valuable differences were found. In Cluster 2, among the further subdivided high and low expression groups, there were significant differences in the number of M2 macrophage infiltration. Due to the fact that our immune infiltration was divided into high and low expression groups based on the expression level of CD274 gene (PD-L1). When the infiltration number of M2 macrophages in the high expression group was relatively large, it might imply that the body was fully mobilizing anti-inflammatory repair forces, attempting to counteract the overly active inflammatory response and restore the homeostasis of the neural microenvironment. On the contrary, the scarcity of M2 macrophages in the low expression group indicated that inflammation might continue to erode neural tissues, increasing the risk of disease recurrence. In contrast, in Cluster 1, there was no significant difference in the immune infiltration of M2 macrophages between the high and low expression groups, indicating that the immune microenvironment of patients in Cluster 1 was relatively stable, or the pathogenic mechanism was less associated with M2 macrophages and was more dominated by other immune cell types and cytokine storms, trapped in a vicious cycle of continuous inflammation and nerve damage, and the disease progression was more severe [<a href="#CR32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR32">32</a>].</p>
<p id="Par68">The abnormal infiltration of immune cells in the CNS of MS patients is not an isolated event, but is closely intertwined with the oxidative stress pathway, forming a vicious cycle that continuously promotes disease progression [<a href="#CR33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR33">33</a>]. When peripheral immune cells, such as pro-inflammatory T lymphocytes and macrophages, break through the BBB and infiltrate the brain parenchyma, on one hand, they release a large number of inflammatory cytokines [<a href="#CR34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR34">34</a>]. On the other hand, they activate glial cells in the CNS, making them “accomplices” in inflammation and oxidative stress [<a href="#CR35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR35">35</a>]. After being activated, glial cells have mitochondrial dysfunction, and the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) surges, far exceeding the scavenging ability of the intracellular antioxidant defense system, triggering an oxidative stress response [<a href="#CR36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR36">36</a>]. Under oxidative stress conditions, pathological changes such as lipid peroxidation, protein oxidative modification, and DNA damage occur successively, further disrupting the metabolism and signal transduction of nerve cells, resulting in aggravated damage to myelin and increased apoptosis of neurons [<a href="#CR37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR37">37</a>]. Meanwhile, the damaged nerve tissues release more chemokines, attracting a continuous influx of immune cells, worsening the inflammatory environment, strengthening oxidative stress damage, and making MS difficult to cure [<a href="#CR38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR38">38</a>].</p>
<p id="Par69">Given the excellent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of PCB2, this study focused on the intersection mining of MS disease genes, DEGs, genes in the WGCNA blue module, and the predicted target genes of PCB2. Through rigorous screening, 25 intersection genes were obtained. Analysis found that the biological processes significantly enriched by these genes at the GO BP level were closely related to oxidative stress. For example, enrichment entries such as “response to oxidative stress” and “cellular response to oxidative stress” indicated that these genes were highly likely to be involved in the key links of cells sensing and responding to oxidative stress stimuli, encoding antioxidant enzymes, stress proteins, or regulating key factors in redox signaling pathways to help cells maintain redox balance [<a href="#CR39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR39">39</a>].</p>
<p id="Par70">To accurately identify the key genes of PCB2 in the treatment of MS, this study adopted eight advanced machine learning algorithms. Using the 49 predicted targets of PCB2 as the core genes, genes closely related to them were screened from the <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE131282" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GSE131282</a> dataset [<a href="#CR40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR40">40</a>]. Moreover, the sample size of 184 is sufficient to meet the standards for exploratory bioinformatics [<a href="#CR41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR41">41</a>]. By randomly assigning 70% of the data to the training set and the remaining 30% to the test set, the objectivity and accuracy of model evaluation were ensured. Among the eight algorithms, the top ten genes in terms of importance were listed respectively, and 33 candidate genes were summarized. Further, the intersection with the previous 25 intersection genes was taken, and finally, 21 intersection genes were obtained. After ranking these genes according to feature importance, the top 8 genes were selected as the key genes. These strategies ensured that the sample size (184 cases) was sufficient relative to the final feature number (8 genes), adhering to the rule of thumb that at least 10–15 samples per feature are needed to avoid overfitting. To further evaluate the potential of these 8 intersection genes as diagnostic biomarker genes for MS, this study adopted the methods of nomogram construction and ROC curve analysis. The nomogram intuitively showed the combined prediction effect of these genes in the diagnosis of MS, providing strong support for clinical decision-making [<a href="#CR42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR42">42</a>]. The ROC curve analysis results showed that the AUC value of the model constructed by these genes reached 0.888, far higher than the discrimination standard of 0.7, fully demonstrating that they have high prediction and discrimination abilities [<a href="#CR43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR43">43</a>]. In addition, KCNH2 (AUC = 0.742), PTGS1 (AUC = 0.802), ESR1 (AUC = 0.823), and VEGFA (AUC = 0.857) all showed reliable prediction abilities. The important roles of these genes in the diagnosis of MS are not only reflected in their high AUC values, but also in their potential connections with the pathogenesis of MS. For example, KCNH2 is a potassium ion channel protein. In CNS, the normal function of KCNH2 affects the excitability and signal transduction of nerve cells. Studies have shown that KCNH2 may participate in the changes in the electrophysiological activities of nerve cells in MS, thereby affecting the disease process [<a href="#CR44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR44">44</a>]. PTGS1 is not only involved in the synthesis of prostaglandins, but also plays a key role in regulating immune and inflammatory responses [<a href="#CR45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR45">45</a>, <a href="#CR46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR46">46</a>]. Previous studies have shown that ESR1 plays a crucial role in the protective effect of estrogen-mediated experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE). Through ESR1, estrogen can regulate the function of immune cells, promote immune tolerance, reduce inflammation in the CNS, and exhibit a neuroprotective effect [<a href="#CR47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR47">47</a>]. VEGFA is a protein that can promote angiogenesis and inflammatory responses [<a href="#CR48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR48">48</a>] and may be involved in the pathological process of MS.</p>
<p id="Par71">To understand the network relationship between these 4 key genes and TFs, miRNAs, a gene-TFs-miRNAs regulatory network was further constructed. It was found that the Serum Response Factor (SRF) could simultaneously regulate the expression of these 4 key genes, indicating that SRF may play an important role in the onset and treatment of MS [<a href="#CR49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR49">49</a>]. However, the interaction in the gene-TFs-miRNAs regulatory network is complex, and the mechanism of SRF in it is not yet fully understood and awaits further exploration.</p>
<p id="Par72">However, as far as this article is concerned, it is more important to delve into the interaction mechanisms between PCB2 and 4 key genes of MS. Therefore, this study adopted two methods: molecular docking and single-gene GSEA. The molecular docking results showed that PCB2 could spontaneously dock with 4 key genes, namely KCNH2, PTGS1, ESR1, and VEGFA, verifying the potential interaction between PCB2 and these genes. The results of single-gene GSEA showed that there were significant differences in these 4 key genes in the oxidative stress pathway of the GO biological process (BP), further confirming the importance of oxidative stress in the pathogenesis of MS and suggesting that these 4 key genes may affect the pathological process of MS by participating in the oxidative stress process.</p>
<p id="Par73">To verify the above results, this study successfully established a CPZ-induced mouse animal model, simulating the pathological characteristics of MS or other related neurological diseases [<a href="#CR50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="CR50">50</a>]. Through behavioral tests, it was found that PCB2 could significantly improve the behavioral performance of CPZ mice, including improving motor coordination ability, reducing anxiety and hyperactivity behaviors, and enhancing spatial learning and memory abilities, indicating that it may have a neuroprotective and/or neuroreparative effect. Further research showed that PCB2 could not only improve behavioral performance, but also increase the expression of MBP, APC, and NG2 in the CC region of CPZ mice, promote the repair of myelin in CPZ mice, and significantly reduce the levels of inflammatory response and oxidative stress in mice. Specifically, after PCB2 treatment, the expression levels of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in mice decreased significantly, while the expression level of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 increased. Meanwhile, the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as CAT, SOD, and GSH-Px also increased significantly. In addition, this study verified through RT-PCR and Western Blot techniques that PCB2 could increase the expression levels of the 4 key genes, namely KCNH2, PTGS1, ESR1, and VEGFA, in CPZ mice, further confirming that PCB2 may play its neuroprotective role by regulating the expression of these genes.</p>
<p id="Par74">By integrating bioinformatics and experimental approaches, our study creates a novel framework for translational medicine research in MS. We focus on specific cells (M2 macrophages), pathways (oxidative stress), and targets (KCNH2, PTGS1, ESR1, VEGFA), aiming to improve diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic outcomes, thus bridging the gap between basic research and clinical application.</p>
<p id="Par75">However, there are several limitations. First, the CPZ-induced mouse model of MS, while useful for mimicking demyelination, exhibits species-specific differences in immune regulation and myelin repair compared to human MS. Future investigations employing the EAE model will further validate PCB2’s immunomodulatory potential in a more comprehensive MS context. Second, The study relies solely on preclinical data without clinical validation of the key genes. Molecular docking and GSEA results need functional validation in human cells. Follow-up studies are needed to bridge the translation gap from preclinical findings to clinical application. </p></section><section id="Sec36"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="Par76">All in all, this study innovatively integrates drug target databases, disease-related public databases, and GEO resources with network pharmacology and bioinformatics techniques. It explores how PCB2 modulates key gene targets involved in oxidative stress, revealing their dual role as diagnostic and therapeutic markers. This framework interprets the translational research paradigm from drug discovery to disease diagnosis and treatment, providing a versatile template for other drug-disease pairs. The findings offer a theoretical basis for personalized MS treatment and highlight potential therapeutic targets. Future studies will validate these key genes clinically and explore PCB2’s mechanism.</p></section><section id="Sec37"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Information</h2>
