<ul id="celist0001" class="list" style="list-style-type:none">
<li id="celistitem0001">
<span class="label">•</span><p class="display-inline" id="para0002">Galectin-7 is significantly upregulated in high-risk thymic epithelial tumors and predicts poor prognosis.</p>
</li>
<li id="celistitem0002">
<span class="label">•</span><p class="display-inline" id="para0003">Galectin-7 promotes tumor progression by activating the MAPK signaling pathway.</p>
</li>
<li id="celistitem0003">
<span class="label">•</span><p class="display-inline" id="para0004">Galectin-7 depletion suppresses malignant phenotypes, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.</p>
</li>
</ul>
<section id="keys0001" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Thymic epithelial tumors, Galectin-7, Risk stratification, Biomarker, Tumor aggressiveness</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abs0002"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objective</h3>
<p>To identify molecular determinants of tumor aggressiveness in TETs and to elucidate their functional roles and underlying mechanisms in tumor progression.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>We performed proteomic profiling using data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry on 40 TET samples and their paired adjacent normal tissues to explore potential molecular differences. TETs were stratified into high-risk (WHO types B2, B3, and thymic carcinoma) and low-risk (types A, AB, and B1) groups based on histological classification. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was applied to the proteomic data to delineate pathways enriched in high-risk tumors. A validation cohort comprising 164 TET patients, along with 6 non-TET controls, was analyzed to assess Galectin-7 expression by immunohistochemistry and to evaluate its prognostic value. To further explore the biological role of Galectin-7, functional assays were performed in Tc1889 cells following Galectin-7 overexpression.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>Proteomic analysis revealed Galectin-7 as a highly upregulated protein in high-risk TETs. GSEA analysis identified enrichment of mitochondrial and extracellular matrix-related pathways in high-risk tumors. Immunohistochemistry showed Galectin-7 expression in 82 % of high-risk TETs and only 13 % of low-risk TETs (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.001), with higher expression correlating with advanced tumor stage and reduced progression-free survival. Functional assays demonstrated that Tc1889 cells with Galectin-7 overexpression exhibited enhanced proliferation and invasion. Additionally, MAPK signaling pathway activation was observed in Galectin-7-overexpressing cells.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h3>
<p>Galectin-7 is a potential biomarker for aggressive TETs, with expression levels associated with features of poor prognosis. These findings provide insight into TET biology and support further exploration of Galectin-7 in tumor stratification and therapeutic research.</p></section></section><section id="sec0001"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="para0012">Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs), which include thymomas and thymic carcinomas, are rare thoracic malignancies arising from the epithelial cells of the thymus, an organ critical for T-cell maturation., [<a href="#bib0001" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0001">1</a>]. They have an annual incidence of approximately 1.3 cases per million individuals in the United States[<a href="#bib0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0002">2</a>] and 4–5 cases per million in East Asia[<a href="#bib0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0003">3</a>,<a href="#bib0004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0004">4</a>]. The prognosis of TETs varies significantly based on histological subtype and tumor stage. Thymomas generally exhibit a 5-year survival rate of 70–90 %, whereas thymic carcinomas, which are less common but more aggressive, have a poorer prognosis, with 5-year survival rates ranging from 30 % to 67 %[<a href="#bib0002" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0002">[2]</a>, <a href="#bib0003" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0003">[3]</a>, <a href="#bib0004" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0004">[4]</a>, <a href="#bib0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0005">[5]</a>].</p>
<p id="para0013">TETs are histologically heterogeneous and clinically diverse, with prognosis closely associated with their histological classification. The classification of thymic tumors is an evolving process. In 2015, the World Health Organization (WHO) published an updated classification of thymic epithelial tumors, categorizing them into type A, AB, B1, B2, B3, and thymic carcinoma[<a href="#bib0006" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0006">6</a>]. This edition of the guidelines was the first to be developed using global data, rather than single-center data, and was created with the support of multidisciplinary experts and the International Thymic Malignancy Interest Group (ITMIG). Although the WHO classification provides a standardized framework for histological diagnosis, its prognostic value is limited by interobserver variability and lack of biological validation such as between B1 and B2 subtypes. In clinical practice and research[<a href="#bib0007" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0007">[7]</a>, <a href="#bib0008" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0008">[8]</a>, <a href="#bib0009" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0009">[9]</a>], TETs are often grouped into low-risk (types A, AB, B1) and high-risk (types B2, B3, and thymic carcinoma) categories based on their aggressiveness and outcomes. This low-risk/high-risk classification provides important prognostic information and directly influences surgical planning, the extent of resection, and decisions regarding adjuvant therapy. However, pathological assessment alone may be insufficient in accurately predicting clinical outcomes, particularly in borderline subtypes. Therefore, identifying molecular markers that can complement histological classification and refine risk stratification is of significant clinical value.</p>
<p id="para0014">Dysregulation and abnormal expression of certain genes and proteins are closely associated with the initiation, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of thymic tumors. Recent genomic studies, including those by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), have revealed the genetic landscape of TETs. For instance, recurrent GTF2I mutations are prevalent in type A and AB thymomas and correlate with favorable prognosis [<a href="#bib0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0010">10</a>,<a href="#bib0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0011">11</a>]. Conversely, type B and C thymomas exhibit p53 downregulation and oncogene upregulation, such as MYC and MYB [<a href="#bib0011" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0011">11</a>]. Recent studies have shown that a significant microRNA cluster on chromosome 19 is overexpressed in type A and AB thymomas, correlating with the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway[<a href="#bib0012" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0012">12</a>]. Furthermore, multiple studies have reported that PD-1 and PD-L1 exhibit high expression levels in TETs, potentially correlated with poor prognosis[<a href="#bib0013" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0013">[13]</a>, <a href="#bib0014" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0014">[14]</a>, <a href="#bib0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0015">[15]</a>, <a href="#bib0016" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0016">[16]</a>]. Despite these advances, existing molecular markers lack the specificity to address the heterogeneity of TETs, underscoring the need for more reliable biomarkers.</p>
<p id="para0015">Quantitative proteomics, particularly data-independent acquisition (DIA) and parallel reaction monitoring (PRM), has emerged as a powerful tool for identifying and validating biomarkers. These techniques provide high sensitivity, reproducibility, and throughput, making them ideal for analyzing large-scale clinical samples[<a href="#bib0017" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0017">17</a>,<a href="#bib0018" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0018">18</a>]. It is of critical importance to identify novel molecular markers that can reliably distinguish high-risk thymic epithelial tumors from low-risk subtypes and provide insight into their malignant potential. Such markers would not only enhance diagnostic accuracy and prognostic evaluation but also offer potential therapeutic targets for more individualized treatment strategies. In this study, we applied these proteomic approaches to thymic epithelial tumors to explore potential molecular markers of clinical relevance and to investigate their biological roles in tumor development and progression.</p></section><section id="sec0002"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Material and methods</h2>
<section id="sec0003"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Patients and study design</h3>
<p id="para0016">The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Shanghai Chest Hospital and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. A total of 164 patients with histologically confirmed TETs who underwent surgical resection were included. Eligibility criteria required complete clinical data and signed informed consent. Patients with a history of other malignancies or concurrent systemic diseases were excluded. Among the enrolled patients, a subset of 40 fresh-frozen tumor samples (17 low-risk and 23 high-risk) was selected for proteomic analysis based on sample availability and RNA/protein integrity. None of the patients in this proteomic cohort had received preoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Additionally, six non-TET patients undergoing cardiac surgery were included as controls to establish baseline protein expression profiles. Tumors were classified according to the 2021 WHO classification[<a href="#bib0019" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0019">19</a>] into low-risk (types A, AB, and B1) and high-risk (types B2, B3, and thymic squamous cell carcinoma [TSCC]) groups. Demographic and clinical variables were collected, including age, sex, histological subtype, TNM stage according to the 8th edition of the AJCC classification, preoperative treatment history, and resection margin status.</p></section><section id="sec0004"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Protein extraction, digestion, and desalting</h3>
<p id="para0017">Fresh tumor and adjacent normal tissues were collected during surgery. Samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for proteomic and RNA analyses. For histological evaluation, tissues were fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin and paraffin-embedded.</p>
<p id="para0018">For proteomic analysis, frozen tissues were lysed in 20 mM HEPES buffer (pH 8.0) containing 0.2 % acid-labile surfactant and protease inhibitors. Tissue homogenization was performed using ceramic beads under cold conditions, followed by 30 min of incubation on ice. Lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 × <em>g</em> for 30 min at 4 °C, and protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay.</p>
<p id="para0019">Proteins were denatured with 6 M urea, reduced with 5 mM TCEP, and alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide. After dilution to reduce urea concentration below 1 M, sequence-grade trypsin (Promega) was added at a 1:100 enzyme-to-protein ratio. Digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were then desalted using C18 spin columns (Thermo Fisher) and vacuum-dried for subsequent analysis.</p></section><section id="sec0005"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Mass spectrometry and spectral library construction</h3>
<p id="para0020">Peptides were dissolved in 0.1 % formic acid and analyzed using a Thermo Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer coupled with a Dionex UltiMate 3000 nano-LC system. Peptide separation was performed on a self-packed C18 column (75 µm × 25 cm) using a binary gradient of 0.1 % formic acid in water and 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile, with a 3–30 % Buffer B gradient over 120 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Data-independent acquisition (DIA) was used for quantitative proteomic analysis across a mass range of 400–1200 m<em>/z</em>, applying variable isolation windows to enhance precursor ion coverage. Raw MS files were collected for all samples for spectral matching and quantification.</p>
<p id="para0021">To construct a spectral library, pooled samples from representative groups were analyzed via data-dependent acquisition (DDA). Peptide identifications were performed against the UniProt human database using Proteome Discoverer, with a 1 % false discovery rate (FDR). The resulting spectra were imported into Spectronaut for spectral library generation, enabling robust peptide identification and quantification from DIA data.</p></section><section id="sec0006"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data processing and functional annotation</h3>
<p id="para0022">DIA-MS raw data were processed in Spectronaut for peptide identification and quantification. Protein intensities were normalized across samples, and differential expression analysis was conducted with thresholds of |log₂ fold-change| &gt; 1 and FDR &lt; 0.05. Protein-level quality control was assessed using principal component analysis (PCA) and correlation heatmaps to ensure data consistency. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R. Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were analyzed based on over-representation analysis (ORA), with significance defined by adjusted p-values &lt; 0.05. The background set included all proteins quantified in the DIA dataset. Pathway enrichment results were used to explore biological processes and signaling pathways associated with different TET subtypes. Data visualization was conducted using R packages including ggplot2 and heatmap. Visualization outputs included volcano plots, hierarchical clustering heatmaps, and PCA plots to highlight molecular distinctions between sample groups.</p></section><section id="sec0007"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunohistochemistry (IHC) validation and immunofluorescence</h3>
<p id="para0023">IHC staining was conducted on 5 µm-thick paraffin sections mounted on adhesive slides. Sections were baked at 65 °C for 2 h, deparaffinized with xylene, and rehydrated through graded ethanol to distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed via microwave treatment in citrate-EDTA buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 25 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3 % hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. After blocking with 5 % goat serum, slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary anti-Galectin-7 antibody (Abcam, ab206435). The following day, slides were treated with a secondary antibody (Dako) for 1 h and visualized using DAB chromogen. Counterstaining with hematoxylin was followed by dehydration, clearing, and mounting for evaluation. Whole-slide images were scanned using a Pannoramic MIDI scanner (3DHISTECH). Staining intensity (0–3) and the percentage of positive cells (0–100 %) were scored to calculate a composite IHC score (range: 0–12). Samples were categorized as low (0–4), moderate (5–8), or high (9–12) expressers.</p>
<p id="para0024">For immunofluorescence, similar antigen retrieval and blocking steps were followed. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Galectin-7 (Abcam, ab206435) and CK19 (Abcam, ab76539). After washing, slides were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 594, Abcam). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Stained sections were visualized using a fluorescence microscope, and images were captured for co-localization analysis.</p></section><section id="sec0008"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Functional assays</h3>
<section id="sec0009"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Cell lines and culture conditions</h4>
<p id="para0025">The human thymic epithelial tumor cell line Tc1889 was kindly provided by Professor Alexander Marx from University Hospital Heidelberg. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2. Cell identity was authenticated using short tandem repeat profiling.</p></section><section id="sec0010"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Generation of galectin-7 overexpression cell line</h4>
<p id="para0026">Lentiviral vectors for Galectin-7 overexpression and corresponding empty controls were obtained from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Tc1889 cells were infected with lentiviral particles in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene, followed by selection with 2 μg/mL puromycin for 7 days to establish stable cell lines. Overexpression efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis, with empty vector-transduced cells serving as controls.</p></section><section id="sec0011"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Proliferation assay</h4>
<p id="para0027">Tc1889 cell lines overexpressing Galectin-7 and empty vectors were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well. Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (HY-K0301, MCE, China) at 24, 48, 72, 96 and 120 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a BioTek microplate reader.</p></section><section id="sec0012"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Migration and invasion assays</h4>
<p id="para0028">Confluent Tc1889 cells were cultured in 6-well plates. A sterile pipette tip was used to create a wound, and debris was removed with PBS. Cells were incubated in serum-free medium, and wound closure was observed under a phase-contrast microscope at 0 and 24 h. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software to calculate the percentage of wound closure. Empty and Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers (8-μm pore size, Corning) were used to evaluate invasion. Cells (5 × 10^4) in serum-free medium were placed in the upper chamber, while 10 % FBS medium served as a chemoattractant in the lower chamber. After 48 h of incubation, cells that invaded the membrane were fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a light microscope in five random fields.</p></section></section><section id="sec0013"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">RNA-Seq and bioinformatics analysis</h3>
<section id="sec0014"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">RNA extraction and quality control</h4>
<p id="para0029">Total RNA was extracted from Tc1889 thymic epithelial tumor cell lines using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were lysed in RLT buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol, and RNA was isolated by column purification. The RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), while RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only samples with RNA Integrity Numbers (RIN) ≥ 7.0 were used for subsequent library preparation.</p></section><section id="sec0015"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Library preparation and sequencing</h4>
<p id="para0030">RNA-Seq libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Messenger RNA was enriched from total RNA using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation into short sequences. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase, followed by second-strand synthesis to generate double-stranded cDNA. Libraries were indexed, amplified by PCR, and purified to remove adapter dimers. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were subjected to quality control using FastQC (version 0.11.9) to check for sequencing quality, and Trim Galore! (version 0.6.6) was used to remove low-quality bases and adapter contamination.</p></section><section id="sec0016"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Bioinformatics analysis</h4>
<p id="para0031">Cleaned RNA-Seq reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2, and transcript abundance was quantified with StringTie. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log2 fold-change| &gt; 1 and FDR &lt; 0.05. GO and KEGG pathway analyses were performed to identify significantly enriched biological processes and pathways, with adjusted p-values &lt; 0.05. Visualizations, including volcano plots and heatmaps, were generated using R to highlight DEGs and their functional associations.</p></section></section></section><section id="sec0017"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<section id="sec0018"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Patients</h3>
<p id="para0032">This study included 164 patients with TETs and 6 non-TET control patients who underwent cardiac surgery. Among 164 patients with TETs, 40 patients (17 low-risk and 23 high-risk) with available frozen tumor tissues were selected for proteomic analyses. TET cases were classified into low-risk (A/AB/B1 thymomas, <em>n</em> = 60) and high-risk groups (B2/B3 thymomas and TSCC, <em>n</em> = 104) based on the WHO histologic classification. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort are summarized in <a href="#tbl0001" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>. No significant differences were observed in the median age at diagnosis (52 vs. 51 years, <em>p</em> = 0.973) or sex distribution between the low-risk and high-risk groups (<em>p</em> = 0.105). High-risk TETs were also associated with advanced IASLC/ITMIG T stages (T3/T4: 47.1 % vs. 3.3 %, <em>p</em> &lt; 0.001) and a higher incidence of incomplete resections (R1/R2: 22.1 % vs. 3.4 %, <em>p</em> = 0.001). Preoperative treatment, including chemotherapy or radiation, was administered more frequently in high-risk patients (16.3 % vs. 1.6 %, <em>p</em> = 0.003). Galectin-7 expression levels, assessed by immunohistochemistry, were markedly elevated in high-risk TETs, with 58.7 % of cases showing moderate to strong expression (scores 5–12) compared to 3.4 % in the low-risk group (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.001). These findings highlight significant clinical and molecular differences between low-risk and high-risk TETs, emphasizing the distinct biological and pathological characteristics of these subgroups.</p>

<p id="para0033">Proteomic profiling was performed on tumor and adjacent normal tissues from 40 TET patients, including TSCC, high-risk TETs (B2/B3 thymomas), low-risk TETs (A/AB/B1 thymomas), and normal thymus tissues. The experimental workflow, including tissue processing, protein extraction, DIA-MS analysis, and data validation, is outlined in <a href="#fig0001" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><strong>a</strong>. Heatmap analysis demonstrated distinct expression patterns across the sample groups, with Galectin-7 being prominently upregulated in high-risk TETs compared to low-risk TETs and adjacent normal tissues (<a href="#fig0001" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><strong>b</strong>). PCA analysis further confirmed clear molecular separation between high-risk and low-risk TETs, with minimal overlap between the two groups (<a href="#fig0001" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><strong>c</strong>). Volcano plot analysis highlighted Galectin-7 as one of the most significantly upregulated proteins in high-risk TETs, with a log2 fold-change of 1.5 and adjusted p-value &lt; 0.05 (<a href="#fig0001" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><strong>d</strong>).</p>

<p id="para0035">Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on thymic epithelial tumor (TET) tissues to validate the differential expression of Galectin-7 observed in proteomic analyses. Paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues revealed significantly higher Galectin-7 expression in tumor samples, with TSCC demonstrating the strongest staining intensity (<a href="#fig0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a><strong>a-b</strong>). Quantitative IHC scoring showed a consistent pattern: high-risk TETs (B2/B3/TSCC) exhibited significantly stronger Galectin-7 expression compared to low-risk TETs (A/AB/B1) and normal thymus tissues (<em>p</em> = 0.0097, <a href="#fig0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a><strong>b</strong>). Galectin-7 staining was predominantly localized to the cytoplasm and nucleus of tumor epithelial cells in high-risk TETs, whereas low-risk TETs showed negligible expression (<a href="#fig0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>c). Notably, TSCC tissues exhibited the most robust and diffuse Galectin-7 staining, correlating with their aggressive clinical behavior (<a href="#fig0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a><strong>c</strong>). Quantitative analysis of IHC scores (<a href="#fig0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a><strong>d</strong>) revealed that Galectin-7 expression in TSCC was significantly higher than in adjacent normal tissues, as well as in A, AB and B1 thymomas (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.001). To further quantify Galectin-7 expression across TET subtypes, <a href="#tbl0002" class="usa-link">Table 2</a> summarizes the distribution of Galectin-7 IHC scores (0–12), showing significantly higher expression levels in high-risk subtypes compared to low-risk subtypes. Further quantitative comparison of Galectin-7 expression between low-risk (A/AB/B1) and high-risk (B2/B3/TSCC) TETs showed a dramatic increase in expression in high-risk subtypes, consistent with their aggressive biological behavior (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.0001, <a href="#fig0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a><strong>e</strong>). In addition to differential staining intensity, co-localization studies revealed specific patterns of Galectin-7 distribution. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that Galectin-7 was strongly co-localized with CK19 in TSCC tumor cells, confirming its epithelial origin and subcellular localization within the nucleus, cytoplasm, and extracellular space (<a href="#fig0002" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a><strong>f</strong>). In contrast, Galectin-7 expression was notably minimal in type A thymomas, consistent with its negligible IHC staining observed in low-risk TETs. These results confirm that Galectin-7 is significantly overexpressed in high-risk TETs and underscore its potential as a biomarker for tumor aggressiveness and subtype differentiation.</p>

<p id="para0036">To explore the transcriptional changes induced by Galectin-7, RNA-seq analysis was performed on Tc1889 cells overexpressing Galectin-7 (Galectin-7 OE) compared to negative control (NC) cells. A global heatmap of gene expression revealed distinct transcriptional profiles between the Galectin-7-OE and NC groups, with clear clustering of differentially expressed genes (<a href="#fig0003" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a><strong>b</strong>). Notably, genes involved in proliferation and signaling pathways, such as MAPK and DUSP6, were prominently upregulated in the Galectin-7-OE group. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further highlighted significant activation of the MAPK signaling pathway (<a href="#fig0003" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a><strong>d-e</strong>). The MAPK signaling pathway was specifically enriched, as demonstrated in the KEGG enrichment plot, with key genes such as MAPK1, MAP2K1, and DUSP6 showing consistent upregulation (<a href="#fig0003" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a><strong>f</strong>) These findings provide molecular evidence supporting the role of Galectin-7 as a driver of tumor progression in TETs.</p>

<p id="para0043">TETs represent a rare and heterogeneous group of neoplasms, and risk stratification remains a clinical challenge due to the lack of reliable molecular biomarkers. This study aimed to identify candidate molecules that could distinguish high-risk from low-risk TETs and provide insight into their malignant potential. To address this, we performed comprehensive proteomic profiling of thymic tumor tissues and identified significant differences in protein expression between high-risk and low-risk subtypes. Functional enrichment analyses revealed that high-risk TETs were characterized by enhanced activity in mitochondrial and extracellular matrix-related pathways, while cell adhesion pathways were suppressed. Among the differentially expressed proteins, Galectin-7 emerged as a top upregulated candidate in high-risk tumors. Given its previously reported roles in tumor progression in other malignancies, we further evaluated its clinical relevance and functional contribution to TET biology.</p>
<p id="para0044">Although all galectins share β-galactoside-binding capacity, they exhibit markedly distinct biological functions depending on their structural type and cellular localization. Galectin-1 and Galectin-3 are the most extensively studied in malignant tumors. Galectin-1 primarily mediates immune suppression and angiogenesis, inducing apoptosis of activated T cells and facilitating tumor immune escape, while Galectin-3 acts as a multifunctional scaffold protein, promoting EMT, metastasis, and resistance through receptor lattice formation and activation of integrin/RTK-dependent signaling cascades[<a href="#bib0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0020">[20]</a>, <a href="#bib0021" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0021">[21]</a>, <a href="#bib0022" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0022">[22]</a>]. Galectin-7—a prototype galectin transcriptionally regulated by p53—shows high epithelial specificity and striking context dependency. Initially identified as a marker of stratified epithelia, Galectin-7 maintains epithelial homeostasis and intercellular adhesion under physiological conditions[<a href="#bib0023" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0023">23</a>]. However, in cancer, its function diverges according to tumor type, microenvironment, and subcellular localization. In several epithelial cancers such as breast and squamous cell carcinoma, Galectin-7 is pro-tumorigenic, enhancing invasion and metastasis through MMP-9 upregulation and activation of MAPK and TCF3 signaling[<a href="#bib0024" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0024">24</a>,<a href="#bib0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0025">25</a>]More recently, Galectin-7 upregulation has been shown to enhance antitumor immunity, converting immunologically “cold” tumors into inflamed, T-cell-infiltrated microenvironments by promoting CD8⁺ T-cell proliferation and cytokine production, as demonstrated in microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer following EHMT2 inhibition[<a href="#bib0027" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0027">26</a>].</p>
<p id="para0045">Elevated Galectin-7 expression in high-risk subtypes, as confirmed by proteomic profiling and immunohistochemistry, supports its value as a biological indicator of aggressive tumor behavior. High-risk tumors (B2/B3 thymomas and TSCC) exhibited markedly higher Galectin-7 expression compared to low-risk counterparts (A/AB/B1 thymomas) and adjacent normal tissues. These results align with previous studies highlighting the role of Galectin-7 in promoting tumor invasion and metastasis in other malignancies, such as cervical, head and neck, and breast cancer[<a href="#bib0024" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0024">[24]</a>, <a href="#bib0028" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0028">[27]</a>, <a href="#bib0029" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0029">[28]</a>]. Furthermore, Galectin-7 expression was closely associated with advanced TNM and Masaoka stages, incomplete resections, and shorter PFS, underscoring its relevance as a surrogate marker of poor clinical outcomes.</p>
<p id="para0046">Previous studies have reported that Galectin-7 is involved in multiple oncogenic or tumor-suppressive signaling pathways, such as the p53, JNK, and NF-κB pathways, which may explain its dual roles in different tumor types[<a href="#bib0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0030">29</a>,<a href="#bib0031" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0031">30</a>]. In our study, we demonstrated that in thymic epithelial tumors, Galectin-7 promotes tumor progression mainly through activation of the p38-MAPK pathway. This finding provides new insight into the functional mechanism of Galectin-7 in thymic tumors. Due to the inherently low expression of Galectin-7 in the Tc1889 cell line and the limited availability of thymic tumor cell line models, gene knockout experiments were not feasible. Although overexpression may not fully mimic endogenous regulation, it offers a practical strategy to investigate the biological functions of Galectin-7 under resource constraints. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed that Galectin-7 overexpression in Tc1889 cells upregulates key components of the MAPK signaling pathway, including MAPK1, DUSP5, and DUSP6. This finding was further validated through western blotting analyses, where Galectin-7 overexpression was shown to activate of p38 MAPK phosphorylation. MAPK signaling is a well-established oncogenic pathway implicated in various cancers, promoting cell proliferation, survival, and metastatic potential[<a href="#bib0032" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0032">31</a>,<a href="#bib0033" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0033">32</a>]. The identification of Galectin-7 as an upstream regulator of this pathway highlights its potential as a therapeutic target. In vitro functional assays confirmed that Galectin-7 overexpression enhances tumor growth and invasiveness. Specifically, Galectin-7-overexpressing Tc1889 cells exhibited increased expression of PCNA and MMP9, markers associated with cell proliferation and ECM degradation, respectively. The robust association of Galectin-7 with tumor stage, resection status, and survival outcomes positions it as a more reliable indicator of tumor aggressiveness.</p>
<p id="para0047">In addition to the Galectin-7/MAPK axis, the thymic microenvironment itself may play a critical role in tumor immune regulation. Human Hassall’s corpuscles, specialized structures within the thymic medulla, highly express thymic stromal lymphopoietin, which activates thymic dendritic cells and promotes the differentiation of regulatory T cells[<a href="#bib0034" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0034">33</a>,<a href="#bib0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0035">34</a>]. Treg cells are essential for maintaining immune tolerance, and their dysfunction has been implicated in autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis. In thymomas, architectural disruption of Hassall’s corpuscles within tumor tissues may lead to impaired Treg development or function, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion. As a “signaling hub” of the thymic medulla, Hassall’s corpuscles maintain T-cell homeostasis through coordinated mechanisms involving cellular degradation, cytokine secretion, and immune interaction. Future studies should further elucidate the molecular regulation of keratinization and signaling processes within Hassall’s corpuscles and explore the therapeutic potential of targeting this structure in autoimmune disorders and thymic malignancies. This small but complex microstructure may hold the key to understanding the deeper mechanisms of immune balance and tumor immunity.</p>
<p id="para0048">Beyond its mechanistic insights, our study provides translational implications linking Galectin-7 expression to prognostic prediction and therapeutic potential in thymic epithelial tumors. Given its strong correlation with advanced stage, incomplete resection, and unfavorable survival, Galectin-7 may serve as a robust biomarker for identifying high-risk patients who could benefit from adjuvant therapy. By delineating the Galectin-7/MAPK axis, our findings also offer a molecular rationale for targeted therapeutic intervention. Inhibition of MAPK components, such as MEK or ERK, has demonstrated efficacy across multiple solid tumors[<a href="#bib0036" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0036">35</a>,<a href="#bib0037" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib0037">36</a>]. Integrating such agents into TET treatment regimens, particularly in combination with immune checkpoint blockade, may represent a promising precision-therapy approach for aggressive subtypes. Collectively, these insights highlight the dual diagnostic and therapeutic relevance of Galectin-7, bridging molecular discovery with potential clinical translation.</p>
<p id="para0049">Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. The sample size for transcriptomic and functional analyses was relatively limited, and further validation in independent and larger cohorts is warranted. Additionally, our in vitro overexpression model may not fully recapitulate endogenous Galectin-7 regulation in thymic epithelial tumors. The use of strong-promoter-driven overexpression may introduce non-physiological effects, and comparisons with cell lines that naturally exhibit high Galectin-7 expression were not feasible. Future work will focus on employing endogenous regulatory systems, such as inducible promoters or CRISPR-based activation platforms, as well as thymic tumor organoid and patient-derived xenograft models, to validate these findings under more physiologically relevant conditions. Moreover, future studies should investigate the upstream regulatory mechanisms of Galectin-7 and its cross-talk with additional oncogenic pathways, including MAPK and immune-related signaling. Ultimately, clinical trials evaluating the therapeutic potential of targeting Galectin-7 and its downstream effectors will be essential to translate these mechanistic insights into clinical benefit for patients with TETs.</p></section><section id="sec0025"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="para0050">In summary, this study identifies Galectin-7 as a biomarker associated with high-risk TETs and provides mechanistic insights into its role in tumor progression. Through proteomic, transcriptomic, and functional analyses, we demonstrated that Galectin-7 contributes to tumor aggressiveness by activating the MAPK signaling pathway. These findings offer a biological rationale for improved risk stratification and provide a potential foundation for targeted therapeutic strategies in TETs.</p></section><section id="sec0026"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Ethical statement</h2>
