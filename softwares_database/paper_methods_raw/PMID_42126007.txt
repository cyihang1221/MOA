
METHODS
Study system
Study area
Napa Valley is 100 km north of San Francisco, California, between
the Vaca and Mayacamas mountains. A Mediterranean climate
coupled with diverse soils renders the valley ideal for winegrape
production (Napa Valley Vintners, 2014). The region supports a
mosaic of almost 500 wineries (about 20,000 ha) surrounded by
native oak woodlands, grasslands, forests, and riparian areas (Napa
County, 2010; Wendt & Johnson, 2017).
Barn owls
Barn owls provide a compelling case for rodent pest control due to
several life history traits. The barn owl group is nearly
cosmopolitan, meaning a variety of agricultural systems around the
world can benefit (Roulin, 2020). Species in the barn owl group are
also generalists that consume rodent species in correspondence to
what is locally available, indicating a capacity to prey‐switch and
remain in an area even when certain rodent species are at low
density (Roulin, 2020; Tores et al., 2005). Furthermore, barn owls
can reach high densities (Smith et al., 1974), are readily attracted
by nest boxes (Labuschagne et al., 2016), and hunt predominantly
near nesting locations (Castañeda et al., 2021; Roulin, 2020).
Rodents
In Napa Valley vineyards, the most common groups of aboveground
foraging rodents are deer mice (Peromyscus spp.), house mice (M. 
musculus), and California voles (M. californicus; Hansen &
Johnson, 2022). Voles and house mice are known to prefer dense
vegetation (Dickman, 1992; Jensen et al., 2003;
Wywialowski, 1987), whereas deer mice are generalists who tend to
prefer more open environments (Garman, 1994; Kamler &
Pennock, 2004; Kaufman et al., 1988; Wolf et al., 2018). For
growers, vole herbivory is particularly harmful to young vines,
especially during eruptive years (every 6–8 years; Ross, 2009). Most
game camera detections in this study were deer mice (see Results ),
consistent with live trapping conducted in 2019, when 98% of
captures were Peromyscus spp. (Hansen & Johnson, 2022). Voles
were detected in less than 1% of photos in this study, also
consistent with 2019 surveys where zero voles were captured
(Hansen & Johnson, 2022). American Barn Owl diets in the region
vary considerably with local habitat composition (St.
George, 2021), ranging from 7% to 53% mice (Mus musculus,
Peromyscus spp.; mean = 22.0%), 5%–76% vole (Microtus
californicus; mean = 49.6%), and <1%–35% gopher (Thomomys
bottae; mean = 17.5%).
Rodent measurements
Index of rodent abundance (chew blocks)
To determine relative rodent abundance, we deployed grids of chew
blocks, which are small (~20 g) cubes of wax and seeds that are
attractive to aboveground foraging rodents such as mice and voles.
Chewing indices effectively monitor population size for numerous
rodent species including California voles, house mice, and deer mice
(Caughley et al., 1998; Engeman & Whisson, 2006; Engeman &
Witmer, 2000; Whisson et al., 2005). Unlike depletable trays of
seeds with harvest rates that decline as the food diminishes (see
GUD below), the visitation to and evidence of foraging on chew
blocks should increase with the density of local aboveground
rodents (Engeman, 2016). We deployed four chew block grids on
each of six vineyards (n = 24 grids), four vineyards in the southern
Carneros region of Napa Valley and two in the mid‐valley near the
towns of Yountville and Oakville. On each vineyard, we chose grid
locations that spanned a range of anticipated American Barn Owl
hunting pressures, based on their distance from existing nest boxes
and past occupancy and nest success data (Carlino, 2024). Grids
were at least 200 m apart to maintain independence. All grid
locations were sampled twice, once in February through March (at
the onset of nest box occupancy and courtship when anticipated
hunting pressure by owls was low), and again in May–June (when
owl nestlings are developing, corresponding to higher hunting
pressures; St. George, 2021). Each grid consisted of 36 chew blocks,
arranged in a pattern of 6 × 6 blocks spaced approximately 5 m
apart (chew blocks were placed in every other vine row). We used
NoTox (Liphatech, Inc., Milwaukee, WI) chew blocks, as we found
this brand to be more water resistant than DETEX (Bell
Laboratories, Inc., Madison, WI). Since the blocks are not truly
waterproof, and rain could influence our results, we placed a 16 qt
plastic storage container (16.75 × 11.88 × 7 cm) with six
rectangular 7 × 7 cm openings in the sides over each chew block to
allow rodent access (Appendix S1: Figure S1). We recorded the
presence or absence of bite marks after 4 days for each chew block.
Eight of the chew blocks in each grid were paired with remote
cameras (four Bushnell Core S‐4K No Glow model #119949C and
four Browning Strike Force Elite HD model BTC‐5HDE) to provide
imagery to confirm the species visiting and chewing on the blocks.
We identified species in four groups: deer mice, house mice, voles,
and unknown. Voles were distinguished from mice based on their
relatively large body size, small tail, and dark coloration. Deer mice
were distinguished from house mice based on a bicolored tail, large
eyes, and large ears.
