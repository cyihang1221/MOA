<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=10854760_js3c00353_0005.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/c816/10854760/1c2d4f2af6d6/js3c00353_0005.jpg" loading="lazy" id="ab-tgr1" height="277" width="671" alt="graphic file with name js3c00353_0005.jpg"></a></p>
<p>Calculating
spectral similarity is a fundamental step in MS/MS
data analysis in untargeted metabolomics experiments, as it facilitates
the identification of related spectra and the annotation of compounds.
To improve matching accuracy when querying an experimental mass spectrum
against a spectral library, previous approaches have proposed increasing
peak intensities for high <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> ranges.
These high <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values tend to be
smaller in magnitude, yet they offer more crucial information for
identifying the chemical structure. Here, we evaluate the impact of
using these weights for identifying structurally related compounds
and mass spectral library searches. Additionally, we propose a weighting
approach that (i) takes into account the frequency of the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values within a spectral library in
order to assign higher importance to the most common peaks and (ii)
increases the intensity of lower peaks, similar to previous approaches.
To demonstrate our approach, we applied weighting preprocessing to
modified cosine, entropy, and fidelity distance metrics and benchmarked
it against previously reported weights. Our results demonstrate how
weighting-based preprocessing can assist in annotating the structure
of unknown spectra as well as identifying structurally similar compounds.
Finally, we examined scenarios in which the utilization of weights
resulted in diminished performance, pinpointing spectral features
where the application of weights might be detrimental.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">1. Introduction</h2>
<p>Mass spectrometry plays
a crucial role in the identification and
characterization of molecules that are found in biological samples.
Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) has emerged as a powerful technique
for elucidating the structure of molecules by generating fragmentation
patterns and spectra.<sup><a href="#ref1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref1">1</a></sup> To predict the chemical
structure, we matched unknown spectra to a library of known spectra
using spectral similarity metrics. However, spectral libraries exclusively
cover a fraction of the chemical space. For instance, the largest
spectral libraries cover less than a million unique structures and
the chemical space is estimated to be 10<sup>60</sup> structures.<sup><a href="#ref2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref2">2</a></sup> Consequently, as spectral libraries expand, optimizing
spectral similarity metrics remains a fundamental approach to enhancing
the accuracy of annotating unknown spectra.</p>
<p>Although newly introduced
machine learning approaches like MS2DeepScore<sup><a href="#ref3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref3">3</a></sup> have been developed, Cosine (dot product) similarity
and other alternative metrics such as spectral entropy<sup><a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup> are regarded as key measures for evaluating the similarity
between pairs of MS/MS spectra. These spectral similarity metrics
function by comparing and analyzing fragment ion intensities and peak
distributions. One notable advantage of these metrics is their transparency.
This transparency enables scientists to explore the fragmentation
patterns that contribute to a high similarity score.</p>
<p>Several
recent studies have compared the relative performances
of different similarity metrics and proposed new ones. In a recent
study by Li et al.,<sup><a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup> which evaluated the
performance of 42 similarity metrics for mass spectral library searches
(matching an unknown spectrum against a reference database) across
two of the largest libraries (i.e., the National Institute of Standards
and Technology (NIST) and MassBank of North America), spectral entropy
outperformed the established methods, including cosine similarity.
More recently, Bittremieux and colleagues<sup><a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> benchmarked modified cosine similarity, cosine similarity, and neutral
loss<sup><a href="#ref6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref6">6</a></sup> on the task of spectral alignment.
Unlike library search, which aims to match two near-identical spectra,
spectral alignment aims to identify similar spectra corresponding
to structurally related compounds that are not necessarily exact matches.
Spectral alignment tasks typically operate with larger <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance windows than library search tasks and
are the methods typically used in molecular networking.<sup><a href="#ref7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref7">7</a></sup></p>
<p>Some of these similarity metrics place
a disproportionate emphasis
on the highest peaks in spectral similarity calculations. For example,
the function for cosine similarity, one of the most commonly used
metrics, involves summing products. In this context, each summand
is the product of two intensities. When two intensities in a product
are high, their contribution to the total sum is substantial. Conversely,
when the two intensities in a product are low, even if they align
well, their contribution to the sum is much lower. To address this
concern, Sokolow et al.<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> were, to our knowledge,
the first researchers to introduce scaling the intensities; in this
case, they proposed applying the square root of an intensity multiplied
by its <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value. This adjustment
aimed to enhance the results of mass spectral library searches by
boosting peak intensities for low peaks, particularly focusing on
the high <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> ranges. Building upon
this work, Stein and Scott<sup><a href="#ref9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref9">9</a></sup> conducted a
benchmarking exercise using the 1992 release of the NIST database.
They discovered that cosine similarity, with intensities raised to
the power of 0.6 times their <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value cubed, emerged as the most effective metric for conducting
library searches. Similarly, Stein<sup><a href="#ref10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref10">10</a></sup> explored
the idea of increasing the weights for low <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values.</p>
<p>In the realm of gas chromatography–mass
spectrometry (GC–MS)
and in a more contemporary context, Horai et al.<sup><a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a></sup> reported that the optimal weight factor is a function of
the square root of the intensity and the square of its <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value. Kim et al.<sup><a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a></sup> proposed a method for determining optimal weights for compound identification
also in the context of GC–MS. They identified 0.53 as the optimal
weight for intensity scaling and 1.3 for mass weighting, utilizing
the NIST database and aligning with prior findings. More recently,
Li and Fiehn<sup><a href="#ref13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref13">13</a></sup> expanded upon their novel
concept of spectral entropy similarity<sup><a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup> by incorporating weighted intensities based on spectral entropy.
Their method keeps the intensity values unaltered in spectra with
a spectral entropy of at least three. If the spectral entropy is below
3, the intensity is raised to the power of 0.25(1 + <em>S</em>) (see <a href="#eq1" class="usa-link">eq <a href="#eq1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>), where <em>S</em> is the spectral entropy of the spectrum. Lastly, weighting
based on the peak occurrence has also been proposed in the field of
proteomics during spectral clustering.<sup><a href="#ref14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref14">14</a></sup> In this particular case, infrequent peaks are upweighted rather
than frequent peaks, conceptually attempting to emphasize more discriminative
signals.</p>

<p>In this study, we investigate the impact of
applying weights to
spectral alignment as a preprocessing for the purpose of identifying
structurally related compounds and mass spectral library searches.
We build upon a variety of similarity metrics and data sets utilized
in recent benchmarks.<sup><a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a>,<a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> We also introduce a weighting
method for spectral intensity values that considers the relative frequency
of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values in a reference database.
This approach involves weighting up peaks with common <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values. Alongside these weights, we assign higher
weights to peaks with low intensities, similar to the methods mentioned
earlier. Regarding the task of identifying structurally related compounds,
we demonstrate enhancements across 42 distinct spectral similarity
metrics through the application of our weighting strategy. Finally,
we demonstrate that applying weights also improves results when querying
spectral libraries.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Methods</h2>
<section id="sec2.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Weighting

<p>In this subsection,

<section id="sec2.1.1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1.1. Weighting Intensities</h4>
<p>Normalizing
the intensities is a common preprocessing step. Previous studies,<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a>−<a href="#ref10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref10">10</a></sup> used intensity weighting functions following the format of <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">eq <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">2</a></a> which considers peak intensities

the frequencies of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values (<a href="#eq3" class="usa-link">eq <a href="#eq3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>), where g is a normalization

Low Intensity Peaks</h4>
<p>Drawing
on previous research,<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a>−<a href="#ref10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref10">10</a></sup> we implemented weights designed to amplify peaks
with lower intensities within a given spectrum. By definition, many
similarity metrics, such as cosine similarity, put disproportionate
emphasis on peaks with higher intensities. These intensity-based weighting
methods were introduced to mitigate the dominance of these high-intensity
peaks in the calculated similarity scores. To address this issue,
Sokolow<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> first introduced an intensity
weighting function that raised the intensity values to a power of <em>n</em> (as shown on the left side of <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">eq <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>). Various values for n have been utilized
in the literature including <em>n</em> = 0.5 (Sokolow et al.;<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> Horai et al.<sup><a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a></sup>), <em>n</em> = 0.6 (Stein and Scott<sup><a href="#ref9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref9">9</a></sup>), and <em>n</em> = 0.53 (Kim et al.<sup><a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a></sup>). We adopt
a similar approach for intensity weighting using <em>n</em> = 0.25. However, we add an additional normalization step by dividing
the intensities of each spectrum by the highest intensity value. This
normalization enabled us to assign weights to peaks based on their
relative intensity in comparison to the peak with the highest intensity
rather than relying solely on their raw intensity values (as illustrated
in <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 1A,B</a>).</p></section><section id="sec2.1.3"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1.3. Rescaling Based on <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> Peak
Occurrence</h4>
<p>In addition to weighting low
intensity peaks, we hypothesized that weighting intensities approximating
the density of peaks across the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> range could enhance the performance of similarity metrics. We implemented
this by calculating the frequency of occurrence for the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values in a reference library. The <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> range had previously been employed for intensity
weighting.<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a>−<a href="#ref10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref10">10</a></sup> However, prior weighting approaches had multiplied
the intensity weights (described in <a href="#sec2.1.2" class="usa-link">section <a href="#sec2.1.2" class="usa-link">2.1.2</a></a>) by the corresponding <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value raised to some power <em>m</em> (as shown on the right side of <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">eq <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>). In our methodology, we take a different approach
by multiplying our intensity weights by the relative frequency of
the corresponding <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value raised
to the power of 0.25 (as shown on the right side of <a href="#eq3" class="usa-link">eq <a href="#eq3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>). This weighting approach accentuates
intensities linked to frequently occurring <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values (see an example in <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 1</a>). To determine the relative <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> frequency values, we employed the GNPS<sup><a href="#ref15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref15">15</a></sup> data set from Bittremieux et al.<sup><a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> We identified the number of spectra within the data set exhibiting
a peak at individual <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values
(rounded to 1 decimal place) (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 2</a>). Subsequently, we calculated the relative frequency for
each <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value by considering the
percentage of spectra in the data set exhibiting a particular <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> value.</p></section></section><section id="sec2.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Spectrum
Similarity Metrics</h3>

the best-performing similarity metrics identified by Li et al.<sup><a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup> in their benchmark, including spectral entropy,

<section id="sec2.3.1"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3.1. Spectral Alignment for Identifying Structurally
Related Compounds</h4>
<p>To evaluate the spectrum similarity measures
on how well they align structurally related compounds, we leveraged
the benchmark data set released by Bittremieux et al.<sup><a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> This data set consists of 495,600 MS/MS spectra from small
molecules available in GNPS.<sup><a href="#ref15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref15">15</a></sup> We applied
filtering steps similar to those in the previous application conducted
in the original benchmark (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Table 2</a>). Given the large number of spectrum pairs resulting from
all possible combinations of the data set, Bittremieux et al.<sup><a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> benchmarked the similarity metrics on 10 million
randomly selected MS/MS spectrum pairs. Thus, we benchmarked all similarity
metrics described in sub<a href="#sec2.2" class="usa-link">section <a href="#sec2.2" class="usa-link">2.2</a></a> on the same 10 million spectrum pairs with and without
applying our weighting approach. Lastly, following the original evaluation,
we compared the resulting similarity scores on these 10 million pairs
to the chemical similarity between the two molecules, which is defined

(using default settings on v2023.03.1).<sup><a href="#ref16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref16">16</a></sup></p></section><section id="sec2.3.2"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3.2. Mass Spectral Library Search Using GNPS</h4>
<p>To evaluate our weighting approach on a retrieval task (mass spectral

spectra, also used by the previous benchmark by Li et al.<sup><a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup> in its prior version. We also leveraged an independent
data set, GNPS, which was described in the previous subsection. We
filtered both data sets using a similar filtering procedure as that
of Bittremieux et al.<sup><a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Table 2</a>). We filtered the GNPS data set to only
include spectra for molecules that were present in the NIST23 data

the filtered GNPS data from 2,776 unique molecules that are also present

NIST23 (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>B)

of 100 and 5,000 ppm to demonstrate how each method would scale when
more diverse spectra were included in the queries (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">1</a></a>C).</p>

<section id="sec3.1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.1. Spectral Alignment for
Identifying Structurally
Related Compounds</h3>
<p>Spectral alignment is crucial for identifying
groups of similar compounds used in applications, such as molecular
networking. This involves calculating the similarity between pairs
of spectra, followed by the representation of compounds from one or
more samples in a network. Each node in the network corresponds to
a compound with edges depicting their spectral similarity. This network-based
approach allows researchers to identify related compounds, infer potential
biosynthetic pathways, and highlight differences or similarities in
metabolic profiles between samples.</p>
<p>In this first application,
we evaluated whether applying the proposed weights to the most commonly
used similarity metrics yielded enhanced outcomes in the identification
of structurally related compounds. To do so, we replicated the recent
benchmark presented by Bittremieux et al.<sup><a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> and expanded it with over 40 additional similarity metrics. Our
results indicate that applying weights on the intensities and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> frequencies improves spectral alignment.
This improvement is evident as the spectral similarities more closely
resemble their corresponding Tanimoto coefficients after applying
weights (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a> and <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 3</a>). For example, when weights
are applied to modified cosine similarity, the top-performing metric
reported by Bittremieux et al.,<sup><a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> we observe
a gradual rise in spectral similarity scores as the Tanimoto coefficient
increases from 0 to 1 (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>A). This trend is observed across most of the benchmarked
metrics, including spectral entropy, Bhattacharya 1, and fidelity
similarity, metrics which showcased superior performance in the spectral
library search benchmark by Li et al.<sup><a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup> (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 3</a>).</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="fig2"><h4 class="obj_head">Figure 2.</h4>

yielded by spectral entropy and modified cosine..</p></figcaption></figure><p>Focusing on the spectral similarities for closely related structures,
we noted a substantial increase in the spectral similarity scores
when weights were applied. To confirm this, we calculated the optimal
spectral similarity threshold to achieve the maximum F1 score in the
case of modified cosine similarity for both distributions without
and with weights. This effectively enabled us to quantify how accurately
can a spectral similarity cutoff separate between structurally similar
and dissimilar pairs, which we define using a standard threshold of
0.7 Tanimoto coefficient.<sup><a href="#ref21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref21">21</a></sup> In the case
of the original spectral similarities obtained with modified cosine,
we found that the optimal threshold was 0.88, which achieved an F1-score
of 0.094. Similarly, commonly used cut-offs such as 0.7 yielded a
F1 score of 0.0854. Using weights on modified cosine with the optimal
threshold (0.67) improved the F1-score significantly to 0.14. To contextualize
these results in the classical application of spectral alignment,
molecular networking, using these optimal cut-offs, applying weights
yields approximately 30% accurately predicted edges (i.e., 3,568 vs
2,773) while reducing the number of false positives by approximately
800 edges (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>B,C). Similarly, we also observed the advantage of applying weights
when increasing the Tanimoto coefficient threshold (e.g., a Tanimoto
threshold of 0.8 yielded a F1-score of 0.089 with the original similarities
(optimal cutoff = 0.95) and 0.125 using weights (optimal cutoff =
0.74)) and optimizing the cutoff based on F2-score in order to favor
recall over precision. Lastly, the advantage of using weights is also
highlighted for pairs of spectra with lower structural similarity,
which constitutes the majority of cases. Importantly, both applying
and not applying weights to pairs of structures with low structural
similarity (i.e., Tanimoto coefficient &lt;0.2) do not yield high
spectral similarities (i.e., false positives). However, for pairs
with a Tanimoto coefficient between 0.4 and 0.8, not applying weights
still yields low spectral similarities, while applying weights significantly
shifts the distributions upward for these pairs with a “medium”
structural similarity.</p>
<p>Additionally, we evaluated the overlap
of the true positives for
the weighted and unweighted versions of spectral entropy and modified
cosine (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>E).
The method with the highest number of accurate predictions is modified
cosined (weighted), followed by spectral entropy (weighted). We would
like to note that it is not surprising that, across all methods, modified
cosine has the highest number of accurate predictions, since it is
the only method we have benchmarked that finds the best possible matches
by adjusting spectral alignment. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that
spectral entropy (weighted) yielded a higher number of true positives
than modified cosine (unweighted). Furthermore, all four methods exhibited
a large overlap (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>E, last column). Lastly, when examining specific intersections, we
found that the largest overlap between two distinct methods was between
modified cosine (weighted) and spectral entropy (weighted), closely
followed by modified cosine (weighted) and modified cosine (unweighted).</p>
<p>Given that applying weights decreases the number of pairs with
a very high spectral similarity (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>A) (e.g., spectral similarity &gt;0.95 (1,295
original vs 89 with weights)), it is not surprising that the optimal
cutoff when applying weights is approximately 0.2 lower compared to
the original modified cosine similarities. While this only occurs
in the case of modified cosine, it reveals the importance of lowering
the typically applied cut-offs, when using weights. To investigate
why the number of pairs with a high spectral similarity is so low
when applying weights, we explored the characteristics of this subset
of spectra and found that pairs with high similarity using modified
cosine similarity typically have (i) a low number of peaks compared
to the rest of the spectra (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>A), a main peak representing more than 0.7 of the relative
total intensity (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">3</a></a>B).</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="fig3"><h4 class="obj_head">Figure 3.</h4>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><a class="tileshop" target="_blank" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/core/lw/2.0/html/tileshop_pmc/tileshop_pmc_inline.html?title=Click%20on%20image%20to%20zoom&amp;p=PMC3&amp;id=10854760_js3c00353_0003.jpg"><img class="graphic zoom-in" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/c816/10854760/7ebf2acfdbd1/js3c00353_0003.jpg" loading="lazy" id="gr3" height="924" width="776" alt="Figure 3"></a></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/fig3/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>(A) Distribution of the number of peaks per spectrum within the
pairs with a high Tanimoto coefficient (0.8–1) grouped by pairs
with a cosine similarity higher than 0.95 (green) and the rest (orange).
The plot shows that pairs of spectra corresponding to structurally
similar compounds (Tanimoto coefficient 0.8–1) that exhibit
a high spectral similarity (&gt;0.95) generally have a lower number
of
peaks compared with the rest of structurally similar compounds (Tanimoto
coefficient 0.8–1). (B) Distribution of the relative intensity
of the main peak. The violin plots show the distribution of the relative
intensities for three groups: (left) pairs of spectra with &gt;0.95
spectral
similarity using modified cosine similarity within the bin of Tanimoto
coefficient 0.8–1, (center) rest of pairs of spectra with less
than 0.95 spectral similarity using modified cosine similarity within
the bin of Tanimoto coefficient 0.8–1, and (right) distribution
of all the spectra in the GNPS data set.</p></figcaption></figure><p>These findings are to be expected when taking into account how
the weights operate since in these pairs of spectra we find a single
dominant peak with high intensity, which is aligned by modified cosine.
When applying the weights, the contribution of the relative intensity
of this main peak to the overall score diminishes, as the nonmatching
smaller peaks are now higher. Thus, the cosine similarity decreases
when weights are applied, since previously the main peaks in each
spectrum were perfectly aligned and the handful of low peaks did not
contribute to the cosine similarity score. When examining the majority
of these pairs, we found an over-representation of glycosidic flavonoids
and other compounds with glycosidic bonds (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 4</a>). Such compounds typically present the previously
mentioned characteristics, as the glycosidic bond is easily broken
but the rest of the flavonoid scaffold cannot be broken. In conclusion,
these insights can serve as a criterion to know when to apply weights
or not. For instance, weights are generally not required if modified
cosine similarity yields a match with a score close to 1, or when
aligning spectra with only a few peaks or a single dominant peak.</p>
<p>Additionally, we investigated the impact of different weight functions
on the intensities. First, we explored the effect of setting all the
intensities to 1 (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 5</a>). Not surprisingly, for pairs with high Tanimoto scores, binarizing
the intensities to 0 or 1 depending on the appearance of a peak did
not yield better results than applying most other reported weights
but still achieved a higher mean compared with the unweighted spectral
similarities (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 6</a>).
Next, we explored using different weights, including the ones proposed
by Sokolow et al.<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> (intensity weight =
0.5, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> weight = 0.5 in <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">eq <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>), Stein and Scott<sup><a href="#ref9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref9">9</a></sup> (intensity weight = 0.6, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> weight = 3 in <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">eq <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>), Horai et al.<sup><a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a></sup> (intensity weight
= 0.5, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> weight = 2 in <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">eq <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>), Kim et al.<sup><a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a></sup> (intensity weight = 0.53, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> weight = 1.3 in <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">eq <a href="#eq2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>) and other commonly used weights (intensity weight
= 0.5, <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> weight = 0). Similar
to our proposed weights, all these previously mentioned weights yield
similarity scores that are closer to their respective Tanimoto coefficient
bins, compared to the original similarities. We then compared these
previously reported weights to ours using Kullback–Leibler
(KL) divergence, a statistical test that allowed us to compare how
close the distribution of the spectral similarities was to the distribution
of the Tanimoto coefficients (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Table 3</a>, <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 6</a>). Our
results demonstrate that our weights best resemble the Tanimoto coefficients.</p>
<p>Lastly, we explored the contribution of the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> frequency weights with respect to intensity rescaling.
As expected, rescaling based on <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> peak occurrence exhibited a minimal impact compared with rescaling
based on peak intensity, since the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> frequency variability is much lower for most spectra (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 1</a>). Consequently, we tested
the impact of the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> frequency
rescaling and found that <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> frequency
weights do improve results and improve KL divergence (KL divergence
= 0.16 only intensity weights, and KL divergence = 0.112 intensity
+ <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> weights) (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Table 3</a>). In conclusion, our study has demonstrated
the effectiveness of both proposed weighting strategies, namely, intensity
and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> frequency, in enhancing
the performance of spectra alignment tasks. Among the various weight
modifications, our approach most accurately approximated structural
similarity.</p></section><section id="sec3.2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Mass Spectral Library Search</h3>
<p>A reference
library in mass spectrometry is a collection of mass spectra from
known compounds. It serves as a valuable resource for compound identification
as it allows researchers to match experimental spectra against the
reference spectra for confident identification of compounds. The similarity
metric used for querying in a reference library is key because experimental
data may exhibit small shifts in <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values and intensities due to various factors such as instrument
calibration, ionization efficiency, or sample preparation.<sup><a href="#ref22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref22">22</a></sup> As a result of distinct approaches taken by
different similarity metrics to align spectra and correct for these
variations, there can be significant differences in the best matches
returned by each similarity metric.<sup><a href="#ref4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref4">4</a></sup></p>
<p>In this subsection, we simulated a real-world library search application
by leveraging GNPS and NIST spectra generated from the same chemical
structures to benchmark different weighting methods using a modified
cosine similarity (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">2</a></a>). First, we explored the precision of the top matches in
this retrieval task (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">4</a></a>A). This metric quantifies the number of true positives within
the top K hits, making it a reliable way to evaluate the quality of
returned matches. Here, we found that the weights from Sokolow et
al.<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> outperformed the other weights in
this task closely followed by our weights and the weights from Kim
et al.<sup><a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a></sup> Surprisingly, Stein and Scott<sup><a href="#ref9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref9">9</a></sup> and Horai et al.<sup><a href="#ref11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref11">11</a></sup> performed worse than the unweighted modified cosine similarity (baseline).
This contrasts with the results from the spectral alignment task,
where both weighted methods outperformed the unweighted method.</p>
<figure class="fig xbox font-sm" id="fig4"><h4 class="obj_head">Figure 4.</h4>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/c816/10854760/a48e2281d6b2/js3c00353_0004.jpg" loading="lazy" id="gr4" height="2026" width="3583" alt="Figure 4"></p>
<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="figure/fig4/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div>
<figcaption><p>Overview of
the results on the mass spectral library search task.
(A) Average precision@K values on the top 1, 5, and 10 matches using
different proposed weights in the literature and our proposed weights.
Library search is run using modified cosine similarity with a parts
per million window of 10. Their performances are compared against
unweighted scores (baseline). (B) Average Tanimoto coefficients of
the structures within the top 1, 5, and 10 matched spectra. (C) Number
of queries that did not return any match with a spectral similarity
higher than 0.5 out of the total 25,437 queries. The bottom set of
bars represents the number of queries without any exact matches. The
upper set represents the number of queries without any matches. (D)
Average number of matched spectra above different thresholds for spectral
similarity (i.e., 0.5, 0.7, 0.9).</p></figcaption></figure><p>Zooming into the top matches, we observed that our proposed weights,
the Sokolow et al.<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> weights, and the intensity
weights with <em>n</em> = 0.5 yielded the most similar molecules
to the one queried, closely followed by the Kim et al.<sup><a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a></sup> weights (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">4</a></a>B). Additionally, we also evaluated our results using
ROC-AUC, although, as mentioned in the <a href="#sec2" class="usa-link">Methods</a>, we believe the ranked-based metric is more informative. When evaluating
using ROC-AUC, our weights slightly outperformed the weights from
Sokolow et al.<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 7</a>). Subsequently, we analyzed the number of queries
that failed to yield any match with a spectral similarity higher than
0.5 for each method (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">4</a></a>C). Overall, all methods returned matches for approximately
90% of the 25,437 queries. However, we observed differences between
methods. For example, the weights from Sokolow et al.,<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> Kim et al.<sup><a href="#ref12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref12">12</a></sup> and our weights
had fewer queries without an exact match than the unweighted method,
while the other methods had more queries without an exact match.</p>
<p>Lastly, we investigated the average number of matches using different
thresholds (i.e., 0.5, 0.7, 0.9). Similar to the spectral alignment
results, we found that the original cosine similarity (i.e., unweighted
baseline) returned the largest number of matches above 0.9 similarity
(<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">4</a></a>D). However,
the number of matches compared to the weighted methods decreases as
the threshold gets lower. Furthermore, we evaluated how the results
changed when using different ppm windows. We performed similar queries
with ppm windows of 100 ppm and 5,000 ppm to access the scalability
of the weighting methods for more varied molecule sets (<a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jasms.3c00353/suppl_file/js3c00353_si_001.pdf" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Supplementary Figure 8</a>). Unsurprisingly, the
overall precision for all weighting methods decreased as we expanded
the searches to more molecules with larger ppm windows. However, the
precision scores for queries employing our weights experienced a comparatively
slower decrease compared to those for the other weighting methods.
These results are consistent with the results from the spectral alignment
task where there was a bigger variety of molecules since no parts
per million window was applied. In that task the weighted methods
were the top-performing approaches.</p></section></section><section id="sec4"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Conclusion</h2>
<p>Here, we demonstrated that weighting the intensities can assist
in the identification of analogs and can improve the annotation of
unknown compounds, regardless of the similarity metric employed. Furthermore,
we introduced a weighting method for spectral intensity values that
accounts for the relative frequency of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> values, which can be used in conjunction with other weights.
In the task of identifying structurally related compounds, we demonstrated
improvement for the most widely used spectral similarity metrics such
as modified cosine similarity by applying our proposed weights, improving
upon previous weighting methods. Similarly, on the library search
task, we demonstrated that applying weights leads to improved performance
and specifically, our weights are on par with the other best-performing
weights proposed by Sokolow et al.<sup><a href="#ref8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref8">8</a></sup> Finally,
we have explored examples where the application of weights has resulted
in diminished performance, identifying spectral characteristics where
the implementation of weights may not yield advantages.</p>
<p>This
work is not without its limitations. First, while applying
weights for spectral alignment demonstrates a predominantly positive
effect on the results across all similarity metrics, it tends to perform
worse when using modified cosine similarity yields a high spectral
similarity (&gt;0.95). However, we identified spectral characteristics
in these cases (i.e., spectra with few peaks, spectra with a single
major peak, and presence of a glycosidic bond) where applying the
weights typically decreases spectral similarity for pairs of structurally
similar compounds. Thus, to determine whether weights should be applied,
we recommend investigating these factors in the query spectra first.
Second, the spectral alignment task is based on the original 10 million
pairs of spectra used by Bittremieux et al.,<sup><a href="#ref5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref5">5</a></sup> which are derived from all the combinations of spectra present in
GNPS. Ideally, this task should be conducted on all combinations,
but computational time constraints make it infeasible. We verified
that the subset is a representative sample of the full GNPS data set.
Third, in the case of the library search task, we presented a real
application where nonoverlapping spectra from GNPS were used to query
the NIST database. However, after the filtering steps, we were only
able to benchmark over 25,000 queries belonging to 2,776 unique molecules.
We hypothesize that this limited data set size coupled with the observation
that many queries either yielded no matches or quickly reached a saturation
point in the number of hits are the primary reasons we exclusively
observed limited improvement using weights for the spectral alignment
task. Finally, we note that applying weights inherently makes the
spectral similarity calculations more abstract and hampers their interpretability.</p>
<p>We aim to pursue several directions for our work in the future.
First, there might exist a variety of optimal weights based on the
type of molecule that we are querying/aligning. Thus, future endeavors
could investigate the optimal weights for different compound classes.
However, unveiling this array of optimal weights is perhaps a task
more suited for deep learning approaches, such as MS2DeepScore.<sup><a href="#ref3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="ref3">3</a></sup> Second, we have expanded our benchmarks beyond
NIST by incorporating GNPS spectral libraries; nevertheless, as new
resources emerge and both of these databases undergo updates, our
proposed weights and the previous ones must be reassessed. Third,
we believe that harnessing collision energy information could potentially
enhance the outcomes of the spectral alignment task. Since weights
could potentially be adjusted based on the employed energy, this information
could potentially offer improvements. Unfortunately, we were unable
to utilize this information since it is generally not available within
the GNPS data set.</p></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
