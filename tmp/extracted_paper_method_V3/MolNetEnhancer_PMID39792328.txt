>Materials and methods   Sponge species   Individuals of  Aplysina caissara  Pinheiro and Hajdu, 2001 and  Aplysina fulva  Pallas, 1766 (Verongiida, Aplysinidae) were collected by scuba in July and October 2018 at Ponta Recife (23°49’30.36”S 45°24’47.879”W) and at the southern rock shores of Praia do Guaecá (23°49’22.8”S 45°28’19.2”W), São Sebastião, north littoral of São Paulo, southwestern Atlantic Ocean (Fig.  1 ). In total, 10 sponge samples were collected at depths ranging between 3 and 8 m, placed in sterile plastic bags containing natural seawater, maintained in a cooler and transported to the laboratory for processing. The specimens were rinsed thoroughly with sterile artificial seawater (ASW [ 37 ]) and fragments of each sample were preserved in 70% ethanol for taxonomic identification. The sponge samples were identified by Dr. Gisele Lôbo-Hajdu and Dr. Márcio Custódio using morphological features and photos.    Fig. 1.     Open in a new tab   Location of sampling sites.  A . Map of Brazil highlighting the State of São Paulo and  B.  Detailed map of the São Sebastiao Channel area with sampling locations (Ponta do Recife and Praia do Guaeca) marked by red squares Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)   Freshly collected  A. caissara  and  A. fulva  sections of 3–5 mm 3  in size were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in sodium cacodylate buffer 0.2 M for two weeks at 4°C. After, the glutaraldehyde was removed and the samples washed with ASW three times for 50 min each under gentle agitation. Samples were maintained one week in ASW at 4°C, and then postfixed in 2% osmium tetroxide, for 1 h at 4ºC. The pieces were dehydrated in an ethanol series (50%, 70%, 95%, 3 × 99.5%), for 10 min each, and embedded in Epon 812 resin (EMS). Ultra-thin sections were prepared with a microtome EM UC7 (Leica) and contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. TEM images were acquired in a Jeol 1200 EX (Jeol) at National Center for Structural Biology and Bioimaging from the Federal University of Rio de Janeiro (CENABIO-UFRJ). Isolation and identification of bacteria associated with A. fulva and A. caissara   For bacterial cultures, 2.5 g of the choanosome was cut and macerated in 25 mL of calcium/magnesium free artificial seawater [ 38 ] using a sterile mortar and pestle. The supernatant was transferred to a falcon tube, and the remaining sponge residue was discarded. A dilution series was prepared in ASW and 100 µL of dilutions 10 −2  to 10 −6  were plated onto ( i ) one enriched medium (Brain Heart Infusion, BHI: 37 g.L −1  and Agar 15 g.L −1 ), ( ii ) two oligotrophic media (Marine Broth, MB: 37.4 g.L −1  and Agar 15 g.L −1 ) and Reasoner’s 2 A agar (R2A: 18.2 g.L −1 ) and ( iii ) two selective media, one for  Actinobacteriota  ( Actinomycete  Isolation Agar, AIA: 22 g.L −1  and glycerol 5 g.L −1 ) and the other for  Cyanobacteria  (SNAX) prepared as described by [ 39 ]. For the initial cultivation, BHI, R2A and MB were diluted 1:10 to allow the fastidious bacteria to grow and once a colony was pure the media was prepared as suggested by the manufacturer’s instructions. All media were acquired from Difco BD, except agar from Sigma-Aldrich and SNAX, and prepared with ASW, except MB that was prepared with deionized water. Plates were incubated at 21 ° C for seven days and daily checked. Visible colony forming units (CFUs) were collected and purified by three successive streaking on the same medium it was initially obtained.   Pure isolated colonies were transferred to the same broth medium that they were obtained (BHI: 37 g.L −1 , R2A: 18.2 g.L −1 , MB 37.4 g.L −1 , the selective media were prepared as explained above without agar) and allowed to grow for 18 h at room temperature with shaking (20–25 ° C, 200 rpm). Two aliquots of each liquid culture were taken and stored in 20% glycerol at −80°C until further use. One milliliter of the remaining culture was centrifuged at 14.000 x  g  for 2 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the bacterial pellet used for DNA extraction with Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and quantity of the DNA were checked. For the amplification of the 16S rRNA gene the primer pair 27 F-1492R [ 40 ] was used. The reaction mixture and thermal cycle were performed as described by Nascimento-Silva et al. [ 11 ]. All PCR amplifications were carried out in a C1000 Touch thermal cycler (Bio-Rad). Amplicons with correct gene size (~ 1500 bp) were subjected to sequencing with the chain termination method in an ABI 3500 automatic sequencer (Applied Biosystems) using the forward primer.   The generated sequences were manually inspected and trimmed with the Sequence Scanner 2 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The 16S rRNA gene sequences were aligned using the SINA web aligner [ 41 ] and classified using the databases present in SILVA [ 42 ,  43 ] with a confidence of 97%. All 16S rRNA gene sequences were deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI [ 44 ]), under accession numbers from  MZ503279 to MZ503487  (Table  S2 ).   To evaluate if there were bacterial strains exclusively obtained from each  Aplysina  species, Venn diagram analysis was performed with the online software jvenn ( https://jvenn.toulouse.inrae.fr  [ 45 ]), . Phylogenetic inferences   The SINA-SILVA software [ 41 ] was used to retrieve the closest sequences to the bacteria associated with  Aplysina  species obtained in the present study. The resulting sequences were verified in terms of identity, host organism and sampling locations using the NCBI and comparing the information with those compiled by Simister et al. [ 46 ]. For further analysis, the following parameters were established for the removal of sequences that were recovered from marine sponges: ( i ) those with no information regarding host species or sampling sites and ( ii ) redundant, except when obtained from the same sponge species from distinct sampling sites.   Preliminary phylogenetic inferences were performed in MEGA X [ 47 ,  48 ] with the aim of removing redundant sequences. The resulting sequences from each phylum or class in the case of  Proteobacteria  were aligned using the MUSCLE software [ 49 ]. Phylogenetic inferences were performed with maximum likelihood method with a bootstrap of 100 random replicates. The best model for each tree was inferred within the MEGA software. For those preliminary trees the selected model was the Tamura-Nei model with a discrete gamma distribution of rate variation between locations (Γ4) and a proportion of invariant locations (I). The outgroups for all inferences were selected from Simister et al. [ 46 ] or sequences from our own database. Then, final phylogenetic inferences were generated using maximum likelihood method with a bootstrap of 1000 random replicates and the method selected by MEGA software was Jukes-Cantor [ 50 ] with uniform rates for all phyla and/or classes. Bootstrap values equal to or higher than 75% were considered to guarantee reliability of nodes [ 51 ,  52 ].   The phylogenetic inferences were also used to investigate whether or not the bacteria isolated from both  Aplysina  species belonged to sponge enriched clusters. These are defined as a clade containing at least three 16S rRNA gene sequences that are more similar to each other than to sequences from other sources and are derived from at least two different sponge species or the same species from distinct locations [ 53 ]. Metabarcoding of the 16S rRNA gene sequencing and analyses   To verify the representativeness and novelty of the bacterial isolates obtained from  A. caissara  and  A. fulva , a comparison was performed with the metabarcoding of both sponge species. For the novelty, considering as putative new species those in which the sequence similarity was < 98.65% and as new genera < 95%, in this case, all the species within a new genus would be novel as well [ 54 ], all the amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) classified above these thresholds were removed from the dataset. To verify the ASVs that were exclusively obtained from each  Aplysina  species, Venn diagram analysis was carried out as explained above. The AMS test was performed as described by Marinho et al. [ 56 ] with some modifications. The bacterial strains were incubated in BHI and R2A broth for 48 h at 25°C. Afterwards, 10 µl of each bacteria culture was punctually inoculated on solid BHI and R2A media. Plates were incubated for 24 h at 25°C or until a visible colony was seen. Then, the colonies were exposed to chloroform vapor for 30 min to render them non-viable. Thus, any antimicrobial(s) produced by the bacteria would be already diffused on the agar plate and no more substance could be synthesized. The indicator bacterial strains  Staphylococcus aureus  (ATCC 29213, Gram-positive),  Pseudomonas aeruginosa  (ATCC 27853, Gram-positive),  Escherichia coli  (ATCC 25922, Gram-negative) and  Enterococcus faecium  (ATCC 19434, Gram-negative) were grown at 37°C in BHI prepared with deionized water. Then, the chloroform-treated colonies obtained from both  Aplysina  species were overlaid with BHI soft agar (37 g.L −1  and 7.5 g.L −1  agar prepared with deionized water) containing 30 µL of the overnight culture of the indicator bacterial strains. The plates were incubated for 24 h at 37°C and, after this period, the occurrence or not of inhibitory halos around the bacterial colonies from  Aplysina  species were verified. The bacteria were considered an AMS-positive only when the size of the halo was equal to or greater than 10 mm. These assays were performed in duplicate in both media. Extraction and sample preparation   Bacterial strains that tested positive in the molecular screening were cultivated at 25ºC in solid BHI and MB media. The stationary phase of the bacteria was verified approximately 72 h after plating. At this point, the cultures were macerated, eluted in 20 ml of ethyl acetate (Synth) and submitted to an ultrasonic bath (Nova Instruments), for 30 min. The liquid fraction was filtered and the residual agar was again dissolved and macerated in 10 ml of methanol (HPLC grade ≥ 99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), filtered and combined to the previously obtained liquid fraction. Each sample was transferred to a rotary evaporator (Heidolph Instruments), placed in a water bath at 50ºC for solvent removal then transferred to a vacuum concentrator (Genevac miVac, Fisher Scientific) until completely dry, yielding the crude extracts.   For LC-MS analysis, the crude extracts were subjected to a clean-up procedure using solid-phase extraction with C18 reverse phase cartridges (Chromabond, 45 μm, 500.0 mg, 6.0 mL). First, the C18 cartridges were pre-activated with 3.0 mL of methanol (MeOH) and equilibrated with 3 mL of MeOH/H 2 O (8:2, v/v). The dried extract (5.0 mg) was dissolved in 1.5 mL of MeOH/H 2 O (8:2, v/v), loaded onto a C18 cartridge, and sequentially eluted with 1.5 mL of the same solvent mixture. The dried eluates were reconstituted in MeOH to a concentration of 2.0 mg ml −1 , centrifuged at 12,000 x  g  for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected into vials for subsequent analysis.   Cytotoxic assay   The extracts were assessed for their cytotoxicity against the human colorectal cancer cells (HCT-116; ATCC CCR-247) through the MTT assay [ 57 ], as described by Tangerina et al. [ 58 ]. Briefly, cells in culture were harvested, diluted and seeded in 96-wells plates at 5 × 10 4  cells/mL. Cells were left to adhere for 24 h and the crude extracts, dissolved in DMSO, were then added to the cells at concentrations of 5 and 50 µg/mL. DMSO and doxorubicin made up negative and positive controls, respectively. After 72 h exposure, the wasted culture medium was removed and replaced in each well with fresh media added with 0.5 mg/mL of MTT to assess remaining metabolic activity of treated cells. Higher metabolic activity is revealed by greater reduction of MTT to a purple formazan precipitate, which was dissolved in 150 µL of DMSO and the absorbance measured using a plate spectrophotometer (Spectra Count Packard). Cytotoxicity of extracts was expressed as the percentage of inhibition of cell metabolic activity relative to that of the negative control. Mass spectrometry analysis   The capacity of the bacterial strains to produce bioactivity compounds was investigated. To this end, the ethyl acetate dried extracts were analyzed by liquid chromatography coupled with electrospray ionization high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry (LC-ESI-HRMS/MS). We used an ultra-fast liquid chromatography system (Prominence UFLC, Shimadzu) equipped with two solvent pumps (LC-20AD), degassing system (DGU-20A3), autosampler (SIL-20AHT), column oven (CTO-20 A), system controller (CBM-20 A) and a diode array detector (SPD-M20AV), connected to a micrOTOF-QII high resolution mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics). LC separations were performed on a 5 μm C18 (150 × 4.6 mm) Kinetex column (Phenomenex). A linear gradient with a mobile phase composed of water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B), both acidified with 0.1% formic acid from 5 to 100% (B) in 45 min was used for the elution of the samples. The injection volume was 10 ml.min −1  and flow rate applied was 1 ml.min −1 . Analytical blanks containing only the solvent MeOH were analyzed every 10 samples.   The acquisition range for full-scan MS spectra was  m/z  50–1500, in a data-dependent positive ion mode and parameters employed were as follows: nebulizer (N 2 ) gas pressure of 4.5 bar, dry gas flow rate of 9 L.min −1 , ion source temperature of 200 °C, capillary voltage of 4500 V, and voltage source of 5 kV. The fragmentation experiments were performed by collision-induced dissociation (CID) with the MS/MS fragmentation of five most intense selected ions and collision energy ramp of 30–60 eV. MS/MS active exclusion was set after four spectra and released after 30 s. The mass spectra were processed in the DataAnalysis software (version 4.3, Bruker Daltonics). Data processing and molecular networking (GNPS)   LC-MS/MS raw data files were converted to mzML format in the MSConvert (ProteoWizard) software using the filter Peak Picking with the vendor checked algorithm. The output data were uploaded directly to the Global Natural Products Social (GNPS,  https://gnps.ucsd.edu )  platform and a molecular network was created using the online workflow ( https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/  [ 59 ]). The data was filtered by removing all MS/MS fragment ions within +/- 17 Da of the precursor  m/z . MS/MS spectra were window filtered by choosing only the top six fragment ions in the +/- 50 Da window throughout the spectrum. The precursor ion mass tolerance was set to 0.02 Da and, MS/MS fragment ion tolerance of 0.05 Da, cosine score above 0.7 and more than four matched peaks. Finally, the maximum size of a molecular family was set to 100, and the lowest scoring edges were removed from molecular families until the molecular family size was below this threshold.   The spectra in the network were then searched against GNPS’ spectral libraries. The library spectra were filtered in the same manner as the input data. All matches kept between network spectra and library spectra were required to have a score above 0.7 and at least four matched peaks. For those detected signals that did not have a match in the GNPS library, a manual spectral annotation was performed. The chemical formula was inferred from the exact mass, and the compounds were annotated based on comparisons with databases such as the Dictionary of Natural Products [ 60 ] and The Natural Products Atlas [ 61 ,  62 ], as well as reported literature data. In addition, proposals were made by careful inspection based on the fragmentation patterns of each molecular family. The Insilico Peptidic Natural Products DEREPLICATOR bioinformatic tool was employed to annotate known peptidic natural products [ 63 ], using parameters such as precursor ion mass tolerance and MS/MS fragment ion tolerance of 0.02 and 0.05 Da, respectively, the VarQuest algorithm [ 64 ] and the PNP database selected, a maximum allowed charge of 2, a minimum of five amino acids (AA), a maximum allowed modification mass of 150 Da, and a minimum of five matched peaks with known compounds. Additionally, the molecular networks were subjected to MS2LDA ( http://ms2lda.org/  [ 65 ]), and Network Annotation Propagation (NAP [ 66 ] workflows. The GNPS MolNetEnhancer [ 67 ] workflow was employed to integrate data from the GNPS Library Search, MS2LDA, NAP, and Dereplicator into the molecular network, thus enhancing the chemical structural information within the network. ClassyFire [ 68 ] chemical ontology was used for the chemical class annotations. The molecular networks were represented visually using Cytoscape software v. 3.8.2 [ 69 ]. Detected features in both the solvent blank and media blank were removed from the resulting feature table. The results of these processing jobs can be accessed in Table S1. Results   Bacterial isolation and diversity   Due to the high densities of microbial cells in the mesohyls of  A. caissara  and  A. fulva  observed in transmission electron microscopy, both species were classified as HMA sponges (Fig.  2 a-b). A total of 209 bacterial isolates were obtained from the two sponge species investigated (Table  S2 ). From these, 60.3% were recovered from  A. fulva  and 39.7% from  A. caissara . Overall,  Firmicutes  was the most abundant phyla associated with both species with 58% and  Proteobacteria  with 27.7% in  A. caissara  and 32.5% in  A. fulva  (Fig.  3 a). The main differences between the sponges were related to  Bacteroidota , associated with 13.3% of the total in  A. caissara  and 3.2% in  A. fulva ; and  Actinobacteriota  with 1.2% in  A. caissara  and 6.3% in  A. fulva  (Fig.  3 a).
