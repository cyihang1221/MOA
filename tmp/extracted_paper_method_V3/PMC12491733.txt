</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ab0005"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>This study aims to authenticate the geographical origin and cultivar (Japonica vs Indica) labeling of rice sold across Chinese markets using stable isotope analysis coupled with multivariate analyses and machine learning models. <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N, <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O, and <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H were investigated in rice using isotope ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS) and analyzed through PCA and t-SNE to visualize regional separation and cultivar differences. Different machine learning models (kNN, SVM, RF) were developed using authentic field samples and tested with market-sourced samples for origin verification. Results revealed significant isotopic discrepancies between field and market labelled samples, suggesting potential mislabeling of cultivar and origin. t-SNE demonstrated superior performance in resolving nonlinear patterns in isotope data compared to PCA. Among the different models, RF showed slightly better classification performance. Although the models achieved perfect classification accuracy on field-sourced samples, a considerable decline in performance was observed when applied to market-sourced samples, suggesting widespread origin mislabeling issues.</p>

<p id="p0035">Rice (<em>Oryza sativa</em> L.) is a widely grown crop and serves as a staple food for more than half of the world's population. It provides basic nutrition and also supports a wide range of health promoting benefits including anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, anti-allergic, and anti-carcinogenic properties (<a href="#bb0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0040">Cicero &amp; Gaddi, 2001</a>; <a href="#bb0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0170">Wadood et al., 2024</a>). Among the Asian population, its consumption surpasses 100 kg/capita annually (<a href="#bb0185" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0185">Wadood, Nie, Li, Rogers, Khan, et al., 2022</a>). China is the world's largest rice producer, and its large population relies predominantly on rice to fulfill their basic nutrition. Although used as a substantial source of energy and nutrition, rice is also prone to food safety risks due to potential contamination with toxic elements during cultivation (<a href="#bb0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0050">Huang et al., 2020</a>). Therefore the ability to verify rice provenance information is crucial since the presence of these toxic elements is largely associated with localized soils and specific cultivation practices (<a href="#bb0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0065">Li et al., 2022</a>).</p>
<p id="p0040">Worldwide, food fraud including rice mislabeling is estimated to cost $40 billion/annually to the food industry, highlighting the magnitude of economic impact and the strong incentive for fraudsters to exploit the rice market (<a href="#bb0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0105">McGrath, 2021</a>). China, as a major producer, dominates global rice consumption, underscoring the heightened importance of food safety and authenticity assurance. In China, Japonica and Indica rice are key cultivars which are widely produced. Japonica rice is mainly cultivated in the northeast region of China, while Indica rice is grown along the Yangtze River basin and southern part of China. Rice produced in northeast region has a greater market share and typically commands a higher premium, up to two or three times the price of rice from other parts of China, due to its unique flavor, taste and superior quality (<a href="#bb0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0085">Liu et al., 2019</a>). However, this also makes it vulnerable to mislabeling for financial gain with broader implications for both consumers and regulators. It has been reported that 90 % of the rice sold under premium regional brand “Wuchang rice” is counterfeit, suggesting there are millions of tons of economically fraudulent product in the Chinese market. This counterfeit practice damages consumer confidence and deters farmers from growing high-value rice as it lowers their overall profits, and complicates regulatory control mechanisms (<em>Counterfeit Rice Spells Trouble</em>, 2015).</p>
<p id="p0045">For Chinese and international regulators, rice mislabeling demands stronger traceability protocols, stricter labeling enforcement, and advanced authenticity testing. In this context, many countries have implemented food regulations; for instance, Japan introduced a Japanese Agricultural Standard (JAS) which must list the cultivar name, geographical origin, and harvest year on rice packaging. European Union (EU) established quality labels such as Protected Designation of Origin (PDO) and Protected Geographical Indication (PGI) to protect the names of premium quality agro-products and foodstuffs associated with specific geographical origins (<a href="#bb0165" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0165">Wadood et al., 2020</a>). In China, the Food Safety Law of People's Republic of China (2015 Revision) strictly deals with food safety issues associated with food fraud and adulteration problems (<a href="#bb0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0085">Liu et al., 2019</a>). However, geographical origin identification and cultivar verification is nearly impossible using visual inspection alone, leading to authenticity issues in premium agricultural products, such as mislabeling, adulteration, and partial or total substitution with inferior varieties. This situation demands the establishment of more sophisticated analytical techniques to address these authenticity issues.</p>
<p id="p0050">In the past, many analytical techniques have been employed to address the authenticity of rice, including spectroscopic techniques (<a href="#bb0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0120">Pérez-Rodríguez et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#bb0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0155">Teye et al., 2019</a>), spectrometry (<a href="#bb0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0060">Kongsri et al., 2021</a>), liquid and gas chromatography (<a href="#bb0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0025">Ch et al., 2021</a>; <a href="#bb0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0090">Long et al., 2017</a>), sensor and molecular techniques (<a href="#bb0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0005">Arslan et al., 2022</a>; <a href="#bb0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0145">Surapaneni et al., 2016</a>). Although some of these techniques are highly sensitive and offer unique fingerprints for determining geographical origin of agro-products, they often require extensive sample preparation, complex purification steps, and incur high equipment maintenance costs, factors that are deemed as bottlenecks in analytical workflow.</p>
<p id="p0055">In contrast, isotope ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS) has recently gained increased attention for its effectiveness in addressing food authentication challenges. Stable isotope values of specific samples can provide useful information regarding their origin since these isotopes depends on various abiotic factors which include: climatic (sunlight, temperature, precipitation, wind, humidity), soil (pH, texture, nutrient, salinity), water-use related, and geographical (altitude and latitude). In addition, biotic factors such as plant physiology, photosynthetic pathways (C3, C4, and CAM) also significantly influence isotopic composition (<a href="#bb0190" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0190">Wadood, Nie, Li, Rogers, Zhang, et al., 2022</a>).</p>
<p id="p0060">Previously, studies have reported the potential of stable isotopes (<em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N, <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O and <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H) to authenticate rice geographical origin (<a href="#bb0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0065">Li et al., 2022</a>; <a href="#bb0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0085">Liu et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#bb0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0170">Wadood et al., 2024</a>). These isotopic techniques offer intrinsic geochemical markers that reflect environmental conditions distinctive to rice growing regions, making them essential tools for provenance verification. However, the majority of these studies primarily relied on authentic field samples for geographical origin modeling, highlighting the importance of well-characterized reference datasets to ensure model accuracy and robustness. To the best of our knowledge, no large-scale research has been conducted to verify origin labelling of rice available in Chinese domestic markets. Addressing this gap is important for improving rice traceability and mitigating the risk of food fraud and mislabeling within marketplace. Therefore, the present study was designed to use authentic field samples collected from major rice producing areas, combined with commercial rice samples purchased from major supermarkets from different provinces in China. This approach aims to assess and verify the labelling claims of rice related to a specific geographical origin, thereby ensuring reliable authentication and protecting rice authenticity in domestic markets.</p></section><section id="s0010"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">2. Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="s0015"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.1. Sample procurement</h3>
<p id="p0065">Two authentic rice cultivars (Japonica and Indica rice) were collected from major rice producing regions in China including the northeastern China (Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning), the Yangtze River Plain (Hunan, Jiangxi, Henan, Jiangsu, and Hubei) and southern China (Guangdong and Yunnan), along with commercial samples procured from major supermarkets across different provinces. Northeastern samples are predominantly comprised of Japonica rice, while Yangtze River Plain and southern regions were mainly Indica rice, except for Jiangsu and Henan, which mostly produce Japonica rice. A total of 282 samples were procured in 2017, among them 141 were authentic field samples and 141 commercial market samples. Field-sourced samples comprised of unpolished (brown) rice, whereas market-sourced samples were predominantly polished rice. Detailed information of market-sourced samples is summarized in Table S1.</p>
<p id="p0070">The harvested rice samples were dried and stored at each farm following the guidelines provided in GB/T 5491-1985: <em>Inspection of Grain and Oilseeds: Methods for Sampling and Sample Reduction</em> (<a href="#bb0115" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0115">National Standard of the People Republic of China, 1986</a>). About 2 kg of freshly picked rice grains were collected from each field site, transferred to the laboratory, and hulled using a hulling machine. The samples were then finely ground, and sieved through a 100-mesh sieve (&lt;150 μm), and the resulting powder was then air-dried at 70 °C for 48 h. All prepared samples were stored in desiccators until further analysis.</p></section><section id="s0020"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.2. Stable isotope (<em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N, <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O, and <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H) analysis</h3>
<p id="p0075">For the <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C and <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N measurements, dried powdered rice samples were weighed in duplicate (4.5–5.5 mg) and packed into tin capsules (3 mm × 5 mm). The samples were analyzed using an elemental analyzer (Vario Pyro Cube, Elementar, Hanau, Germany) combined with an isotope ratio mass spectrometer (IsoPrime100, Isoprime Ltd., Manchester, England). The combustion of samples was carried out in a combustion furnace at 1150 °C and reduction of the gases occurred at 850 °C over copper wire. An inert gas (He) with a flow rate of 230 mL/min was passed through a CentrION prior to mass spectrometry. Acetanilide (Puriss. p.a., Sigma-Aldrich) was used to calibrate elemental %C and %N. Reference standard materials including IAEA-CH-6 (sucrose, <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C = −10.4 ‰), B2155 (protein, <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C = −27.0 ‰, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N = +6.0 ‰), USGS64 (glycine, <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C = −40.8 ‰, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N = +1.8 ‰), USGS40 (L-glutamic acid, <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C = −26.4 ‰, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N = −4.5 ‰), and IAEA-N-2 (ammonium sulfate, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N = +20.3 ‰) were used for multipoint calibration. B2155 was supplied by Elemental Microanalysis (United Kingdom). The <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C and <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values were measured relative to V-PDB and AIR, respectively.</p>
<p id="p0080">For <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H and <em>δ</em>
<sup>18</sup>O analysis, samples and reference materials were freeze-dried at −60 °C for three days to remove all exchangeable water and subsequently equilibrated for five days in the laboratory exposed to local atmospheric conditions prior to H and O analysis. Around 1.0 mg powdered rice sample was weighed into silver capsules (6 mm × 4 mm) in triplicate and analyzed using EA-IRMS. Pyrolysis of rice samples was performed at 1450 °C to produce gaseous H<sub>2</sub> and CO, respectively. The analytes were finally transferred into the IRMS for isotope determination. The <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H and <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O values were measured relative to Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water (V-SMOW). Reference standard materials USGS55 (Mexican ziricote, <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H = −28.2 ‰, <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O = +19.1 ‰) and USGS54 (Canadian lodgepole pine, <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H = −150.4 ‰, <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O = +17.8 ‰) were used to calibrate the <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H and <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O measurements. The analytical precision for <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N, <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H and <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O was less than ±0.1 ‰, ± 0.2 ‰, ± 2 ‰, and 0.5 ‰, respectively. Reference materials were obtained from the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA, Austria) and the United States Geological Survey (USGS, United States).</p>
<p id="p0085">The delta values (<em>δ</em>) were calculated as follows:</p>
<table class="disp-formula p" id="fo0005"><tr><td class="formula"><math id="M1" display="block" altimg="si1.svg"><mi>δ</mi><mi mathvariant="normal">E</mi><mspace width="0.25em"></mspace><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mo>‰</mo></mfenced><mo linebreak="goodbreak">=</mo><mfenced open="(" close=")"><mrow><mspace width="0.25em"></mspace><mi>R</mi><mtext>sample</mtext><mo>/</mo><mi>R</mi><mtext>standard</mtext><mspace width="0.25em"></mspace></mrow></mfenced><mo linebreak="badbreak">−</mo><mn>1</mn></math></td></tr></table>
<p>where <em>δ</em>E represents <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup> N, <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H, and <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O whereas <em>R</em><sub>sample</sub> and <em>R</em><sub>standard</sub> represent the<sup>13</sup>C/<sup>12</sup>C, <sup>15</sup>N/<sup>14</sup>N, <sup>2</sup>H/<sup>1</sup>H or <sup>18</sup>O/<sup>16</sup>O ratios in samples and references, respectively (<a href="#bb0140" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0140">Skrzypek et al., 2022</a>).</p></section><section id="s0025"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">2.3. Data analysis</h3>
<p id="p0090">Different data analysis techniques including box and whisker plots, dimensionality reduction and machine learning models were employed on isotopic data to authenticate the market-sourced rice samples. Box and whisker plots combined with post hoc tests were applied to identify significant differences between field and market-sourced samples in each region using Origin 2022 (Origin Lab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Multivariate analysis employed Python 3.9 with scikit-learn (v1.0.2) for both machine learning and dimensionality reduction tests. Three machine learning models with the following optimized parameters: k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN; K = 7, Manhattan distance), support vector machine (SVM; Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, C = 100), and random forest (RF; 300 estimators, sqrt feature selection), were applied to the data with classification performance evaluated through accuracy, F1-scores, and confusion matrices.</p>
<p id="p0095">Dimensionality reduction was achieved using principal component analysis (PCA; n_components = 2) to extract linear relationships and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE; perplexity = 30, random_state = 42) for nonlinear pattern visualization, both preceded by feature standardization (StandardScaler). All model visualizations were generated with matplotlib and seaborn, incorporating 95 % confidence ellipses (Gaussian Mixture Models) and dual-coded points (color-coded by category, shaped by source) to enhance interpretability. The workflow ensured reproducibility through fixed random states (random_state = 42), version-controlled dependencies, and high-resolution (300 dpi) output figures, with complete code archived for accessibility. This integrated approach enabled comprehensive examination of both linear and nonlinear patterns in the isotopic data while maintaining rigorous computational standards. The complete analysis was executed in a Jupyter notebook environment on Windows 11 (64-bit), with package dependencies managed via Conda (v4.12.0).</p></section></section><section id="s0030"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">3. Results and discussion</h2>

Significant <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H differences in Indica rice between field and market source samples (up to ∼17.3 ‰ and ∼20.6 ‰) were observed for Hubei and Guangdong. Japonica rice samples from Heilongjiang, Jilin and Liaoning also showed significant <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H differences between field and market rice samples with the largest difference (∼ 32.0 ‰) observed for Liaoning region samples. Interannual isotopic variations cannot be ignored since climatic factors varies across different years which affect hydrogen isotope values; however, the large mean <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H difference (∼32.0 ‰) between field and market-source samples suggest widespread discrepancies beyond normal annual climatic variations. This difference is considerably higher than previously reported interannual differences of less than ∼10 ‰ in <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H values for wheat samples from the same region across three years (H. <a href="#bb0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0075">Liu et al., 2015</a>).</p>
<p id="p0130">The largest <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O variations between field and market-sourced rice were observed for Yunnan (6.8 ‰), followed by Hubei (3.6 ‰), Jiangxi (3.4 ‰), Hunan (3.3 ‰), and Guangdong (2.7 ‰), respectively. Japonica rice from Heilongjiang and Liaoning also exhibited significant <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O differences, with different mean <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O values for field (19.5 ‰ and 23.6 ‰) and market (21.3 ‰ and 21.8 ‰) samples, respectively.</p>

<p id="p0135">Principal component analysis (PCA) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) were employed to visualize the cultivar and geographical origin classification of rice samples using stable isotopes (<em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N, <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O, and <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H, <a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>). Both PCA and t-SNE are dimension reduction techniques and operate in different ways. More specifically, PCA is a linear dimension reduction technique used to summarize large datasets by identifying directions (principal components) ranked by the amount of variance they explain, making it suitable for feature reduction and interpretation (<a href="#bb0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0055">Jolliffe &amp; Cadima, 2016</a>). On the contrary, t-SNE is a nonlinear technique effective in visualizing high-dimensional data by preserving local relationships, and demonstrates the potential to reveal sample clusters and patterns that PCA may miss (<a href="#bb0160" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0160">Van Der Maaten &amp; Hinton, 2008</a>).</p>

Principal component analysis extracted two PCs which explained a combined variance of 66.8 % with the maximum variance being explained by PC1 (38.8 %), respectively (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>a). PCA showed that a significant proportion of the variability in the isotope data is captured in two dimensions, indicating effective dimensionality reduction and visualization (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>b). The variables loading plot shows that PC1 is driven primarily by variations in <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C and <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O, with loadings of 0.69 and 0.61, respectively. These isotopes captured the majority of the variation on PC1, suggesting they served as key discriminatory markers for separating the rice cultivars, likely due to differences in their photosynthetic pathways and water-use efficiency. In contrast, <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H contributed negatively (−0.35) to PC1, slightly counteracting the influence of other variables. This nuanced relationship highlights the complex interplay of isotope ratios in assigning specific cultivar signatures.</p>
<p id="p0145"><em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N significantly contributed to PC2 (28.0 %) with a strong positive loading of 0.86 %, suggesting that PC2 mainly explains the differences in nitrogen isotopic composition among the rice samples. This suggests <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N is a key marker for distinguishing rice cultivars, as it reflects nitrogen nutrient sources and cultivation conditions (<a href="#bb0200" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0200">Wu et al., 2025</a>). The PCA scores plot shows reasonable separation between Indica and Japonica rice cultivars. Within each cultivar group, field and market-sourced samples also appear to be grouped. Cluster cohesions (silhouette scores) for field and market-sourced samples were 0.336 and 0.399 (moderate), respectively supporting the PCA grouping but highlighting the partial overlap in sample characteristics. Authentic Indica and Japonica field samples showed a clear separation, while the market-sourced rice samples overlapped to a greater extent. Overall, the <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C (reflecting photosynthetic carbon assimilation and water use) and <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N (reflecting nutrient sources and nitrogen cycling) values contributed significantly in distinguishing rice cultivar groups. The t-SNE plot significantly improved the separation between Japonica and Indica rice samples, based on high dimensional data projected into a two-dimensional space. In the t-SNE plot, the two cultivars occupy distinctive regions in the plot and can be visualized into separate clusters, with Japonica samples aggregating towards the upper right and Indica samples grouping to the lower left (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>c). Although a small overlap exists between some samples which could be attributed to natural biological variation, environmental effects, or potential hybridization events, the majority of the data depicts a clear separation, suggesting that the selected features effectively distinguish between the two rice cultivars.</p>
<p id="p0150">The 95 % confidence ellipses further confirm this separation, demonstrating minimal overlap and distinctive grouping, which reflects isotopic differences between cultivars. Indica's rice ellipse appears slightly more horizontally extended, while the Japonica's rice ellipse is more vertical, indicating further variability patterns in the data. Importantly, there is an even distribution of sample markers (both field and market-sourced rice) within each cultivar cluster, suggesting that the observed separation is mainly due to genetic or phenotypic differences rather than sample origin. Distinct isotopic composition differences were previously noted among different cultivars and are associated with physiological traits including water use efficiency, stomatal activity, diffusive conductance, vegetative cycle of water, which vary consistently with the genetic and metabolic characteristics of the cultivars (<a href="#bb0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0170">Wadood et al., 2024</a>). Variations in the water use efficiency mainly influence <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C discrimination because stomatal activity affects the ratio of internal to ambient CO<sub>2</sub>, ultimately leading to <sup>13</sup>C discrimination during photosynthesis. Similarly, <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H in plant tissues reflects source water composition, modulated by stomatal conductance and the vegetative water cycle (<a href="#bb0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0135">Sanchez-Bragado et al., 2019</a>). These light stable isotope ratios thus serve as integrative markers of each cultivar's specific physiological and environmental processes. Integrating these isotopes with phenotypic and genetic markers offers a robust framework to capture how these underlying physiological characteristics manifest as distinct isotopic signatures, thereby enabling accurate cultivar discrimination.</p></section><section id="s0040"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.2. Isotopic analysis of field and market-sourced samples</h3>
<p id="p0155">The <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N, and <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O values of Indica rice samples showed a good discrimination ability between field and market-sourced samples across different regions (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>). However, Indica field rice samples showed consistently lower mean <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C values for different regions (ranging between −28.9 ‰ to −28.2 ‰) than market-sourced samples (−27.8 ‰ to −27.5 ‰). While these <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C values align with their C3 plant status, they indicate possible processing effects which have slightly altered the <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C values of market-sourced rice. Alternatively, these effects could be due to substitution of the market-sourced samples with another origin rice that has more positive <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C values, or blending of rice from different farms within the region that were not sampled in this study.</p>
<p id="p0160">It has been reported that blending rice crops grown under different water sources such as surface water (derived from rain) vs irrigation water (derived from groundwater) could cause shift in <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C signatures, as different water sources may occur with different weather (climatic events). Water use efficiency effects or reduced crop irrigation may also cause plant-water stress which affects photosynthetic discrimination and discriminates against <sup>12</sup>C (<a href="#bb0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0045">Ghashghaie &amp; Badeck, 2014</a>; <a href="#bb0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0130">Rogers et al., 2025</a>).</p>
<p id="p0165">Indica rice from field samples showed more positive regional <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values (up to 2.2 ‰) compared to market-sourced samples (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>). Regional rice <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values may vary according to different fertilization protocols, post-harvest rice treatment or origin mislabeling of market rice (<a href="#bb0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0110">Muñoz-Redondo et al., 2024</a>; <a href="#bb0180" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0180">Wadood et al., 2023</a>). Higher Indica field rice <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values may reflect the use of organic fertilizers, including composts or manures which denitrify (enriching the manure residue in <sup>15</sup>N) due to microbial fractionation. Denitrified manure transfers <sup>15</sup>N enriched nutrients to the paddy soil which can increase the rice <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values by up to 8 ‰ (<a href="#bb0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0035">Choi et al., 2017</a>).</p>
<p id="p0170">Another possible reason for more positive field rice <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values is postharvest treatments such as polishing. All market-sourced samples used in this study were polished rice whereas field samples were collected and analyzed as brown (unpolished) rice. The removal of the grain husk or outer layers during polishing could lead to the more negative <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values seen in market rice, since the husk portion is usually enriched in <sup>15</sup>N due to its distinct biochemical composition such as protein, cell wall components (lignin etc.), enzymes and secondary metabolite contents (<a href="#bb0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0030">Chi et al., 2024</a>). In addition, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N differences of &gt;2.2 ‰ between market and field-sourced samples may also suggest mixing or substitution of market-sourced rice with rice from different origins or fertilizer regimes, and/or cultivars.</p>
<p id="p0175">Both Japonica and Indica market-sourced samples showed more positive <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O values in all regions except for Liaoning (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>). These variations may also be associated with post-harvest processes (e.g. polishing) as it has been previously reported that polishing (removal of the rice husk) significantly increases the rice <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O values compared to brown rice due to <sup>18</sup>O differences between the outer surface tissue and the endosperm. The outer layer may contain water with distinct isotopic composition or be more influenced by transpiration related fractionation. The endosperm (polished fraction) may retain water enriched in <sup>18</sup>O, possibly due to evaporative effects or metabolic concentration during grain development (<a href="#bb0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0150">Suzuki et al., 2022</a>).</p>
<p id="p0180">Other factors such as post-harvest storage conditions can't be ignored since a moisture and/or temperature imbalance during storage may alter biochemical aspects of the grain composition (<a href="#bb0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0100">Moraes et al., 2023</a>), indirectly affecting internal isotopic tissue turnover and exchange. Regardless, larger <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O differences of <strong>&gt;</strong>3 ‰ between market and field rice from the same region strongly suggests partial or total cultivar substitution or origin mislabeling of market rice. This study also revealed large <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H differences between farm and market-sourced rice samples of up to 32.0 ‰ in some regions, indicating possible post-harvest treatment effects, such as washing/steaming, non-local processing or blending of rice from different regions and climate zones.</p>
<p id="p0185">Overall, Indica rice samples showed a higher variability in isotopic composition between field and market-sourced samples across all regions than Japonica rice. This suggests that Indica rice is more easily influenced by external factors such as interannual production variation, soil and water resource management, postharvest processing effect than Japonica rice, or that Indica rice is more prone to market mislabeling. Japonica field and market-sourced rice from Jilin, Henan and Jiangsu had the least isotopic variability and showed fewer significant differences between field and market-sourced samples than other regions, suggesting that their origin and cultivar labeling was most likely authentic.</p></section><section id="s0045"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.3. Isotopic analysis of regional rice samples</h3>
<p id="p0190">Eight rice producing areas (with <em>n</em> = 268 samples) were selected and grouped into three regions: north-east (NE), Yangtze River (YR) and southern China (Southern). Japonica rice is mainly produced in NE regions (Heilongjian, Jilin, and Liaoning) while Indica rice is grown mainly in YR (Hunan, Jiangxi, and Hubei) and Southern (Guangdong and Yunnan) regions. Mean <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N, <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O, and <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H values of regional rice ranged between −28.3 ‰ to −26.7 ‰, 3.8 ‰ to 5.6 ‰, −68.1 ‰ to −59.4 ‰, 17.1 ‰ to 21.3 ‰, respectively (Table S2).</p>
<p id="p0195">Mean regional <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C values showed significant differences between regions (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) with the most positive mean <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C value (−26.7 ‰) found for NE rice and the lowest mean <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C value (−28.3 ‰) from Southern rice. All three regions showed significant differences for mean <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values with the highest mean value (5.6 ‰) observed in NE and lowest (3.8 ‰) in YR. Regionally, mean <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H values exhibited significant differences at <em>p</em> &lt; 0.05, between NE (−67.7 ‰) and Southern (−59.4 ‰) samples, and Y R (−68.1 ‰) and Southern (−59.4 ‰) samples. Mean <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O values of regional rice also showed significant differences among all three origins with the highest mean value (21.0 ‰) found in NE, and the lowest (17.1 ‰) in Southern regions.</p>
<p id="p0200">To more accurately assess isotopic variations among different regions, we excluded market-sourced samples and conducted the descriptive statistical analysis using only authentic field samples (<em>n</em> = 134, results, Table S3). Mean field rice <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C values showed significant differences between NE and YR, and NE and Southern field rice, with more positive mean <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C values found in NE rice (−26.9 ‰), and more negative mean <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C values in Southern (−28.7 ‰) and YR (−28.8 ‰) rice. The uptake of <sup>13</sup>C during photosynthesis in C3 plants mainly depends on climate conditions for the assimilation of atmospheric <sup>13</sup>CO<sub>2</sub> during sugar synthesis (<a href="#bb0175" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0175">Wadood et al., 2018</a>). The NE region generally experiences drier, cooler climatic conditions (<a href="#bb0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0065">Li et al., 2022</a>), and more positive mean <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C values in NE regions reflects prevailing climate conditions. Plants discriminate against the heavier <sup>13</sup>C isotope during photosynthesis by preferentially taking up the lighter <sup>12</sup>C isotope. This in turn influences the <sup>13/12</sup>C ratio of internal leaf CO<sub>2</sub> concentration to atmospheric CO<sub>2</sub>. Under drier climatic conditions, plants partially close their stomata to reduce water loss, consequently reducing internal CO<sub>2</sub> concentration which causes less discrimination against <sup>13</sup>C. Hence under water stress, plants tends to incorporate relatively more <sup>13</sup>C, leading to more positive <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C values in their tissues (<a href="#bb0125" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0125">Rao et al., 2017</a>). Similarly, cooler temperatures can reduce plant photosynthetic activity and metabolic rates, which may in turn influence <sup>13</sup>C uptake (<a href="#bb0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0070">Lin et al., 2005</a>).</p>
<p id="p0205">Field rice <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values showed similar patterns with the highest mean <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N value observed in NE rice (6.0 ‰) and lowest mean <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N value in YR rice (4.5 ‰). Significant differences were observed between NE and YR, and YR and South samples (<em>p</em> &lt; 0.05) (Table S4). Generally, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values of plants reflect the soil nutrient properties and cultivation practices of their growing region. In rice crops, <sup>15</sup>N fractionation occurs during various physiological processes, such as nitrogen absorption from the soil, nitrate assimilation and reduction, and recycling of nitrogen into the rice grains (<a href="#bb0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0170">Wadood et al., 2024</a>). Maximum <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values in NE and Southern samples (up to 9.1 ‰ and 14.9 ‰, respectively) indicates the probable use of organic manures during cultivation in these regions. Lower <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N values found in YR rice (median 4.4 ‰) reflects partial or mixed source nitrogen inputs, possibly involving fresh (less denitrified) organic fertilizers, soil-derived nitrogen, or only limited synthetic inputs, rather than extensive use of synthetic fertilizers (<a href="#bb0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0195">Wang et al., 2020</a>).</p>
<p id="p0210"><em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H values exhibited a distinct regional pattern, with the highest mean <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H value found in NE region (−58.3 ‰), followed by Southern (−65.6 ‰) and Y-R (−66.0 ‰) regions; NE and YR showed a significant regional difference, although YR and Southern values did not exhibit any significant regional differences. Variations in <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H values across regions arise from isotopic fractionation processes in precipitation as atmospheric water moves from low to high altitudes, coastal to inland regions, or warmer to cooler climates, leading to increasingly negative <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H and <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O values (<a href="#bb0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0085">Liu et al., 2019</a>; <a href="#bb0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0170">Wadood et al., 2024</a>). More positive mean <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H values observed in NE rice reflects different surface water (from rain) or groundwater irrigation patterns in this region compared to YR and Southern regions.</p>
<p id="p0215">Regional mean <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O values followed a similar trend as <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H, with the highest mean value found in NE region (21.5 ‰) and lowest mean value in Southern region (14.9 ‰) (Table S3). <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O fractionation is primarily associated with local precipitation, altitude, latitude, distance from ocean, and rate of evapotranspiration. The latitudinal range is around 40°N to 50°N for NE regions, 28°N to 34°N for YR regions and 21°N to 28°N for south regions. While <sup>18/16</sup>O ratio in rainwater generally decreases with increasing latitude, plant <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O values can follow a different trend due to combined influence of environmental factors and physiological processes beyond the isotopic composition of source water (<a href="#bb0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0015">Bowen &amp; Wilkinson, 2002</a>; J. <a href="#bb0080" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0080">Liu et al., 2023</a>). In this study, <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O values showed significant increase with latitude away from the equator. This pattern can be associated with a similar evaporative enrichment seen in leaf water, as during transpiration, the lighter oxygen isotope (<sup>16</sup>O) evaporates more readily, leading to an enrichment of <sup>18</sup>O in remaining leaf water (<a href="#bb0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0010">Barbour, 2007</a>). In addition, higher latitude regions (i.e. NE) experience drier climatic conditions that promote increased evaporation and fractionation (loss) of <sup>16</sup>O in paddy water. This enhanced evaporative loss can lead to <sup>18</sup>O enrichment of the remaining water pool, resulting in more positive plant <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O values (<a href="#bb0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bb0020">de la Casa et al., 2022</a>).</p></section><section id="s0050"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">3.4. Multivariate classification of regional rice samples</h3>
<p id="p0220">PCA and t-SNE analysis were applied to visualize geographical origin discrimination by incorporating both market and field samples based on their isotopic compositions (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>). PCA analysis (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>a) generated two principal components (PC1 and PC2) that together explained a combined 66.4 % of the total variance in the dataset, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 38.6 % and 27.8 %, respectively. The overlain loading matrix shows strong positive influence of <em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C and <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O (r ≈ 0.62) on PC1. PC2 was primarily dominated by <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N (<em>r</em> = 0.89), while <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H exhibited a modest negative influence on PC1. PCA achieved a moderate separation, as samples from different regions formed partially overlapping clusters, and are shown by 95 % ellipses enclosing most of the NE, YR, and Southern region samples. NE region rice samples formed a relatively tight cluster and were generally distinguishable from YR and Southern rice samples, although YR and Southern samples had comparably much more overlap.</p>

Predictive modeling of market rice authenticity using machine learning</h3>
<p id="p0230">Based on the chemometric differences between PCA and t-SNE, machine learning models, k-NN, SVM and RF, were used to predict their ability to authenticate origin labelling of market-sourced samples (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>). All models were tested with different parameter settings, and the best features for each model were identified through optimization. The optimal configuration for the k-NN model used 7 neighbors, distance weighting, and Manhattan distance metric to effectively handle high-dimensional data. The SVM model performed best with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel, a regularization parameter (C) of 100 and a gamma value of 0.1; which are parameters that favor the detection of subtle, nonlinear patterns. A random forest (RF) model achieved best test set performance using 200 estimators, a maximum tree depth of 5, and minimal constraints on sample splits, providing a balance between accuracy and overfitting.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/t0005/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section><p id="p0240">In case of Indica rice, the three prediction models (k-NN, SVM, and RF) achieved a good classification on training data (field-sourced samples) with accuracies between 87 % and 100 %, and F1 scores of 1.00 across all Indica rice growing regions (Guangdong, Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi, and Yunnan) (<a href="#f0020" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a>, <a href="#t0010" class="usa-link">Table 2</a>). However, a sharp decline in prediction performance was observed in the testing set (market-sourced samples), with test accuracies of 30 %, 21 %, and 37 % for k-NN, SVM, and RF models, respectively. Only Hunan (F1 = 0.56) and Jiangxi (F1 = 0.52) market-sourced samples showed moderate performance, while the remaining samples were misclassified. Among the tested models, RF showed slightly better performance than k-NN and SVM, which suggests it is a more robust model against data inconsistencies or noise. The high performance of RF on the field-sourced samples validates that the models were optimized properly and parameters were accurately tuned. In contrast, the low RF Indica origin prediction score on the market-sourced samples is likely due to mislabeling, inconsistencies or adulteration of the Indica market-sourced rice samples.</p>

<div class="p text-right font-secondary"><a href="table/t0010/" class="usa-link" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open in a new tab</a></div></section><p id="p0245">Finally, all models were revaluated by splitting the field-sourced dataset into half, where one part served as field-sourced samples and other part as a proxy for market-sourced samples (Table S5). All models were able to correctly predict origin, with testing accuracies exceeding 84 %, further validating the reliability and robustness of the developed models when tested on authentic origin field rice data. These findings reinforce the need for stricter controls and implementing traceability regulations within China's domestic rice supply chain. This study also provides the necessary evidence to show that stable isotopes combined with specific data mining techniques is an effective screening tool for rice geographical origin verification. While this study focuses on rice, the use of stable isotope fingerprinting combined with machine learning workflow can be adapted to other staple crops (wheat, maize, and soy) facing similar issues of mislabeling and food fraud, thereby strengthening traceability and regulatory compliance on a broader scale.</p></section></section><section id="s0060"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">4. Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0250">The present study demonstrates that stable isotope analysis (<em>δ</em><sup>13</sup>C, <em>δ</em><sup>15</sup>N, <em>δ</em><sup>18</sup>O, and <em>δ</em><sup>2</sup>H) is a powerful tool to verify the correct origin-labeling of rice sold in Chinese domestic markets. Significant isotopic variations were observed among different rice growing regions, cultivars, and between authentic field-collected and market-sourced rice samples, indicating substantial issues with rice mislabeling within the supply chain. Chemometric models, particularly t-SNE, effectively captured complex nonlinear patterns in the rice dataset, exhibiting superior discrimination between rice cultivars and their respective geographical origins compared to linear methods like PCA. Although, machine learning models achieved excellent origin prediction performance on authentic field samples, their substantially reduced performance with market rice samples further underscores authenticity challenges in commercial rice products. These results confirm that isotope fingerprinting combined with a machine learning approach offers a scientifically reliable framework for enforcing food authenticity regulations and enhancing traceability. Future studies should focus on expanding sampling across broader regions, verifying different harvest years and assess safety of these samples through heavy metal analysis to address current limitations. In addition, rapid and cost effective screening methods are needed to facilitate routine application in quality control and regulatory frameworks. Overall, this study provides essential insights and practical tools for safeguarding consumers and ensuring labelling integrity in the Chinese rice market.</p></section><section id="s0065"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">CRediT authorship contribution statement</h2>
