<span class="label">•</span><p class="display-inline" id="p0005">Full metabolomes of bubble-tip anemone and upside-down jellyfish were analyzed for stress markers.</p>

<section id="sec1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h3>
<p>Climate change poses various threats to marine life, particularly in shallow tropical waters. Objective: The impact of increased temperature and ultraviolet (UV) exposure on two photosymbiotic cnidarians, a common bubble-tip anemone and an upside-down jellyfish, was investigated.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h3>
<p>To illustrate the response of aquatic organisms, the metabolomes of unstressed <em>Entacmaea quadricolor</em> and <em>Cassiopea andromeda</em> were compared for detailed metabolite profiling. UHPLC-MS coupled with chemometrics and GNPS molecular networking was employed for sample classification and identification of markers unique to stress responses in each organism.</p></section><section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>Several compounds with bioactive functions, including peptides and terpenoids, were reported for the first time in both organisms, viz. cyclic tetraglutamate, campestriene, and ceramide aminoethyl phosphonate (CEAP d18:2/16:0). Both anemone and jellyfish were subjected to either elevated UV-B light intensity up to 6.6 KJ m<sup>−2</sup> or increased temperatures (28 °C, 30 °C, 32 °C, and 34 °C) over 4 days. Phospholipids, steroids, and ceramides emerged as chief markers of both types of stress, as revealed by the multivariate data analysis. Lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC 16:0), LPC (18:0/0:0), and echinoclasterol sulfate appeared as markers in both UV and thermal stress models of the anemone, whereas methyl/propyl cholestane-<em>hexa</em>-ol were discriminatory in the UV stress model only. In the case of jellyfish, nonpolar glycosyl ceramide GlcCer (d14:1/28:6) served as a marker for UV stress, whereas polar peptides were elevated in the thermal stress model. Interestingly, both models of jellyfish share a phospholipid, lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE 20:4), as a distinctive marker for stress, reported to be associated indirectly with the activity of innate immune response within other photosymbiotic Cnidaria such as corals and appears to be a fundamental stress response in marine organisms.</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h3>
<p>This study presents several bioinformatic tools for the first time in two cnidarian organisms to provide not only a broader coverage of their metabolome but also broader insights into cnidarian bleaching in response to different stressors, i.e., heat and UV light, by comparing their effects in anemone versus jellyfish.</p></section></section><section id="s0005"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p id="p0025">Anemones and jellyfish are aquatic members of the phylum <em>Cnidaria</em>, which includes corals and hydroids. Anemones belong to the class Anthozoans, whereas jellyfish belong to the class Scyphozoa. Cnidarians are known for their specialized stinging cells, which contain tiny harpoon-like structures called nematocysts. These nematocysts are used for defence and predation <a href="#b0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0005">[1]</a>.</p>
<p id="p0030"><em>Entacmaea quadricolor</em> is a species of sea anemone that belongs to the family Actiniidae, also commonly known as the bubble-tip anemone due to the distinctive “bubble-like” appearance of their tentacles. It is known for its mutual symbiotic relationship with certain species of clownfish <a href="#b0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0010">[2]</a>, particularly those from the genera <em>Amphiprion</em>
<a href="#b0015" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0015">[3]</a> and <em>Premna</em>. The latter live within the tentacles of anemones protected against predators and unharmed by the host’s nematocytes. It has not been confirmed whether anemones produce a protective mucous coating, or whether the mucous coat prevents nematocyte discharge <a href="#b0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0020">[4]</a>. <em>E. quadricolor</em> has a mid-range haemolytic and neurotoxic toxicity. Furthermore, the bubble-tip anemone forms associations with 16 of the 28 anemonefish species <a href="#b0025" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0025">[5]</a>. The bubble-tip anemone has unique tentacle shapes that increase the surface area in which the anemonefish can hide. Therefore, <em>E. quadricolor</em> is considered the most popular host of anemonefish, yet research on its venom composition is limited <a href="#b0030" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0030">[6]</a>. Anemones provide habitat and sustenance to many marine organisms and are threatened by coral bleaching upon disruption of such a symbiotic relationship. Bleaching occurs when cnidarians lose their vibrant colors and become noticeably clear, opaque, or white because of the absence of their photosymbiotic organisms, i.e.<em>,</em> zooxanthellae and other symbiotic algae <a href="#b0035" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0035">[7]</a> under stress conditions such as elevated temperatures associated with climate change <a href="#b0040" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0040">[8]</a>. Stressors, such as elevated seawater temperatures, pollution, ultraviolet radiation exposure, and ocean acidification, can disrupt this symbiotic relationship <a href="#b0045" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0045">[9]</a> and threaten marine life <a href="#b0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0050">[10]</a>. For example, the sea anemone <em>Aiptasia pallida</em> experiences increased zooxanthellae expulsion when exposed to elevated UV-B and temperature <a href="#b0055" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0055">[11]</a> and the <em>E. quadricolor</em> is known to respond differently to thermal stress, dependong on their phenotype color and hence endosymbiont community composition <a href="#b0060" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0060">[12]</a>. For the sea anemone <em>Anemonia viridis</em>, a major transcriptomic response to thermal stress was found within 24 h, which returned to baseline after two days <a href="#b0065" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0065">[13]</a>. In comparison, UV radiation alone had little effect but potentiated the response to thermal stress if combined, affecting several specific biological pathways involved in the stress response, including mesoglea loosening, cell death, and calcium homeostasis.</p>
<p id="p0035"><em>Cassiopea andromeda</em> is a species of jellyfish belonging to the family Cassiopeidae, also known as the upside-down jellyfish, as it lives resting with its flattened bell on the sea floor <a href="#b0070" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0070">[14]</a>. Like other jellyfish, <em>C. andromeda</em> possesses stinging cells called nematocysts, which are needed for animal feeding and defense. Recent studies have also indicated that jellyfish may serve as a valuable source of bioactive compounds <em>such as</em> antioxidant peptides <a href="#b0075" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0075">[15]</a>, <a href="#b0080" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0080">[16]</a>. As jellyfish populations have increased dramatically in many marine ecosystems, causing detrimental effects on marine life, there has been growing scientific concern regarding the impact of these animals on marine ecosystems <a href="#b0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0085">[17]</a>. <em>Cassiopea</em> spp. occur commonly across a wide range of shallow tropical habitats, and <em>C. andromeda</em>, particularly, even made an appearance in non-indigenous habitats raising the need to investigate and mitigate such phenomenon <a href="#b0090" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0090">[18]</a>. The effects of elevated temperature and UV-B radiation on jellyfish are complex, including changes in behavior at sublethal temperatures and enhanced performance (ie.e. greater bell pulsation and diameter) under heat exposure, potentially facilitating range expansion <a href="#b0095" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0095">[19]</a> to decrease photosynthetic efficiency and generally reduce the ability to thrive induced by both elevated UV-B and temperature <a href="#b0100" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0100">[20]</a>, inhibited growth, increased aerobic respiration, and altered enzymatic activities under UV exposure <a href="#b0105" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0105">[21]</a>. These studies highlight the complex interplay between environmental stressors and cnidarian physiology, emphasizing the need for further research to understand the long-term effects of climate change on these organisms.</p>
<p id="p0040">Understanding the impact of global warming on cnidarian metabolism and metabolite production can provide insights into these ecological effects and may lead to solutions <a href="#b0085" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0085">[17]</a>. Nevertheless, the consequences of climate change on the cnidarian metabolome remain relatively unexplored field <a href="#b0110" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0110">[22]</a>, <a href="#b0115" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0115">[23]</a>, <a href="#b0120" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0120">[24]</a>, although several studies have focused on detecting metabolites derived from cnidarians <a href="#b0125" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0125">[25]</a>, <a href="#b0130" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0130">[26]</a> and their possible uses in health treatment <a href="#b0135" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0135">[27]</a>. For example, several bioactive metabolites have been reported, such as peptides with strong antioxidant potential, such as hydrolyzed collagen, or with cytotoxic potential on cancer cells <a href="#b0140" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0140">[28]</a> hence, their medical importance. Among the chemicals reported in jellyfish, fatty acids and lipid derivatives are the major classes of metabolites <a href="#b0145" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0145">[29]</a>. Cnidarians reportedly produce noticeably fewer antimicrobial, antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral macrolides than other aquatic organisms, suggesting that they are less likely to exert antimicrobial actions <a href="#b0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0150">[30]</a>.</p>
<p id="p0045">The consequences of cnidarian bleaching extend beyond cnidarians themselves, as many marine organisms depend on them for either protection from predators or their role in nutrient cycling in marine ecosystems <a href="#b0010" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0010">[2]</a>. As a result of coral bleaching, the entire marine ecosystem can be disrupted, leading to a decline in biodiversity and wide-reaching ecological and economic impacts on coastal communities <a href="#b0020" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0020">[4]</a>. The mutualistic association between corals and intracellular, photosynthetic dinoflagellates belonging to the family Symbiodniaceae is crucial for the survival of coral reef ecosystems. These symbionts are physiologically varied and show distinct patterns of environmental and host dispersion according to genetic data <a href="#b0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0155">[31]</a>. Therefore, a better understanding of the metabolomic responses and potential interactions of host cnidarians and their diverse photosymbiotic partners is crucial.</p>
<p id="p0050">This study focused on determining the major effects of environmental climatic change on two different aquatic organisms. <em>E. quadricolor</em> and <em>C. andromeda</em> through two main stressors: elevated temperature and ultraviolet radiation (UV) as a novel approach. In addition to identifying key metabolites that contribute to the ecological health of marine organisms and are biomarkers of environmental stress caused by global warming, this study aids in predicting the impact of the bleaching process in an earlier phase. The results obtained from this study provide a better understanding of the impact of environmental stress, i.e.<em>,</em> UV radiation and thermal stress exposure, on these animals at the metabolome level. The study encompassed four different temperatures (28 °C, 30 °C, 32 °C, and 34 °C), or a daily UV-B (285 and 310 nm) radiation dose of 2.2 KJ m<sup>−2</sup>, at four different time points.</p>
<p id="p0055">The Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) platform and chemometric tools were employed to aid peak identification. The novelty of this study is that it integrates all the aforementioned bioinformatic tools for the first time in both marine organisms to provide not only a broader coverage of their metabolome, but also broader comprehensive insights into cnidarian bleaching in response to different stressors, i.e.<em>,</em> heat and UV light, by comparing their effects in anemone versus jellyfish.</p></section><section id="s0010"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials and methods</h2>
<section id="s0015"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Culture of animals</h3>
<p id="p0060">To conduct a comparative study with two cnidaria species, namely a jellyfish (<em>Cassiopeia andromeda</em>) and a sea anemone (<em>Entacmaea quadricolor</em>), an <em>ex-situ</em> experiment was carried out in the marine experimental facilities (MAREE) at the Leibniz Centre for Tropical Marine Research (ZMT), in Bremen, Germany. The stock cultures of these animals have been cultured in aquaria for about ten years under similar conditions, and both species are therefore assumed to contain highly homogeneous photosymbiont communities. Both species host specific clades of dinoflagellate symbionts from the Symbiodiniaceae family. <em>Cassiopea</em> predominantly hosts <em>Symbiodinium</em> (clade A), while <em>Entacmaea quadricolor</em> typically contains symbionts from <em>Cladocopium</em> (clade C), though it can harbor multiple clades depending on environmental conditions <a href="#b0160" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0160">[32]</a>, <a href="#b0165" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0165">[33]</a>. All experiments in this study were conducted in accordance with German ethics on animal welfare.</p>
<p id="p0065">Before the experiment, <em>C. andromeda</em> and <em>E. quadricolor</em> specimens were reared in aquarium tanks (200 L) connected to an automated water treatment system, and the water was circulated through a biofilter and a protein skimmer (EHEIM, Germany). Following the recirculating water treatment, approximately 25 % of the culture water was exchanged every week to maintain water quality at the highest level. The tanks were filled with artificial seawater (Red Sea Salt, Red Sea Fish, Israel) with water temperature and salinity set at 26 °C and 35 S<sub>A</sub>, respectively. The ambient light was provided artificially using Aquaillumination Hydra FiftyTwo HD (AI Hydra 52 HyperDrive, USA) lamps with seven types of LEDs, emitting the full spectrum (380–680 nm) of photoactive radiation (PAR) with a photon flux density of 100 μmol photons m<sup>−2</sup> s<sup>−1</sup> under a 12:12 h light/dark cycle. The specimens were fed daily with freshly hatched <em>Artemia nauplii</em> and kept under such conditions for at least six months before being used in this study.</p></section><section id="s0020"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Acclimatization and experimental setup</h3>
<p id="p0070">For each species, jellyfish (size from 2 to 6 cm, life span ca. 4 years) and sea anemone (size from 2 to 4 cm, life span ca. 10 years), 48 equally sized (according to wet weight), intact, and healthy individuals were removed from the MAREE baseline tanks and placed into an experimental system. The experimental setup consisted of three water baths (200 L), each containing four plastic aquaria (10 L) carrying eight test organisms (four of each species). In the experimental setup, test specimens were acclimated for one week to baseline salinity (35.0 PSU) and light conditions (blue/white combination of fluorescence bulbs 4×54 W T5 at a 12 h day/night cycle). The temperature was incrementally increased to 28 °C during the acclimatization phase. After this week of acclimatization, an experimental phase with a length of four days was started. Throughout the experimental phase, the water temperature in one water bath was elevated (2 °C/day) to reach 30 °C on day two, 32 °C on day three, and 34 °C on the final day four. In the second water bath, the test specimens were exposed to a daily dose (2.2 KJ m<sup>−2</sup>) of UV-B (285 and 310 nm) irradiation. The daily UV-B dose was emitted throughout 1 h over the experimental time the specimens received a total UV-B dose of 2.2 KJ m<sup>−2</sup> on day two, 4.4 KJ m<sup>−2</sup> on day three, and 6.6 KJ m<sup>−2</sup> on day four. The third water bath served as a control treatment, with constant environmental parameters set at the end of the acclimatization phase. To maintain good water quality in the closed plastic aquaria, the test specimens were not fed during the acclimatization and experimental phases, and 30 % of the water volume was exchanged daily. To maintain a constant salinity in the aquaria, the volume of evaporated water was refilled daily with osmosis water before water exchange.</p>
<p id="p0075">In addition to the monitoring of water parameters, the following data collection and sampling routine was conducted daily throughout the experiment: 1) documentation of general vitality parameters for <em>C. andromeda</em> (number of bell pulsations per minute) and <em>E. quadricolor</em> (organism closed or tentacles spread out) and observations such as behavioral abnormalities and injuries. 2) quantum yield of photosystem II (PSII) (Fv/Fm) using pulse amplitude-modulated fluorometry (Diving-PAM; Walz, Effeltrich, Germany). 3) Sampling one specimen of each species from every plastic aquarium (n = 4) per treatment by snap-freezing the whole specimen in liquid nitrogen and storing it at −80 °C until further chemical analyses.</p></section><section id="s0025"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Chemicals and reagents</h3>
<p id="p0080">LC/MS grade solvents were purchased from Baker (The Netherlands), and MilliQ water was used for all LC analyses. Sarcophine standard was purchased from AG Scientific (San Diego, CA, USA). Louis, MO, USA).</p></section><section id="s0030"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample preparation for (UHPLC-ESI-LIT-Orbitrap-MS) analysis</h3>
<p id="p0085">Sample extraction followed the protocol described in a previous report <a href="#b0170" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0170">[34]</a> with some modifications explained as follows. Approximately 20 mg of powdered freeze-dried anemone or jellyfish tissues was ground with a pestle in a mortar under liquid nitrogen. The powder was then homogenized with 2.0 mL 100 % ethanol containing 10μg ml<sup>−1</sup> umbelliferone (as an internal standard for UHPLC-MS) using an ultrasonic bath for 20 min. The extracts were then centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min to remove debris. For UHPLC-MS analyses, 500 μL was aliquoted and filtered through a 22 μm filter. For each specimen, four biological replicates were extracted and analyzed in parallel under the same conditions.</p></section><section id="s0035"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolites profiling of animal extracts via high-resolution ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis (UHPLC-ESI-LIT-Orbitrap-MS)</h3>
<p id="p0090">For metabolite profiling in control and stressed animal specimens at different time points, runs of UHPLC-ESI linear ion trap-orbital trap hybrid mass spectrometry were carried out using a Dionex UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany). UHPLC separation was carried out on a Waters Acquity HSS T3 column (particle size 1.8 µm, pore size 100 Å, 100×1 mm ID, Waters GmbH, Eschborn, Germany), column temperature 40 °C using the same binary gradient and flow rate described for the LCQ Deca XP MAX system using water and acetonitrile supplemented with 0.1 % formic acid as eluents A and B, respectively. The flow rate was set at 0.15 mL min<sup>−1</sup>, and the following separation gradient starting on injection was implemented: 1 min of 5 % B, linear ramp to 100 % B in 10 min, isocratic part at 100 % B for the next 8 min with ramp back to 5 % B in 1 min and a re-equilibration phase of 10 min, with an injection volume of 2 μL. Mass spectra were collected in negative and positive ion modes for the <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> range of 200 – 2,000, heated ESI source set at 250 °C, spray voltage 4.0 kV, capillary temperature 300 °C, FTMS resolution 30,000. In the positive and negative modes, the sheath, auxiliary, and sweep gases (nitrogen) were adjusted to 18, 12, and 0 arbitrary units or 20, 5, and 1 arbitrary units, respectively. The CID mass spectra (buffer gas: helium) in the data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode were recorded using a normalized collision energy of 35 % with an FTMS resolution of 15,000. The data were evaluated using Xcalibur software 2.2 SP1.48.</p></section><section id="s0040"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">UHPLC-MS data processing, metabolites identification, and modeling</h3>
<p id="p0095">Raw data were visualized using the open-access data analysis software Mzmine 2.53 <a href="#b0175" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0175">[35]</a>, where peaks were identified according to their retention times, accurate masses, elution order, fragmentation patterns, and formulas suggested by the software. The files were further analyzed by exporting mgf-files to Sirius 4.9.12+ CSI:FingerID software <a href="#b0180" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0180">[36]</a> (<a href="https://bio.informatik.uni-jena.de/sirius/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://bio.informatik.uni-jena.de/sirius/</a>) to manually search structural databases, i.e., PubChem, a phytochemical dictionary of natural products, to aid in identification, where structural hits search was set to 20 ppm as <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> tolerance cut off.</p>
<section id="s0045"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Feature-based molecular networking</h4>
<p id="p0100">The Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) platform is a powerful database that has revolutionized the identification of metabolites, enabling scientists to explore the immense potential of natural marine products. Compounds are aligned in generated molecular networks that serve as visual representations of the chemical relationships between different compounds <a href="#b0185" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0185">[37]</a>. GNPS was employed to aid peak identification by comparing the tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) data to online databases and distributing metabolites in classified clusters, especially as the first attempt in both marine organisms.</p>
<p id="p0105">Raw UHPLC-MS/MS files were uploaded as mgf files to the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking open-access website (<a href="https://gnps.ucsd.edu" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://gnps.ucsd.edu</a>). Data were analyzed using an online workflow, where the precursor ion mass tolerance and MS/MS fragment ion tolerance were both set to 0.07 Da. Accordingly, networks were created where the edges had a cosine score &gt;0.7 and more than four matching peaks. Further, the spectra in the network were queried against GNPS spectral databases (NIST13, MassBank, and Respect) and further visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape software.</p></section><section id="s0050"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">UHPLC-MS multivariate data analysis</h4>
<p id="p0110">Furthermore, chemometric tools were employed to aid in species classification and marker identification in response to different stressors. Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA), in addition to supervised methods such as orthogonal projection to least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), was employed <a href="#b0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0050">[10]</a>. The raw files of UHPLC-MS of all samples were converted into “.abf” files, using an ABF converter (<a href="https://www.reifycs.com/AbfConverter/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.reifycs.com/AbfConverter/</a>). Accordingly, MS-DIAL software (<a href="https://prime.psc.riken.jp/compms/msdial/main.html" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://prime.psc.riken.jp/compms/msdial/main.html</a>) was employed for data extraction using the following parameters: retention time (0–25 min), mass range (50–1,000 Da), and accurate mass tolerance for MS1 (0.01 Da) and MS2 (0.025 Da). The peak abundance mass list was exported for the multivariate data analysis. The exported data were subjected to unsupervised and supervised multivariate data analysis, i.e., PCA, hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA), and OPLS-DA using SIMCA 14.1 software (Umetrics, Sweden) to show the segregation and aggregation patterns between samples and highlight the variation. All input variables were scaled to the Pareto scale and mean-centered.</p></section></section></section><section id="s0055"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results and Discussion</h2>
<section id="s0060"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">UV light and thermal impact on cnidarian organisms' appearance and PSII quantum yield</h3>
<p id="p0115">The visual examination of animals during the study of increasing UV light exposure versus increasing tank water temperature from 28 °C to 34 °C over 4 days period revealed how stressors affected the morphological appearance of both organisms. The day 1 samples (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 1</a>A) shows anemones and jellyfish under control conditions without UV exposure and at 28 °C. UV-treated anemones sampled on day 2 (2.2 KJ m<sup>−2</sup>) showed closed tentacles, as depicted in <a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 1</a>B1, while in the case of jellyfish, mucus was discharged, and their tentacles looked centered forming a crown shape <a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 1</a>B2, and some jellyfish lost their tentacles. On the 3rd day of UV exposure (4.4 KJ m<sup>−2</sup>), jellyfish started to flip over and lose control of their position with more mucus discharge and total loss of tentacles <a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 1</a>(C1, C2). In contrast, the anemones seemed swollen as if they had absorbed an excess of water, showing an oddly translucent appearance, unlike their normal state, and started to turn slightly transparent, as illustrated in <a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 1</a>C3. The elevated temperature did not show obvious morphological differences, except for the sudden death of the anemone on day 4 as the temperature reached 34 °C (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 1</a>D1). Conversely, on the 4th day of UV exposure (6.6 KJ m<sup>−2</sup>), anemones appeared filled with much less water (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 1</a>D2, compared to their counterparts under control conditions. In addition, jellyfish showed minimal activity and could not regulate locomotive functions (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 1</a>D3).</p>
<p id="p0120">When exposed to elevated temperature or UV radiation, Fv/Fm declined in anemone reaching minimum value on the 4th and 3rd day, respectively (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Table 1</a>). Symbionts conferred some tolerance to the host on the first two days, most obvious on the 2nd day. At 32 °C and 34 °C, anemone the thermal tolerance was lost as Fv/Fm declined significantly. In contrast, the decline of Fv/Fm was less pronounced in anemone upon UV exposure suggesting that anemone may differentially acclimate to light levels based on stress phenotype. The jellyfish were more constantly affected by both stressors as manifested in the Fv/Fm decline reaching the lowest value on the 4th day (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Table 2</a>). In addition, bell pulsations per minute were declined especially upon exposure to UV radiation. Interestingly, Fv/Fm declined faster in anemone compared to jellyfish. This may be attributed to different symbiont genotypes in both animals. Upon exposure to certain stress at the same dose, jellyfish increased their rate of photosynthesis even if PSII damage occurred while, anemone relied on photoinhibition to prevent PSII damage from occurring in the first place <a href="#b0190" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0190">[38]</a>.</p></section><section id="s0065"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Metabolites profiling in cnidarian organisms via UHPLC-MS aided by molecular networking</h3>
<p id="p0125">To understand the changes in metabolite profiles associated with the bleaching process, alongside anemone and jellyfish metabolome profiles unreported in the literature, untargeted metabolomics in the two cnidarian organisms was attempted via UHPLC-MS in control animals and post-exposure to stressors, i.e.<em>,</em> elevated temperature and UV radiation. A total of 134 primary and secondary metabolites were annotated in the control and stressed organisms, as detected in both positive and negative ionization modes, to provide a comprehensive overview of the metabolomes of animals. The identified metabolites were categorized into several classes including phospholipids, glycolipids, fatty acids, peptides, sphingolipids, ceramides, and terpenoids. Metabolite annotation was based on the elution range, i.e.<em>,</em> retention time, accurate mass (<em>m</em>/<em>z</em>) and molecular formula, tandem MS/MS, and isotopic distribution, as detailed in <a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Tables S3 and S4</a>.</p>
<p id="p0130">Furthermore, global natural product social (GNPS) molecular networking was employed for the first time to aid in the peak identification and annotation of unknown peaks, especially as the first attempt in both species to characterize their metabolome. The relative abundance of each metabolite in different specimens is illustrated in a pie chart for each node, where each sample was encoded with a different color to aid in the visual observation of the segregation patterns (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>). The colored codes of all samples are explained for each network.</p>

The following subsections explain the identification details for each metabolite class (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. S3-S9</a>) alongside major changes observed upon exposure to stress conditions in both animals.</p></section><section id="s0070"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Phospholipids (PLs)</h3>
<p id="p0155">Phospholipids (PLs) act as integral membrane components that maintain the integrity of cell membranes <a href="#b0205" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0205">[41]</a> and are likely to play a major role in the heat stress response. PLs also contribute to maintaining normal cell physiological functions, such as the production and transport of crucial peptides into the cells of jellyfish <a href="#b0210" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0210">[42]</a>. Phospholipids were the most identified metabolite class represented by 21 peaks in <em>E. quadricolor,</em> and 25 peaks in <em>C. andromeda,</em> with the largest clusters in molecular networks for both positive and negative ionization modes. Phospholipids can be subcategorized into phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylinositol (PI), lysophospholipids (LP), and lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE).</p>
<p id="p0160">GNPS aided in the identification of phospholipid classes based on specific mass differences of 12 amu, 16 amu, and 28 amu. This was demonstrated in the negative mode networks of anemone, where a node with a precursor mass of <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 478 was connected to three nodes at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 466, 494, and 450 through 12, 16, and 28-amu labeled edges, respectively (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. S2</a>a and b). Likewise, a tetra-nodal cluster built up of precursor masses at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 480 (peak A34), 498 (peak A7), 500 (peak A11), and 522 (peak A47) exhibited 18, 20, 22, and 24 amu labeled edges separating the nodes (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. S2</a>b). These peaks were annotated as LPE (18:0), PE (O-20:5/0:0), LPE (20:4), and PC (O-18:0/O-1:0).</p>
<p id="p0165">Additionally, a parent ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 464 with the molecular formula [C<sub>23</sub>H<sub>47</sub>NO<sub>6</sub>P]<sup>-</sup> in peak A39 showed fragmented ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 421 and 267 due to the successive loss of ethyl amine and glycerophosphate moieties, respectively (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. S3</a>).</p>
<p id="p0170">In jellyfish, lysophosphatidylcholines (LPCs) were identified mostly based on fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 184 <a href="#b0215" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0215">[43]</a>, exemplified in LPC (O-16:0) at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 482 [M + H]<sup>+</sup> (C<sub>24</sub>H<sub>53</sub>NO<sub>6</sub>P<sup>+</sup>), and LPC (O-14:0) at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 454 [M + H]<sup>+</sup> (C<sub>22</sub>H<sub>49</sub>NO<sub>6</sub>P<sup>+</sup>). LPC appeared to decline upon both thermal and UV exposure, thus affecting the cell membrane integrity, which might lead to component diffusion. PEs were mostly detected in the negative ionization mode (<a href="#f0005" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a>B, <a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. S2</a>) and identified based on the product ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 140 and 196 (from [M−H]<sup>-</sup>). Saturated PLs were exemplified in PE (P-16:0) detected at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 436 (C<sub>21</sub>H<sub>43</sub>NO<sub>6</sub>P<sup>-</sup>) and PE (O-18:0) at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 464 (C<sub>23</sub>H<sub>47</sub>NO<sub>6</sub>P<sup>-</sup>) as the major peaks in jellyfish. Polyunsaturated PEs, i.e.<em>,</em> LPE (20:4) at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 500 (C<sub>25</sub>H<sub>43</sub>NO<sub>7</sub>P<sup>-</sup>), showed a 1.4-fold increase upon thermal and UV stresses, and opposite to the observed pattern in the case of LPC. Additionally, lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI 18:0) at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 599 (C<sub>27</sub>H<sub>52</sub>O<sub>12</sub>P<sup>-</sup>) was annotated based on the fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 241 (C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>10</sub>O<sub>8</sub>P<sup>-</sup>) <a href="#b0220" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0220">[44]</a>, whereas phosphatidylserine (PS 18:0/0:0) at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 526 was annotated based on the loss of 87 amu corresponding to the serine group, in accordance with literature <a href="#b0215" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0215">[43]</a>.</p></section><section id="s0075"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Glycolipids</h3>
<p id="p0175">Glycolipids function as receptors on cell membranes and may account for specific cellular contacts and signal transduction. They have been reported in jellyfish-associated organisms <a href="#b0225" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0225">[45]</a> exemplified in monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (MGDG) and digalactosyldiacylglycerol (DGDG). A total of seven peaks were detected in jellyfish, compared to four from this class in anemone (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Table S3 and S4</a>).</p>
<p id="p0180">MGDG was annotated based on the typical fragmentation pattern in glycolipids resulting from the combined loss of ammonia NH<sub>3</sub> (17amu), followed by a hexose moiety loss (162amu) leading to an [M + H-179]<sup>+</sup> fragment ion, and the other fragment ion corresponding to [M + H-197]<sup>+</sup>, which is formed by the combined loss of NH<sub>3</sub> (17amu) followed by a hexose ring loss (180amu) <a href="#b0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0195">[39]</a>. MGDG was exemplified in MGDG (38:9) at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 814 (C<sub>47</sub>H<sub>76</sub>NO<sub>10</sub><sup>+</sup>), and MGDG (36:7) at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 790 (C<sub>45</sub>H<sub>76</sub>NO<sub>10</sub><sup>+</sup>). All the identified MGDGs showed a massive decline upon exposure to UV and heat.</p>
<p id="p0185">Similarly, DGDGs represented in DGDG (36:8) at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 950 (C<sub>51</sub>H<sub>84</sub>NO<sub>15</sub><sup>+</sup>) and DGDG 38:9 at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 976 (C<sub>53</sub>H<sub>86</sub>NO<sub>15</sub><sup>+</sup>) were identified according to reported fragmentation, showing key fragment ions [M + H-197]<sup>+</sup> and [M + H-359]<sup>+</sup>
<a href="#b0195" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0195">[39]</a>. Both MGDG and DGDG showed an approximately 1.4-to 1.8-fold increase in abundance after thermal exposure.</p></section><section id="s0080"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sphingolipids/ceramides</h3>
<p id="p0190">Ceramide clusters were observed in positive ion mode with mass shifts on the edges of 2 (H<sub>2</sub>), 26 (C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>2</sub>), and 28 (CO<sub>2</sub>) amu (<a href="#f0010" class="usa-link">Fig. 2</a>b). Aquatic organisms such as cnidaria (i.e.<em>,</em> jellyfish and anemones) are characterized by the presence of sphingophospholipids, which contribute to cellular membrane protection and signaling <a href="#b0230" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0230">[46]</a>. In contrast to the aforementioned glycolipids, ceramides are correlated with cold acclimation by increasing unsaturated fatty acid levels in cold habitats <a href="#b0230" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0230">[46]</a>. Both organisms encompassed similar numbers of peaks in this class.</p>
<p id="p0195">In anemone, peak A67 [M + H]<sup>+</sup> was detected at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 554 with a base peak at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 494 corresponding to the loss of water and propyl groups, in addition to the key fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 146 attributed to the <em>glycero</em>-amide unit (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. S4</a>) and annotated as Cer (t18:0/16:0) (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Table S3</a>).</p>
<p id="p0200">In both organisms, ceramide aminoethylphosphonate (CAEP d18:2/16:0) was detected in peak A27 and as regioisomer peaks J23 and J38 at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 643 [M + H]<sup>+</sup>, with C<sub>36</sub>H<sub>72</sub>N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub>P<sup>+</sup> as a major peak and annotated with a neutral loss of 125 amu, corresponding to the aminoethylphosphonate moiety <a href="#b0215" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0215">[43]</a>. Additionally, glucosylceramide (GlcCer d14:1/28:6) was annotated at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 800 [M + H]<sup>+</sup> and C<sub>48</sub>H<sub>82</sub>NO<sub>8</sub><sup>+</sup> upon neutral loss of 162 amu and 180 amu <a href="#b0215" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0215">[43]</a>. Most ceramides showed increased levels in both treatments, ranging from 1.3 to 3 fold increase.</p></section><section id="s0085"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Peptides</h3>
<p id="p0205">Compared to the abundance of lipid classes in jellyfish, polar cyclic peptides have been detected in both organisms despite the difficulty in their identification due to their low abundance <a href="#b0005" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0005">[1]</a>. There is increasing interest in marine peptide characterization owing to their biological activities such as antimicrobial, antitumor, and antiviral properties <a href="#b0235" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0235">[47]</a>. It should be noted that in both organisms, peptides were only detected in the negative ionization mode, highlighting the value of comparing the results from both ionization modes.</p>
<p id="p0210">The molecular ion peak at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 582 was identified as tuftsin-hexylamide (peak A48), identified in the anemone, showing a base peak at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 522, corresponding to the loss of NH<sub>2</sub> and CH<sub>3</sub>-CHOH groups (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. S5</a>).</p>
<p id="p0215">Cyclic tetraglutamate (C<sub>20</sub>H<sub>27</sub>N<sub>4</sub>O<sub>12</sub><sup>-</sup>), also known as cnidarin peptide, at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 515 <a href="#b0240" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0240">[48]</a> was detected in <em>C. andromeda,</em> which was previously reported in the class <em>Cubozoa,</em> known as box jellyfish. Identification was based on the consecutive loss of the glutamyl fragment (129amu), and the appearance of fragment ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 386 [M−129]<sup>-</sup> and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 257 [M-129–129]<sup>-</sup>. Additionally, the loss of 62 amu in peak J5 confirmed the peptide structure, which summed up the loss of water (18amu), followed by C<sub>2</sub>H<sub>4</sub>O loss (44amu). Cyclo(isoleucylprolylleucylprolyl) (C<sub>22</sub>H<sub>35</sub>N<sub>4</sub>O<sub>4</sub><sup>-</sup>) at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 419 was previously reported in a jellyfish-associated microbiome <a href="#b0245" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0245">[49]</a> and its biosynthesis should be examined to identify the role of the symbiont and/or host in its production.</p></section><section id="s0090"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Terpenoids/steroids</h3>
<p id="p0220">Terpenoids, a major secondary metabolite class in marine organisms, play major ecological roles and are abundantly produced by cnidarians <a href="#b0250" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0250">[50]</a>, in addition to their several health benefits in humans <a href="#b0255" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0255">[51]</a>. The number of identified peaks was slightly higher in anemones (13peaks) than in jellyfish (11peaks) (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Table S3 and S4</a><strong>)</strong>. They can serve as chemical defence compounds, enhance microbial attachment, and promote symbiosis among different organisms <a href="#b0260" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0260">[52]</a>. An algal-associated terpenoid was detected at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 498 (peak J10) [M−H]<sup>-</sup> (C<sub>30</sub>H<sub>44</sub>NO<sub>5</sub>)<sup>,</sup> annotated as the symbiotic terpenoid zooxanthellamine <a href="#b0265" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0265">[53]</a>, which decreased upon exposure to both stressors. Zooxanthellae algae live within cnidarian tissue, providing the host with energy through photosynthesis <a href="#b0050" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0050">[10]</a>. This symbiotic relationship is disrupted in cnidarians during thermal stress <a href="#b0270" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0270">[54]</a> and thus likely accounts for the decline in zooxanthellamine levels observed here.</p>
<p id="p0225">A bisabolene terpenoid (peak J19), previously reported in soft corals <a href="#b0275" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0275">[55]</a>, another cnidarian, was detected at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 203 [M + H]<sup>+</sup> (C<sub>15</sub>H<sub>23</sub>)<sup>+</sup>. Peak A23 in the anemone exhibited a molecular ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 487 and a fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 395, corresponding to the respective loss of ring A and 2H<sub>2,</sub> forming two double bonds. The major fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 377 was attributed to the loss of the side chain attached to C<sub>13</sub> and two water molecules (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. S6</a>). Therefore, peak A23 was annotated as methyl cholestadiene tetrol acetate. The molecular ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 327 [M−H]<sup>–</sup>of peak A45 yielded daughter ions at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 283, 229, and 191, respectively. The fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 229 was attributed to the loss of acetate and the C<sub>7</sub>-C<sub>8</sub> unit (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. S7</a>), whereas the fission between C<sub>6</sub>-C<sub>7</sub> and C<sub>1</sub>-C<sub>14</sub> yielded a daughter ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 191. Hence, peak A45 was annotated as brassicolene <a href="#b0280" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0280">[56]</a>. Brassicolene, a cytotoxic diterpenoid, has demonstrated efficacy against lung adenocarcinoma and lymphoma <a href="#b0285" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0285">[57]</a>, and this is the first time it has been identified in anemones.</p>
<p id="p0230">Campestadiene (peak J62) was detected in jellyfish, while other steroids exemplified in campestriene (peaks A64, J57) were found in both organisms and annotated based on literature data <a href="#b0290" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0290">[58]</a> at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 383, and <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 381 with proposed formulas [M + H]<sup>+</sup> (C<sub>28</sub>H<sub>47</sub>)<sup>+</sup>, and (C<sub>28</sub>H<sub>45</sub>)<sup>+</sup>, respectively. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of all detected terpenoids in jellyfish, and their importance has yet to be examined based on extensive metabolite profiling.</p></section><section id="s0095"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Polyketides (macrolides)</h3>
<p id="p0235">Macrolides are a subclass of natural compounds, known as polyketides. Six peaks were identified in the anemone, compared to only two peaks in the jellyfish. Macrolides are well-known pharmaceuticals with antimicrobial, antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral activities <a href="#b0150" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0150">[30]</a>. Cytotoxicity is the most significant property <a href="#b0295" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0295">[59]</a>, adding to the potential of these animals as a source of drugs. Peak A5, assigned to salarin B, exhibited [M−H]<sup>-</sup> at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 719 and a base peak at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 673 owing to the loss of methyl and methoxy groups. Furthermore, the fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 415 was due to cleavage at the ester group (C<sub>1</sub>) and alkyl bond at C<sub>10</sub>-C<sub>11</sub> in addition to the loss of an acetyl group. In contrast, the fragment ion at <em>m</em>/<em>z</em> 397 was attributed to the cleavage of the amide bond (C<sub>7</sub>), C<sub>11</sub>-C<sub>12,</sub> and C<sub>16</sub>-C<sub>17</sub> (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. S8</a>). Macrolides increased from 1.3 to double folds to both stressors, suggesting their role in stress mitigation.</p></section><section id="s0100"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">UHPLC-MS multivariate data analysis (MVA) in response to thermal and UV stress</h3>
<p id="p0240">To assess changes in the anemone and jellyfish metabolome after thermal and UV exposure for marker identification using the UHPLC-MS dataset, a PCA model was constructed in an unsupervised manner. Furthermore, supervised orthogonal partial least squares (OPLS-DA) was employed in cases where PCA failed to predict the markers. The OPLS-DA model was assessed by R<sup>2</sup> and Q<sup>2</sup> values, indicating the model fit and predictability degree, respectively, to avoid model overfit.</p></section><section id="s0105"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Multivariate data analysis of anemone <em>E. quadricolor</em> UHPLC-MS dataset</h3>
<section id="s0110"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Unsupervised PCA of the whole <em>E. quadricolor</em> UHPLC-MS dataset post-thermal and UV stress</h4>
<p id="p0245">PCA modeling of the datasets of the anemone <em>E. quadricolor</em> showed low total variance coverage in both the negative and positive modes along PC1 and PC2, with a total coverage of 51.7 % in the positive mode model versus 39.8 % in the negative mode model (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 10</a>A). The negative mode-based model revealed that phosphatidylethanolamine (PE O-18:0/0:0) was the most distinct biomarker for heat stress based on loading plots (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 10</a>B), whereas other markers such as peptides, i.e.<em>,</em> seongsanamide A and tuftsin-hexylamide, were less distinctive markers. Markers revealed from the positive ionization mode included LPC (18:0/0:0) and LPC (16:0) as the most discriminating markers, followed by steroids, i.e.<em>,</em> methylcholestane-hexaol (peak A20) and propyl-cholesta-hexaol (peak A42) <a href="#b0300" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0300">[60]</a> under both heat and UV stress. Hierarchical cluster analysis (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 10</a>C) in negative mode showed three main clusters, with the highest temperature (34 °C) and longest UV exposure period (day 4) clustered together in the same subdivision, elaborating the comparable effect on the anemone metabolome and likely to represent extreme stress conditions at all time points.</p></section><section id="s0115"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Unsupervised PCA of <em>E. quadricolor</em> dataset post UV stress</h4>
<p id="p0250">For better marker identification of each stressor effect, PCA models were constructed for UV and thermal stress separately. PCA models of both ionization modes (positive and negative) exhibited a total variance coverage of 64.2 % and 48 %, respectively, higher than the combined PCA of both stressors (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Fig. 10</a>). The most discriminating metabolites in the UV exposure treatment in the positive ionization mode belonged to lysophospholipids, i.e.<em>,</em> LPC (18:0/0:0) and LPC (16:0), followed by steroids, i.e.<em>,</em> methylcholestane-<em>hexa</em>-ol (peak A20) and propylcholesta-<em>hexa</em>-ol (peak A42) (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>B). In contrast, in the negative ionization mode, phospholipids were the main biomarkers of UV stress, represented by PE (O-18:0/0:0), PE (O-20:0/0:0), PI (18:0/0:0), and PC (O-16:1/0:0) (<a href="#f0015" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>D).</p>

Thermal stress caused a noticeable shift in both the nonpolar metabolome pool (as in LPE 20:4) and the polar pool, as well represented by these peptides.</p></section><section id="s0160"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Supervised OPLS-DA of <em>C. andromeda</em> dataset post-thermal stress</h4>
<p id="p0315">Similar to the thermal stress exposure PCA model, an OPLS-DA model was employed for the highest temperature (34 °C), modeled against all other temperatures (<a href="#s0175" class="usa-link">Suppl. Future studies should therefore include the use of genetic markers such as ITS2 sequencing to detect differences in the Symbiodiniaceae assemblages (for example, following Hume et al. 2019 <a href="#b0350" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0350">[70]</a> and LaJeunesse et al. 2018 <a href="#b0355" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0355">[71]</a>. Accordingly, this will make it possible to analyze and compare high-throughput sequencing data in a phylogeny-based context <a href="#b0155" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0155">[31]</a> and might lead to a better understanding of the variety of marine invertebrate symbionts and how cnidarians adapt to changing environmental conditions.</p></section></section></section><section id="s0165"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusion</h2>
<p id="p0320">Metabolomes of the bubble-tip anemone <em>Entacmaea quadricolor</em> and the upside-down jellyfish <em>Cassiopea andromeda</em> were studied as examples from the family Cnidaria. These two species may have approximate nutritional and symbiotic strategies, and as tropical corals, benefit from photosymbiosis with carbohydrate-producing endosymbiotic algae, which makes them sensitive to environmental changes. Nevertheless, some metabolic changes differed between the two species under stress. This may be due to genetic variation among hosts and/or unicellular symbionts. Additionally, sea anemones have a mutualistic symbiotic relationship with clownfish, which makes them different from jellyfish.</p>
<p id="p0325">UHPLC-MS coupled with GNPS and chemometrics was used to identify markers for each stress in both marine animals. The two modes of ionization aided in the characterization of different metabolite classes. For example, the negative ionization mode revealed terpenoids and steroid classes, while ceramides and glycolipids were unraveled using the positive ionization mode, highlighting the importance of dual acquisition. Phospholipids represent the major cluster of identified compounds because of their role in maintaining the structural integrity of cell membranes and were detected in both ion modes. PLs served as markers for stress in most MVA models, i.e., LPC (18:0/0:0), LPC (16:0), PE (O-20:0/0:0), PI (18:0/0:0), and PC (O-16:1/0:0) were the most discriminatory markers in anemones, whereas in jellyfish, structural unsaturation played a role in their survival, as represented by LPE (20:4), serving as the phospholipid marker. It should be noted that in jellyfish, levels of unsaturated lipids increase upon thermal and UV stress exposure as a defense mechanism to maintain vital fluidity and viscosity of cell membranes. This is attributed to jellyfish thriving and blooming in warmer environments and phosphonate moieties <a href="#b0360" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0360">[72]</a> that contribute to the desaturation process compared to anemones. This can explain why jellyfish were less affected by temperature elevation than anemones. These results confirmed that the observed metabolomic changes were not mediated by unspecific physical contact between the two species, but rather by a dose–response specific stress, i.e.<em>,</em> either thermal or UV radiation, as proposed when compared to the control. In the anemone, the thermal stress models showed significant markers, in contrast to the UV stress models, where a significant model was not readily observed. Conversely, the jellyfish thermal stress models did not show significant markers, while UV stress revealed some markers. This aligns with the observed rapid deterioration of jellyfish morphological characteristics upon UV exposure compared to anemones. Moreover, it complies with the observed sudden death of anemones owing to extended temperature elevation, which contrasts with the resilience of jellyfish under the same stress conditions.</p>
<p id="p0330">In jellyfish, cyclic peptides and terpenoids appear to increase upon thermal stress, but not under UV stress. This is suggestive of a differential response in the case of peptides and terpenoids, and is contrary to phospholipids that were identified in response to both stressors in both organisms. Ceramide phosphonate, sterols, and peptides were the main markers, in addition to PLs, that were revealed among all models of both organisms. However, ceramide markers were exclusively found in jellyfish only after UV exposure. A diagrammatic sketch summarizing all markers revealed in response to thermal or UV stress in anemones and jellyfish is depicted in <a href="#f0045" class="usa-link">Fig. 9</a>. In the future, it should be determined whether these chemicals function just as markers for the stress response process in both organisms or contribute towards mitigation against such stressors. Additionally, profiling of other polar low molecular weight chemicals in jellyfish should aid in providing a complete understanding of metabolome changes within that organism, considering that UHPLC-MS targeted mostly the detection of non-polar metabolites, i.e.<em>,</em> lipids and terpenes. Identification of the molecular mechanisms underlying these responses should now follow, based on metabolomics results using other tools such as transcriptomics and proteomics. The sequencing of organisms' microbiomes should also aid in identifying changes in the microbiome consortium inside the animals in response to stress and in detecting strains that could aid in increasing the resilience of these organisms in the future. Likewise, the role of zooxanthellae/Symbiodiniaceae in the stress response mechanisms of these taxa and their interactions within the holobiont, i.e., the exchange of metabolites between host cnidarians and endosymbiotic algae, should be further investigated. Future assessment of Symbiodiniaceae diversity is critical for understanding the symbiotic ecology of corals and the differential responses observed in both marine organisms. The application of genetic markers, such as ITS2 sequencing, following the SymPortal framework <a href="#b0350" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="b0350">[70]</a> to constrain Symbiodiniaceae diversity should therefore be considered in future work. It should be noted that metabolome monitoring represents a holistic pool of all existing organisms inside animals with no dissection of metabolite origin, except in the case of zooxanthellamine; thus, it has yet to be determined by revealing the exact biosynthetic pathway of chemicals in such a complex consortium.</p>
