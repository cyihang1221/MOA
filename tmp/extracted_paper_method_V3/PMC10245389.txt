</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="ABS1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p id="P2">Enhanced in-source fragmentation/annotation (EISA) has recently been shown to produce fragment ions that match tandem mass spectrometry data across a wide range of small molecules. EISA has been developed to facilitate data dependent acquisition (DDA), data independent acquisiton (DIA), and multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) enabling molecular identifications in untargeted metabolomics and targeted quantitative single quadrupole MRM (Q-MRM) analyses. Here EISA has been applied to peptide-based proteomic analysis using optimized in-source fragmentation to generate fragmentation patterns for a mixture of 38 peptides which were comparable to the b- and y-type fragment ions typically observed in tandem MS experiments. The optimal in-source fragmentation conditions at which high abundant peptide fragments and precursor ions co-exist were compared with automated data dependent acquisition (DDA) in the same quadrupole time-of-flight (QTOF-MS) mass spectrometer, generating a significantly higher fragment percentage of peptides from both singly and doubly charged b- and y-type fragment (b<sup>+</sup>, y<sup>+</sup>, b<sup>2+</sup>, and y<sup>2+</sup>) ions. Higher fragment percentages were also observed for these fragment ion series over linear ion trap instrumentation. An XCMS-EISA annotation/deconvolution program was developed, making use of the retention time and peak shape continuity between precursor fragment ions, to perform automated proteomic data analysis on the enhanced in-source fragments. Post-translational modification (PTM) characterization on peptides was demonstrated with EISA, producing fragment ions corresponding to a neutral loss of phosphoric acid with greater intensity than observed with DDA on a QTOF-MS. Moreover, Q-MRM demonstrated the ability to use EISA for peptide quantification. The availability of more sophisticated in-source fragmentation informatics, beyond XCMS-EISA, will further enable EISA for sensitive autonomous identification and Q-MRM quantitative analyses in proteomics.</p></section><section class="abstract" id="ABS2"><h2>Graphical Abstract</h2>
<p class="img-box line-height-none margin-x-neg-2 tablet:margin-x-0 text-center"><img class="graphic" src="https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/4628/10245389/5410e09a9eb1/nihms-1901298-f0001.jpg" loading="lazy" height="256" width="640" alt="graphic file with name nihms-1901298-f0001.jpg"></p></section><section id="S1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">INTRODUCTION</h2>
<p id="P4">Proteomics has traditionally used tandem mass spectrometry (MS<sup>2</sup>) to characterize peptides by comparing experimental MS<sup>2</sup> spectra with theoretical MS<sup>2</sup> data.<sup><a href="#R1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R1">1</a>-<a href="#R3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R3">3</a></sup> This MS<sup>2</sup> data is typically generated using either data-dependent acquisition (DDA) or data-independent acquisition (DIA) and has proven to be valuable in identifying proteins and post-translational modifications.<sup><a href="#R4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R4">4</a></sup> Conventional DDA involves the scanning of all precursor peptide ions during the survey scan (MS<sup>1</sup>) and the selection of a predefined number of precursor ions for subsequent fragmentation (MS<sup>2</sup>).<sup><a href="#R5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R5">5</a></sup> While DIA is based on the fragmentation of all detected precursor ions within a survey scan, DIA has gained increasing attention in the proteomics community.<sup><a href="#R6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R6">6</a></sup></p>
<p id="P5">Enhanced in-source fragmentation annotation (EISA) has been described<sup><a href="#R7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R7">7</a>-<a href="#R9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R9">9</a></sup> as an alternative approach for producing fragmentation data on metabolites that is very similar to those generated with MS<sup>2</sup>. EISA was introduced as a simplified alternative to DIA and DDA in metabolomics, allowing for straightforward metabolite annotation to facilitate metabolite identification using a relatively simple single mass analyzer instrumentation.<sup><a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a></sup> EISA has also been developed for quantitative analysis using single quadrupole multiple reaction monitoring (Q-MRM).<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup></p>
<p id="P6">Here we demonstrate that EISA can also facilitate enhanced coverage in bottom-up proteomics for protein identification, PTM characterization, and peptide quantification (via Q-MRM). The EISA experiments were optimized from a digest of BSA and compared to data generated from conventional DDA analysis. We also used EISA to examine PTMs (phosphorylation). We developed an informatic approach (XCMS-EISA) to help deconvolve the proteomics data. Our results indicate that a single mass analyzer instrumentation (e.g., the time-of-flight reflectron), can be repurposed for proteomics experiments, which have historically been driven by hybrid instrumentation.<sup><a href="#R11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R11">11</a>,<a href="#R12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R12">12</a></sup></p></section><section id="S2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">EXPERIMENTAL SECTION</h2>
<section id="S3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Materials.</h3>
<p id="P7">The BSA digest was purchased from Fisher Scientific, Catalog number PI88341. The MS PhosphoMix Standards mix 1 (Catalog number MSP2L PhosphoMix 1), consisting of 10 singly and doubly phosphorylated peptides, was purchased from Sigma Aldrich company. Mix 1 was designed to include phosphopeptides having a broad range of characteristics including peptide ionizability (both ESI and MALDI), molecular weight, chromatographic retention time, charge state, and isoelectric point.</p></section><section id="S4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Peptide mix analysis using EISA and auto DDA methods.</h3>
<p id="P8">The BSA peptide mixture and phosphopeptides mixture were analyzed with a liquid chromatography electrospray ionization QTOF-MS (LC-ESI-QTOF-MS) (BRUKER impact II) in positive ESI modes using a ZORBAX 300 SB-C18 column (0.5 × 150 mm, 5 μm, Agilent). Peptides were separated by gradient elution at a flow rate of 20 μL/min starting at 5% (v/v) B, held for 5 min, increased to 95% B within 45 min, held for 5 min, and reverted to 5% B at 56<sup>th</sup> min, held for 5 min, with a total run time of 61 min. The mobile phases comprised water containing 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (B).</p>
<p id="P9">The original ESI source parameters were set as follows: dry temperature 180 °C, dry gas 7 L/min, nebulizer 20.3 psi, and capillary voltage 4500 V and – 4000 V for positive and negative modes, respectively. The transfer parameters were: funnel 1 RF 150 Vpp, funnel 2 RF 200 Vpp, isCID energy 0 eV, and hexapole RF 50 Vpp. The ion energy in the quadrupole was set to 4 eV and the collision energy applied in the collision cell was set at 7 eV. Nitrogen was used as the collision gas in the collision cell. The instrument acquisition range was set at <em>m/z</em> 50-1200 and the MS acquisition rate was 2 spectra/s.</p>
<p id="P10">As discussed in the results section, transfer isCID energy was optimized to increase the in-source fragmentation while maintaining the intensity of precursor ions. After optimization, 50 eV transfer isCID energy were selected as the enhanced in-source fragmentation condition in the analysis of BSA peptides mixture, with all other parameters fixed.</p>
<p id="P11">For the auto DDA of MS/MS spectra, both the MS and MS<sup>2</sup> acquisition rates were set at 2 spectra/s to acquire over the <em>m/z</em> range 50-1200. A stepping collision energy from 16 to 40 eV was used with an intensity threshold of 10,000 and a cycle time of 3 s. A smart exclusion was employed after two spectra and a precursor reconsideration was performed if the intensity difference is over 2 times.</p></section><section id="S5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Peptide mix analysis using conventional linear trap quadrupole (LTQ) mass spectrometry</h3>
<p id="P12">Peptides were analyzed by reverse-phase chromatography prior to mass spectrometry analysis using the following method. Nanoelectrospray capillary column tips were made in-house by using a P-100 laser puller (Sutter Instruments). The columns were packed with Zorbax SB-C18 stationary phase (Agilent) purchased in bulk (5-mm particles, with a 15-cm length and a 75-mm inner diameter). The reverse-phase gradient separation was performed by using water and acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid) as the mobile phases. The gradient consisted of 5% acetonitrile ramped for 5 min followed by a gradient to 8% acetonitrile for 5 min, 55% acetonitrile for 40 min, 95% acetonitrile for 5 min and re-equilibrated with 5% acetonitrile for 10 min.</p>
<p id="P13">DDA data were obtained with a Thermo Finnigan LTQ linear ion trap mass spectrometer using a home-built nanoelectrospray source at 2 kV at the tip. One MS spectrum was followed by 4 MS<sup>2</sup> scans on the most abundant ions after the application of the dynamic exclusion list. Tandem mass spectra were extracted by use of Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All MS/MS samples were analyzed by using Mascot<sup><a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">13</a></sup> (version 2.1.04; Matrix Science, London, United Kingdom) with S. epidermidis proteins contained in the NCBI protein database, assuming the digestion enzyme trypsin. Mascot was searched with a fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.80 Da and a parent ion tolerance of 2.0 Da; identification was done at the 95% confidence level with a calculated false-positive rate of &lt;1% as determined by using a reversed concatenated protein database. Peptide identifications were accepted if they could be established at a greater than 95.0% probability as specified by the Peptide Prophet algorithm<sup><a href="#R14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R14">14</a></sup>. Protein identifications were accepted if they could be established at a greater than 99.0% probability and contained at least two identified peptides as specified by the Protein Prophet algorithm. Proteins that contained similar peptides and could not be differentiated based on MS/MS analysis alone were grouped to satisfy the principles of parsimony.</p></section><section id="S6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Data Analysis.</h3>
<p id="P14">The data collected using EISA (MS<sup>1</sup>) and auto MS/MS methods were manually inspected using the Bruker Compass Data Analysis software (Version 4.4). A selection criterion of ± 0.01 Da was used in analyzing the extracted ion chromatogram (EIC) data to identify the fragment ions that belong to peptide features. Theoretical exact mass of each fragment ion of every peptide in both the BSA peptide mixture and phosphopeptide mixture was calculated using Fragment Ion Calculator. For the identification of each peptide, three types of precursor ions, including [M+H]<sup>+</sup>, [M+2H]<sup>2+</sup>, and [M+3H]<sup>3+</sup>, and both singly and doubly charged b- and y-type fragment ions (b<sup>+</sup>, y<sup>+</sup>, b<sup>2+</sup>, and y<sup>2+</sup>) were analyzed. For the identification of phosphopeptides, singly and doubly charged b- and y-type ions and the corresponding fragments with a neutral loss of phosphoric acid were analyzed. Here, we used the fragment percentage to evaluate the peptide identification confidence, calculated using the following equation.</p>

<p id="P17">The following procedure was used for protein identification: (1) searched the precursor ion <em>m/z</em> in the MS<sup>1</sup> chromatogram of peptides mixture generated by EISA using a predefined selection criteria (± 0.01 Da); (2) carefully selected the peak of precursor ion based on the retention time and acquired the MS spectra at the peak apex; (3) recorded the in-source fragments that matched the theoretical masses of peptide fragments using the predefined mass accuracy criteria (± 0.01 Da); (4) checked the quality of in-source fragments at low abundance. Two factors were considered for distinguishing low abundant features from background noise: a genuine low abundant fragment ion has an isotopic pattern; signal intensity of a fragment ion is changing with the increase of in-source fragmentation energy. (5) calculated the fragment percentage for each fragment ion series (b<sup>+</sup>, y<sup>+</sup>, b<sup>2+</sup>, and y<sup>2+</sup>) of each peptide; (6) assigned putative protein identification based on the collected peptide information. The fragments outside the mass acquisition range (below <em>m/z</em> 50) were not considered. Statistical analysis was conducted with GraphPad Prism 8.<sup><a href="#R15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R15">15</a></sup></p></section><section id="S7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">XCMS-EISA.</h3>
<p id="P18">An informatics algorithm, termed as XCMS-EISA, was developed to formalize the heuristics employed in the manual curation process as well as to achieve autonomous peptide identification. XCMS-EISA is an R script which uses the XCMS (v3.14.1) package to read and process the EISA MS<sup>1</sup> spectral data.<sup><a href="#R16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R16">16</a>-<a href="#R18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R18">18</a></sup> First, a MS/MS spectral library was established for the peptides of target protein. Next, the raw data was converted to mzML file format with msconvert from ProteoWizard (v3.0.19346).<sup><a href="#R19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R19">19</a></sup> Subsequently, all precursor ion <em>m/z</em> (1+, 2+ and 3+) and fragment ion <em>m/z</em> (b<sup>+</sup>, y<sup>+</sup>, b<sup>2+</sup>, and y<sup>2+</sup>) were taken from the database to create EICs using the predefined mass accuracy criteria (± 0.01 Da). EICs which contain no data, i.e. have more than 80% missing data were removed. For each peptide EICs where made for all the precursor ions and fragment ions. A small retention time window (±15s) was used to select only a part of an EIC containing the peak of the precursor or fragment. Following the XCMS SWATH approach, a correlation coefficient was calculated between each fragment EIC and the precursor EIC with the highest signal. A high correlation coefficient (&gt; 0.8) between a fragment EIC and precursor EIC indicated that they belong together.</p>
<p id="P19">For all fragment ions a similarity value we computed as the cosine similarity between the theoretical and measured isotopic distribution. The first four isotopes were used to calculate the similarity. The combination of a high correlation coefficient and high similarity (&gt; 0.75) confirmed that a fragment belongs to a precursor. Sometimes the similarity was low caused by another ion overlapping the 3<sup>rd</sup> or 4<sup>th</sup> isotope. In this case the 3<sup>rd</sup> and 4<sup>th</sup> isotope were removed from the calculations. XCMS-EISA can be found at <a href="https://github.com/ricoderks/proteomicsEISA" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://github.com/ricoderks/proteomicsEISA</a>.</p></section><section id="S8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Quantitative analysis of peptide using single quandrupole multiple fragment ion monitoring MS (Q-MRM).</h3>
<p id="P20">Following a protocol described earlier,<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup> a peptide drug, leucine enkephalin, was measured quantitatively using the single quadrupole multiple fragment ion monitoring MS technique, abbreviated as Q-MRM. In brief, the separation and quantification of leucine enkephalin were performed using the ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters) and an Agilent 6130 Quadrupole LC-MS (Santa Clara, CA), respectively. A precursor ion with <em>m/z</em> 556.4 and fragment ion with <em>m/z</em> 120.1 were used for the quantification purpose. Limit of quantification (LOQ) and linear dynamic range were determined as described previously.<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup></p></section></section><section id="S9"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS AND DISCUSSION</h2>
<section id="S10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">In-source fragmentation optimization for peptide mixture analysis</h3>
<p id="P21">In previous EISA metabolomic experiments, we demonstrated that optimized electrospray ionization (ESI) in-source fragmentation conditions of endogenous metabolites can generate abundant in-source fragments that are comparable with those generated in MS/MS experiments, while maintaining high precursor ion intensity.<sup><a href="#R8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R8">8</a></sup> In these analyses, we applied the same in-source fragmentation approach to optimize peptide fragmentation and facilitate the subsequent protein identification.</p>
<p id="P22">A BSA peptide digest (5 picomole on column containing 38 peptides) at multiple transfer isCID energies ranging from 10 to 100eV in 10eV increments was used to optimize and validate the EISA approach; transfer isCID energy has been demonstrated as the major parameter controlling the in-source fragmentation energy in Bruker Q-TOF instrumentation. To determine the precursor ion for the target peptides, we manually analyzed the data for both singly and doubly charged precursor ions for each peptide, the triply charged precursor ion was also used for confirmation. EISA peptide fragmentation was examined for the formation of b- and y-type fragment ions, of the 38 peptides measured, all were observed to have singly charged fragment ions, and 33 peptides had doubly charged fragment ions. Further, one in-source fragmentation condition (50 eV isCID energy) was selected and used to compare the number and intensity between singly and doubly charged in-source fragment ions of the 33 peptides. As shown in <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Fig. S1a</a>, more singly charged in-source fragment ions (median percentages of detected fragments of b<sup>+</sup> [75%] and y<sup>+</sup> [82%]) are detected in comparison to their doubly charged counterparts (median percentages of detected fragments of b<sup>2+</sup> [44%] and y<sup>2+</sup> [64%]); median fragment intensity of the singly charged ions are also higher than their doubly charged counterparts (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Fig. S1b</a>). Therefore, singly charged b- and y-type fragment ions were used to illustrate the relationship between in-source fragmentation energy and peptide fragmentation performance.</p>
<p id="P23">The relationship between the percentage of in-source fragments of each peptide generated at each condition (versus the total number of fragments calculated theoretically) and the in-source fragmentation energy was evaluated. As shown in <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1a</a> and <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">1b</a>, increasing in-source fragmentation energy provided the median fragment percentage observed for both b- and y-type fragments with the most significant fragmentation observed at 50 eV isCID energy for y-type fragments. For b-type fragments, the median fragment percentage reached a maximum at 60 eV isCID energy and then decreased with the increase of in-source fragmentation energy (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1a</a>). We further examined the relationship between median fragment intensity and the in-source fragmentation energy. As shown in <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1c</a> and <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">1d</a>, the median fragment intensity for both b- and y-type fragments increased significantly from 40 to 50 eV isCID energy, and remained relatively stable from 50 to 70 eV isCID energy, then decreased as the in-source fragmentation energy was raised further. Overall, both the number and intensity of peptide in-source fragments increased significantly at 50 eV isCID energy, a level that was maintained until 70 eV isCID energy.</p>

<p id="P26">DDA is often used to acquire high-quality fragment spectra of known peptide precursor ions. To evaluate the fragmentation performance of EISA we acquired the fragmentation data using both auto DDA method (16-40 eV) on the same Bruker QTOF instrumentation as well as EISA data. MS/MS fragments of three types of precursor ions, including [M+H]<sup>+</sup>, [M+2H]<sup>2+</sup>, and [M+3H]<sup>3+</sup>, were manually evaluated and the precursor ion producing the highest abundant fragments was used to represent the selected peptide. Of the 38 peptides measured in the BSA peptide digest, fragment information of 36 peptides were collected using the auto MS/MS in a single run with three replicate analyses. Here, we collected all the matched peptide fragments by manually inspecting the matched fragment ions for both techniques, regardless of the signal intensity, as shown in <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>. A biologically meaningful low abundance feature differs with background noise in at least two of the following ways: 1) a clear isotopic pattern can be observed for a low abundant feature; 2) the change of signal intensity can be observed with the increase of in-source fragmentation energy.</p>
<p id="P27">In bottom-up proteomics, peptide identification typically involves searching observed fragment spectra against theoretical peptide fragment spectra produced by in silico fragmentation of a protein sequence database.<sup><a href="#R20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R20">20</a>-<a href="#R22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R22">22</a></sup> Major protein identification algorithms including Sequest<sup><a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a></sup>, X!Tandem<sup><a href="#R21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R21">21</a></sup> and Mascot<sup><a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">13</a></sup> have been using the <em>m/z</em> match between experimental fragment spectra with theoretical ones to assign peptide sequences to fragmentation spectra.<sup><a href="#R24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R24">24</a></sup> Thus, similar as peptide ion score,<sup><a href="#R25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R25">25</a></sup> the fragment percentage, calculated using the number of matched fragment ions versus the total number of theoretically calculated fragments within each fragment ion series for each peptide, is used to evaluate the peptide identification confidence in this study. As shown in <a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4a</a>, fragment percentages in all of the four types of fragment series (b<sup>+</sup>, y<sup>+</sup>, b<sup>2+</sup>, and y<sup>2+</sup>) were calculated for every peptide using the fragment information acquired with both EISA (50 eV) and auto DDA. For singly charged b<sup>+</sup> and y<sup>+</sup> fragments, respective median fragment percentage was 74% and 83% for EISA, while the numbers were 37% and 72% respectively for the fragments produced using auto MS/MS method. For b<sup>2+</sup> and y<sup>2+</sup> fragments, the median fragment percentages were 39% and 60%, respectively, for EISA method, while the numbers were 0% and 17% for auto MS/MS. A greater number of fragments can be obtained when multiple collision energies were used together.<sup><a href="#R26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R26">26</a></sup> Overall, fragment percentages acquired in b<sup>+</sup>, b<sup>2+</sup>, and y<sup>2+</sup> ion series using EISA are significantly higher than those acquired using auto DDA method (p&lt;0.01), providing higher confidence in peptide identification. For y<sup>+</sup> fragment series, fragment percentages acquired using EISA method ranged from 29% to 100% at a median increase of 11% over those acquired using auto DDA method.</p>

To further evaluate the fragmentation performance of EISA, we recalculated the fragment percentage using those fragments with intensity over 1000 (based on the noise level observed in the EISA MS<sup>1</sup> spectrum in this study). All the matched peptide fragments with intensity over 1000 are summarized in <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>. As shown in <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Fig. S3a</a>, EISA method not only showed significant higher fragment percentages over auto DDA method b<sup>+</sup>, b<sup>2+</sup>, and y<sup>2+</sup> ion series, but also in y<sup>+</sup> fragment series, indicating that signal intensities of EISA fragments are higher than those observed in auto DDA method.</p></section><section id="S12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">EISA QTOF versus DDA with LTQ</h3>
<p id="P29">Most current mass spectrometers used for proteomics employ a linear ion trap<sup><a href="#R27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R27">27</a>-<a href="#R29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R29">29</a></sup>, which allows for both isolation and fragmentation of peptide ions. Here, we also collected the tandem mass spectral data using a Thermo Finnigan LTQ linear ion trap mass spectrometer for comparative analyses to the EISA QTOF results. Fragment spectra generated using LTQ mass spectrometer were normally searched against a protein database using Mascot for protein identification.</p>
<p id="P30">We compared the fragment percentages in four types of ion series acquired using both EISA and the LTQ. Because the matched fragments obtained using the LTQ are acquired using a searching algorithm with a signal intensity cutoff of 1000, we first calculated the fragment percentages for EISA using fragments with intensity over 1000 (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Fig. S3</a>; <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S2</a>). As shown in <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Fig. S3b</a>, although no significant differences were observed in singly charged fragment ions between the two methods, EISA demonstrated significantly higher fragment percentages in both doubly charged fragment ion series (p &lt; 0.01). After taking those fragments with intensity less than 1000, yet statistically significant, into account, EISA also showed significantly higher fragment percentages in b+ fragment series (p &lt; 0.01) (<a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Fig. S3</a>; <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>). Taken together, EISA QTOF generated higher identification confidence in characterizing the investigated peptides.</p></section><section id="S13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Proteomics Data Annotation with XCMS-EISA</h3>
<p id="P31">Informatics (XCMS-EISA) were developed to enable the autonomous annotation of proteomic data. XCMS-EISA makes use of XCMS’s approach for SWATH analysis to correlate fragment ions with specific precursors. In addition, the similarity value between the theoretical isotopic distribution and the measured isotopic distribution was used to determine if a fragment EIC should be discarded (low similarity value) or kept (high similarity value). The isotopic pattern of the fragment ion was also taken into consideration in determining the correlation coefficient. A high correlation coefficient between the precursor EIC and the fragment EIC indicates that the fragment belongs to a specific precursor. Important here is the retention time window around the peak top. If the retention time window is too large, possible additional peaks in the EIC will most likely lower the correlation coefficient. An overly short retention time window will result in inaccurate correlation coefficient values, because there is not enough data to compare the peak shapes. The best correlation coefficients will be obtained when the whole chromatographic peak is used without additional baseline, meaning that the retention time window needs to be approximately the same as the peak width at baseline. In this case the retention time window should be around 20-30 seconds. An example is provided in <a href="#F5" class="usa-link">Fig. 5</a> showing the scripts ability to correlate EIC peak shape and how it performed for b- and y-type fragments across different retention time windows.</p>

<p id="P32">The eukaryotic proteome is much more diverse than its corresponding genome due to post-transcriptional and post-translational modifications.<sup><a href="#R30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R30">30</a>-<a href="#R31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R31">31</a></sup> Post translational modification (PTM) of proteins is normally achieved by covalent addition of chemical units or chemical alteration of the constituent amino acids.<sup><a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">32</a></sup> PTMs play key roles in almost all biological processes and are normally measured by mass shifts caused by the modification.<sup><a href="#R32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R32">32</a></sup> The modified residue site can be further interpreted by its site-specific fragments.<sup><a href="#R33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R33">33</a>-<a href="#R35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R35">35</a></sup> Therefore, the mapping of PTMs is an important dimension to help describe the entire proteome.</p>
<p id="P33">Phosphorylation is one of the most common and biologically relevant PTMs where addition of a phosphate group to a protein can influence many of its properties.<sup><a href="#R36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R36">36</a>-<a href="#R37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R37">37</a></sup> However, electronegativity of the phosphoryl group and the labile nature of the phosphoester bond made phosphopeptides less efficiently ionized/fragmented in comparison to unmodified peptides.<sup><a href="#R38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R38">38</a></sup> Results of MS/MS obtained from phosphopeptide analysis under general CID conditions demonstrate loss of labile metaphosphoric acid or phosphoric acid.<sup><a href="#R39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R39">39</a></sup> To determine the feasibility of EISA and test its strengths and weaknesses for phosphoproteomics workflow, we analyzed phosphopeptides with molecular weight ranging from 800 to 2800 Da at increasing isCID energy ranging from 10 to 100 eV at 10 eV increments. We investigated singly and doubly charged b- and y-type phosphopeptide fragment ions, with and without a neutral loss of phosphoric acid. Due to the low concentration of each peptide injected on column (1 picomole), the intensity of fragments observed for these phosphopeptide are relatively low with intensity of the majority fragments at hundreds to thousands of counts, as shown in <a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>, thus we didn’t observe a clear pattern between the intensity of fragment ions and in-source fragmentation condition. However, the relationship between the percentage of detected fragments and the in-source fragmentation energy showed that an optimal in-source fragmentation condition can be achieved at 60 to 70 eV isCID energy (<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Fig. 7a</a>-<a href="#F7" class="usa-link">d</a> and <a href="#SD1" class="usa-link">Fig. S4</a>). We further compared the fragment generation performance between EISA (70 eV) and auto DDA (16-40 eV) as shown in <a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Fig. 7e</a>. Phosphopeptide pTKLIpTQLRDA[K] has a total of 5 fragment ions in the [y-H<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2+</sup> ion series; all of them can be observed at EISA (70 eV), while only one fragment ion can be found in the mass spectrum produced using DDA (16-40 eV) method (<a href="#F6" class="usa-link">Fig. 6</a>).</p>

<p id="P34">Leucine enkaphelin as examined to demonstrate the utility of Q-MRM for peptide quantification. In addition to Q-MRM, a newly developed the CIM algorithm was used to facilitate these analyses. This algorithm autonomously processes, aligns, filters, and compiles multiple ions within one chromatogram enabling both precursor and in-source fragment ions to be correlated within a single chromatogram, also enabling the detection of co-eluting species based on precursor and fragment ion ratios. Single quadrupole instrumentation can provide MRM level quantitative performance by monitoring/correlating precursor and fragment ions facilitating high sensitivity analysis on existing single quadrupole instrumentation that are generally inexpensive, easy to operate, and technically less complex.</p>
<p id="P35">The leucine enkaphelin experiments were designed to compare QqQ-MRM to Q-MRM. They included a comparison of the techniques using a dilution series of the peptide, resulting in the same limit of quanification (~1 nM) and dynamic range (~3×10<sup>5</sup>) between the two techniques. These preliminary Q-MRM results (<a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a>) are consistent with recent work<sup><a href="#R10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R10">10</a></sup> on endogenous and exogenous metabolites, suggesting the utility of Q-MRM can be extended to peptides.</p>

<p id="P36">Collision energy, collision gas type and pressure have been known to affect fragment generation in mass spectrometry.<sup><a href="#R40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R40">40</a></sup> Although there are considerable differences in these parameters between EISA and collision-induced dissociation (CID) techniques, this study demonstrates that EISA in-source fragmentation can produce peptide fragment ions similar to those produced with CID tandem mass spectrometry, thus enabling bottom-up protein identification. When compared with CID fragment mass spectra including DDA, EISA in-source fragment spectra showed a significantly higher fragment coverage that matched to the theoretically calculated fragments of the target peptides. While CID can only be used in hybrid mass spectrometry technologies including QqQ, QTOF and Q-Orbitrap, EISA technique can be used on much simpler instrumentation such as ESI single quadrupole and TOF instruments. Further analysis of in-source fragements of phosphopeptides showed that EISA can also be used in the characterization of post-translational modifications. As observed in metabolomic experiments, since EISA does not require a collision cell, it generally provides higher signal intensities since there are no related collision cell ion losses. While this attribute of EISA is attractive, at the time of this publication protein data processing algorithms (e.g., Mascot<sup><a href="#R13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R13">13</a></sup> and Sequest<sup><a href="#R23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R23">23</a></sup>) do not support the direct search of in-source fragment spectra against the protein databases therefore limiting EISA not because of performance, but rather the available informatics. This paper, for the first time, provided an informatics algorithm (XCMS-EISA) to facilitate the annotation of peptides using the EISA in-source fragment spectra. Small molecule identification using EISA MS<sup>1</sup> spectral data may also benefit from this approach.</p>
<p id="P37">Despite the advantages of EISA in-source fragmentation technique, some limitations exist. The EISA technique requires that the peptide sequence associated with a putative peptide-spectrum match be known. Additionally, a variety of factors may affect the correct identification of peptides, such as the usage of a short LC gradient or a complex matrix, which may result in the coelution of interfering ions and cause retention time shifts between runs.<sup><a href="#R41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="R41">41</a></sup> This decreases the potential to identify peptides with high confidence.</p>
<p id="P38">Overall, our results show that enhanced in-source fragmentation can play a significant role in making high sensitivity and comprehensive proteomic analyses more accessible to a broader population of scientists. It accomplishes this by making protein identification, PTM characterization, and (through Q-MRM) protein quantification using simplified mass spectrometry technology.</p></section><section id="SM1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Supplementary Material</h2>
