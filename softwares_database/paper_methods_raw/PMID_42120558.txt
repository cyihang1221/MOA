Introduction
Yoga1–3, which has its roots in ancient India, is an all-encompassing
practice combines mental, spiritual, and physical elements aims to
promote consciousness, body-mind harmony, and overall well-
being. Recent research shows the positive effects of yoga on
postural stability, musculoskeletal flexibility, cardiovascular
control, and psycho-physiological balance which significantly
enhance their neuromuscular coordination and decrease stress
markers in the human body.
Despite these benefits, proper yoga posture execution is very
important. In particular, strain or injury to the knees, ligaments,
and spine can result in the formation of unbalanced weight
distribution when yoga posture is not executed properly.
Conventional yoga relies on qualified teachers who use visual
examination to evaluate students’ muscle activation, alignment, and
stability4–6. This kind of service is not available to practitioners due
to the lack of skilled persons and increase in the population. This
disparity has led to the development of automated yoga posture
recognition (YPR) systems, which use image processing technology
to assess, inspect, and adjust postures in the real time.
Automated identification systems can be used in a variety of
applications, such as online yoga systems provide the users with
live supervision, interactive rehabilitation, personal wellness apps,
and senior fitness tracking. These systems can assist practitioners
without direct instructor supervision while preserving the safety
and caliber of training when they are driven by computer vision
and sensor technology. Thus, an efficient scalable, affordable
solutions for individualized wellness management are offered by
YPR research by employing computer vision, human–computer
interaction, and health informatics.
In YPR, most of the existing frameworks often rely on conventional
CNN-based feature extraction combined with static fusion strategies
such as feature concatenation or late decision-level integration.
These approaches often lacks in the systematic modelling of skeletal
linkages, adaptive cross-modal interaction, and personalized
learning processes. However, to overcome these challenges, the
proposed framework provides a hybrid Vision–IMU architecture
which integrates temporal inertial analysis, adaptive multimodal
fusion, and graph-based spatial modelling for an accurate yoga
posture detection.
In the proposed system, Visual skeletal important points are
recorded as structured graphs and are interpreted using Graph
Convolutional Networks (GCNs), to represent joint connections and
body topology in a detailed manner. Additionally, an attention-
driven fusion method has been proposed that dynamically adjusts
each modality’s contribution based on contextual dependability and
improves resilience against occlusion, sensor noise, and
environmental variations. Furthermore, the proposed method
provide the adaptable module which provides reinforcement
learning to enable user-specific model adaptation and customized
feedback.
Challenges in yoga posture recognition
Developing an automated system to identify and assess yoga
postures presents a number of complex issues. Individual
differences in limb length, muscle tone, flexibility, and movement
style represent a basic limitation of human anatomy. As a result,
assessing joint angles and position data across the multiple
populations is very challenging for a single global model.
Technical and environmental limitations may make this type of
model visual-based identification difficult. Changes in the
illumination, camera positioning, garment occlusions, or crowded
backdrops can significantly impact the accuracy of joint detection.
These drawbacks7–9 are especially noticeable at home, where
consumers employs online tools to perform yoga postures by using
webcams and motion detection systems.
Temporal modelling is another important significant limitation; as
yoga transitions is the blend of fast movement sequences with small
pauses, methods that can represent both joint spatial configuration
and temporal evolution are needed. Concurrent or transformer-
based systems must mut provide trade-off between accuracy and
real-time viability, whereas CNNs’ frame-wise analysis records
posture at certain points in time but lacks temporal continuity.
Moreover, there are no such publicly accessible databases on yoga
positions.
Most of the currently employed datasets contain limited pose
classes (e.g., 10–20 asanas), limited environments, and moderate
sample sizes. These restrictions limit the model generalization and
reduces the transfer learning. Furthermore, the necessity for yoga
personalization is frequently disregarded; a successful system
should not only recognize the posture but also provide customized
personal guidance based on the user’s prior injuries and physical
limits. These issues collectively reinforce the need for hybrid,
sensor-integrated, adaptive yoga systems.
Role of AI, deep learning, and sensors
Posture and human activity recognition limitation problems have
changed as a result of deep learning10 and uses of artificial
intelligence. Convolutional neural networks, or CNNs, required to
provides the visually understanding of human postures and derive
hierarchical spatial representations from images or videos. Current
models such as PoseResNet, HRNet, and OpenPose demonstrate
have the ability to accurately identify critical joints for the many
individuals in chaotic environments. Even in cases of partial
occlusion, transformers models such as ViT-Pose and HRFormer
provide the feature of fine-grained skeleton coordinates, improving
spatial contextualization.
Hybrid sensor approaches, which can enable sensor fusion across
data, feature, or decision levels, use these two modalities. It is
possible to generate efficient multi-modal embeddings through
fusion by obtaining a spatial posture estimate from vision and
temporal motion characterization from IMUs. For adaptive
feedback, Reinforcement Learning (RL) is another possible
approach11. Reinforcement learning agents can create reward
functions based on posture correctness metrics such as limb
symmetry, balance stability, or joint deviation to learn the optimal
remedial actions for each user. These self-learning procedures can
be utilized to design dynamic training tools which adapt user
progresses, which goes beyond the static posture assessment.
Motivation for a hybrid vision–IMU framework
Although both vision and IMU-based recognition techniques12 have
achieved significant accuracy, their use is limited because of their
limitations inherent. Vision-only systems perform poorly in
situations with clutter, occlusions, or inadequate camera input, but
they achieve excellent accuracy when the lighting and background
are controlled. However, IMU-only techniques are inefficient in
detecting misalignments such as non-uniform joint extension or
shoulder tilt because they are not spatially interpretable and are
still resistant to visual distortion. Motivated all these observations,
this work proposes a hybrid vision–IMU13 posture detection system
which efficiently combines the kinematic dependability of IMU
sensors along with spatial precision of computer vision models. In
the proposed work, IMUs proposed temporal dynamics and
orientation stability, and provides structural context, which
leverages these complementary qualities through feature-level or
data-level fusion in an efficient manner.
Moreover, hybrid frameworks14,15 improve error correction if one
sensor stream degrades by permitting cross-validation across
modalities. Robust adaptive training is made possible by real-time
multi-modal synchronization, which replicates the atmosphere of a
genuine home or studio even when there are outside disturbances.
Reinforcement learning-based feedback can be used to continuously
enhance the system’s guiding policy, resulting in a flexible personal
trainer that adapts the frequency of input and the severity of
corrections dependent on the user’s success.
Research problem and objectives
The main objectives of the proposed system are given as follows:
1. To develop an effective deep learning based hybrid model
which blends optical and inertial sense detection.
2. To set up the efficient advanced sensor fusion algorithms
which can quickly align multi-modal, asynchronous data
streams.
3. To improve the way tailored feedback which can be delivered
by using reinforcement learning techniques to make sure that
modifications are user-specific and performance-driven.
4. To evaluate the design using a diverse, multi-subject yoga
dataset, accounting for differences in camera circumstances,
body sizes, and skill levels.
